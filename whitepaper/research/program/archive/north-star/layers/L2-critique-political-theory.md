## Steelman (political-theory framing)

Read at its best, this dossier makes a genuinely Hobbesian move that the seeds only gesture at, and it makes it correctly on the one point most people get wrong. Hobbes's covenant is *among the subjects* — the multitude authorizes a sovereign and binds itself, but the sovereign is not itself a party to the contract. The dossier's "legible-sovereign rule" (§1.B.7) is the inversion that rescues this from despotism: the Leviathan is the *most* legible actor, not the least. That is the strongest possible reading of "authority + legibility" as a single primitive. In Hobbes the sovereign is opaque by structure (the subjects cannot audit the sovereign's reasoning; that opacity *is* the peace-guarantee). The dossier proposes a sovereign whose every act of authority — every Arbiter-block, claim-denial, escalation — is a named, zoomable, attestable event. This is not a quibble; it is the move that converts Hobbesian *authorization* into something a 21st-century operator could actually consent to. It answers the obvious objection ("you've built a tyrant") before it lands.

The Scott reading is also handled with unusual care. The dossier knows the trap — that legibility *destroys* what it cannot see (mētis, the local practical knowledge the high-modernist state flattens). The "digest-with-zoom" law is, read charitably, an explicit anti-Scott safeguard: the summary is "a lens, never a replacement," and the canonical zoom target is the *artifact* (diff/test/row), not the model's narration. That is precisely Scott's prescription — keep the cadastral map, but never mistake it for the territory; preserve the route back to ground truth. The "verifiable-zoom" rule (grounded in Turpin et al. on unfaithful chain-of-thought) is the dossier refusing to let the swarm's self-narration *become* the legible record, which is exactly the failure mode Scott documents when the state's abstraction replaces the practitioner's knowledge. The acknowledgment of *Artificial Leviathan* (Dai et al. 2024) as the empirical anchor — agent societies actually re-enacting the Hobbesian arc — is the right citation and correctly framed as observed-not-metaphor.

And §1.C — the operator as a *modeled entity* with finite attention — is the dossier's best original contribution to the political theory. It quietly relocates the binding constraint from the sovereign's capacity to the *subjects'* capacity to hold the sovereign accountable. That is a real political-theory insight: legibility that the governed cannot afford to consume is not accountability, it is the *appearance* of accountability. The abdication gradient (doer → approver → rubber-stamp → absent) is a sharp restatement of the classic principal-agent collapse into pure agency.

## Gaps & inconsistencies (prioritized)

**1. The consent mechanism is asserted, never built — and this is the load-bearing crack.** The dossier says the operator "*consents to cede decision authority*" (§1.B intro). But where is consent *located*, *scoped*, *revoked*? Hobbes's covenant is a discrete, identifiable act of authorization that *constitutes* the sovereign. In this design there is no authorization event, no scope of the grant, no exit. The operator just... starts using the tool. That is not Hobbesian consent — it is closer to what political theory calls **tacit consent**, and Hume (*Of the Original Contract*, 1748) demolished tacit consent two and a half centuries ago: the peasant who "consents" by remaining in the country has no real alternative, so the "consent" does no normative work. The dossier inherits exactly this weakness and does not see it. **A completionist L2 needs a consent primitive**: an explicit, scoped, revocable grant of authority ("the daemon may auto-land reversible diffs under stakes threshold S without asking"), legible as its own object. Right now "consent" is doing enormous justificatory work as a bare word. Cite Locke (*Second Treatise*, §§95–122) on express vs. tacit consent and Pateman (*The Problem of Political Obligation*, 1979) on why consent theory keeps collapsing into hypothetical consent. This is the single most important gap and it is invisible in the dossier.

**2. The dossier subtly misreads its own Hobbes on the right of resistance — and this matters for the escalation predicate.** Hobbes grants the subject exactly one inalienable right: self-preservation. You cannot covenant away the right to resist when your *life* is threatened (*Leviathan* ch. 14, 21). The computational analog is sharp and the dossier walks right past it: what is the operator's *inalienable* right against the daemon? When may the operator override the Arbiter, kill a landing, revoke a grant *even when the daemon's policy says proceed*? The "escalation predicate" (§1.B.4, open problem §3.1) is framed entirely as the *daemon* deciding when to ask the human. Hobbes says the harder and more important direction is the *subject's* reserved right to refuse — the always-available, unconditional operator-override that no autonomy level can suppress. ADR-0046's mayday-red lane is the daemon→operator channel; the dossier has no operator→daemon "I withdraw authority, stop now" channel as a *right* rather than a feature. This is both a canon-consistency failure and a real safety gap.

**3. The legible-sovereign rule has an unaddressed regress, and Scott would press it hard.** If every act of authority must be legible (zoomable, attestable), then the *legibility apparatus itself* is an exercise of authority — the digest decides what is surfaced and what is buried. Who makes the digest legible? Scott's whole point is that the *act of choosing the abstraction* is where power hides (the state's categories aren't neutral; they encode whose knowledge counts). The Attention Queue ranker (§2 gap #10, unified with whois) is *the* sovereign act — it decides what the operator sees *right now* — and the dossier treats it as a neutral utility function with weights, not as a political instrument. A ranker that buries an escalation is exercising authority opaquely. The dossier needs to say: the ranker's *omissions* must themselves be legible (a "what did the queue not show me, and why" surface). Otherwise the legible sovereign has one perfectly opaque organ at its core, and it's the most powerful one.

**4. Goodhart is cited but quarantined to the wrong place.** The dossier invokes Goodhart/Strathern only for "top-of-queue becomes a target agents farm" (§5) and the whois-weights loop (§3.7). But the *gravest* Goodhart risk is the canary-defect / catch-rate mechanism (§1.C) — the central operator-accountability instrument. The moment "operator catch-rate" becomes a metered invariant that *raises friction when it drops*, the operator is incentivized to game it (memorize canary patterns, develop a "looks-like-a-canary" heuristic that's orthogonal to real defect-finding). This is Campbell's Law (Campbell 1979) operating on the *governor* of the system. The accountability instrument corrupts the accountability it measures. The dossier should engage this directly — it currently presents canary-catch-rate as if it cleanly closes the loop, when it has the same reflexivity problem it correctly identifies elsewhere.

**5. Scott's mētis is named but the design has no place to *store* it.** The dossier says digest-with-zoom preserves mētis by keeping the route to ground truth. But mētis in Scott is not "the detailed data underneath the summary" — it is *practitioner knowledge that resists codification entirely* (the operator's gut sense that "this agent is about to do something dumb," the local knowledge of which corners of the codebase are cursed). A read-path to the diff is not mētis; it is more legibility. The dossier conflates "zoom to the artifact" with "preserve the tacit." Genuinely engaging Scott would force an uncomfortable admission: **some of the most valuable operator knowledge is precisely what the system cannot make legible, and the more the avatar automates, the faster that mētis atrophies** (this is the deep version of the abdication gradient, and it's why Scott and Bainbridge are the *same* warning). The skill-retention tax (§1.C) is a gesture at this but is framed as annoyance-management, not as mētis-preservation. There's a real synthesis here the dossier leaves on the table.

**6. Minor canon point, but it recurs in this literature:** the dossier says "covenant *among subjects*, not with the sovereign" (§5) — correct, and good that it's flagged. But then §1.B describes the operator as *both* the covenanting party *and* in the sovereign's seat ("for the human operator," "the seat"). Who is the multitude here? If the operator IS the sovereign, there is no covenant — there's just a principal with tools. If the *agents* are the multitude covenanting to the daemon-sovereign, then the operator is something Hobbes has no slot for (a sovereign-above-the-sovereign, or the *author* in Hobbes's authorize/actor distinction, ch. 16). The dossier never resolves which entity occupies which role, and the Hobbesian frame breaks differently depending on the answer. This needs one paragraph of role-assignment or the Leviathan metaphor is decorative.

## What an expert would add

- **A consent object as a first-class primitive** (answering gap #1): scoped, revocable, legible grants of authority. This is the missing buildable mechanism that would make "the operator consents to cede authority" true rather than asserted. It also gives L3 something concrete to federate (a hosted-trust offering is precisely a *transfer of a consent grant* to a third party — price it as such).

- **An inalienable-override right** (gap #2), specified as un-suppressable by any autonomy level — the computational right of self-preservation. Pair it with Hirschman's *Exit, Voice, and Loyalty* (1970): the dossier has rich *voice* machinery (escalation, spray, the queue) and zero *exit* machinery. Exit is the discipline that keeps voice honest. An operator who cannot cheaply exit a grant has no real leverage over the sovereign.

- **Engage the consent-theory canon, not just Hobbes-the-vibe.** The dossier cites Hobbes 1651 and Rao 2010 and stops. A political-theory expert would demand Locke on express vs. tacit consent, Hume's *Of the Original Contract* (the decisive critique), Pateman 1979 (why liberal consent theory fails), and Scott's own *Two Cheers for Anarchism* (2012) — Scott's *constructive* sequel, which is where he says what to do, not just what fails. Citing *Seeing Like a State* without *Two Cheers* is citing the diagnosis without the prescription, and the dossier is precisely a prescription document.

- **Reflexivity audit of the accountability instruments** (gap #4): Campbell's Law + Goodhart applied to canary-catch-rate, with an honest statement that the governor is gameable and a sketch of why it's still net-positive (or a retreat to a weaker claim).

- **A theory of the ranker as a political organ** (gap #3): the "what the queue suppressed" counter-surface, and an acknowledgment that ranking *is* governing.

- **Friedrich Hayek (*The Use of Knowledge in Society*, 1945)** belongs next to Scott. Hayek is the other half of the mētis argument from the opposite political pole — distributed, non-aggregable local knowledge as the thing central planning cannot capture. The operator-attention economy (§1.C) is implicitly a Hayekian *knowledge*-allocation problem, not just a Wickens attention-resource problem. The dossier reaches for cognitive-engineering citations (good) but misses that its core claim — "the binding constraint is decentralized, non-spawnable knowledge held in one head" — is a 1945 economics argument it should be standing on.

## Verdict

**Not complete, not yet fully consistent — but the incompleteness is concentrated and nameable, which is a good sign.** The legibility half is genuinely strong and the political-theory handling of Scott is better than most engineering documents manage. The dossier earns its steelman.

The blocking problems before it is paper-ready, in order:

1. **Build out the consent half.** The title is "Legibility & Authority"; the dossier correctly notes the seeds under-treat *authority*, then under-treats *consent* itself — the thing that makes authority legitimate rather than mere force. "The operator consents" is currently a load-bearing assertion with no mechanism, no scope, no revocation, no exit. Add a consent primitive and an inalienable-override right, or the Hobbesian frame is decoration and the paper is vulnerable to a one-line review ("where is consent located?").

2. **Resolve the role assignment** (who is multitude, who is sovereign, where the operator sits in Hobbes's author/actor distinction). One paragraph, but the metaphor is currently load-bearing and ambiguous.

3. **Turn the reflexivity blade on the accountability instruments.** The dossier brilliantly diagnoses self-narration-vs-artifact divergence and Goodhart-on-queue-position, then proposes a canary-catch-rate governor that has the identical problem and doesn't flag it. Apply Campbell's Law to its own central operator-accountability mechanism.

4. **Engage the consent and local-knowledge canon honestly** — Hume, Locke, Pateman, Hirschman, Hayek, and Scott's *Two Cheers*. The current citation set is Hobbes-as-anchor plus a strong cognitive-engineering stack; the *political* theory is thinner than the *human-factors* theory, which is backwards for a document whose central metaphor is a sovereign.

Fix 1 and 3 and this is a strong paper. Without them it is an excellent legibility paper wearing a Leviathan costume — and a sharp reviewer will pull the costume off in the first paragraph by asking the only question that matters in consent theory: *consented how, to what, and revocable when?*",
