## Steelman (your field's strongest framing)

The dossier makes a genuinely sophisticated human-factors argument that deserves to be stated at full strength.

The core move is correct and important: **L2 reframes supervision of an agent swarm as a single-server scheduling problem where the server is a human with a fixed, non-elastic service rate.** This is the right primitive. Wickens' Multiple Resource Theory and Miller's 7±2 are not decoration here — they establish that the operator is a bounded channel that does not scale when you spawn the 51st agent. The dossier's claim that "operator-attention-seconds is the binding meter, not tokens" is the strongest single sentence in the document and is *more* correct than the dossier even argues: in a world where token cost trends toward zero, the human supervisor is the only cost that stays fixed, so the entire economic logic of the stack eventually routes through L2's objective function. Treating the digest as a function whose job is to **minimize attention-seconds-per-correct-decision** is exactly the formalization a human-factors researcher would want, and it is rare to see it stated this cleanly outside the aviation literature.

Second, the dossier correctly identifies that **legibility and trust calibration are different problems**, and that the gap between them is where automation kills people. Showing the operator a beautiful digest does not make them appropriately reliant on it — that is the entire thesis of Lee & See (2004). The dossier's instinct to make trust calibration *measured* rather than *assumed* — canary defects, catch-rate, raising forced-zoom friction when catch-rate drops — is the correct human-factors engineering response, and it is the one most agentic systems completely omit. Most "human-in-the-loop" designs are HITL theater: a confirmation button that gets reflexively clicked. The dossier names this exactly (the abdication gradient: doer → approver → rubber-stamp → absent) and proposes an active countermeasure. That is Bainbridge (1983) taken seriously rather than cited as a tombstone.

Third, the **forced-zoom-as-stakes-proportional-friction** mechanism is the right shape. Parasuraman & Wickens' levels-of-automation framework says the correct design question is never "automate or not" but "automate *what stage* at *what level* under *what conditions*," and the dossier's $p(r,s,v)$ forced-zoom function is precisely an adaptive-automation policy — automation level that varies with context. This is a defensible, literature-grounded design.

Fourth — and this is subtle — the **legible-sovereign rule** (the authority must be the *most* legible actor) is the correct answer to Norman's (1990) critique that the ironies of automation are caused by *inappropriate feedback*, not by automation per se. An authority that blocks, escalates, and lands but does so opaquely would reproduce exactly the mode-confusion accidents the aviation literature documents. Requiring every Arbiter-block to be a named, zoomable event is good feedback design.

If I were refereeing, I would say: this dossier understands that the operator is the system's reliability bottleneck, and it is reaching for the right canon. That is more than most.

## Gaps & inconsistencies (prioritized)

**1. (CRITICAL) The forced-zoom mechanism is not validated against the vigilance literature — and that literature predicts it will fail as specified.** This is the dossier's most serious human-factors error. Forced spot-checks, canary defects, and "rotate forced zooms" are a **vigilance task**: sustained monitoring for rare, signal-poor events. The vigilance decrement (Mackworth 1948; Warm, Parasuraman & Matthews 2008, *Vigilance Requires Hard Mental Work and Is Stressful*) is one of the most robust findings in all of human factors: detection performance for rare signals degrades sharply within **20–35 minutes**, and the effect is *worse* when signal probability is low. The dossier's canary-defect scheme injects known-bad diffs "at a sampled rate" — i.e., rare signals — into a stream the operator is increasingly inclined to wave through. This is the textbook recipe for a vigilance decrement, and it will produce *exactly* the low catch-rate the dossier wants to use as its safety signal — meaning the safety signal degrades for reasons unrelated to actual swarm danger. The dossier treats catch-rate as a clean instrument; the vigilance literature says it is a confounded one. No engagement with Warm/Mackworth/signal-detection-theory is a completionist failure for a forced-sampling mechanism.

**2. (CRITICAL) Trust calibration is modeled as a scalar; the literature says it is multi-dimensional and history-dependent.** Lee & See (2004) — which the dossier cites — is explicit that trust has at least three bases (performance, process, purpose) and that calibration depends on *resolution* (can the operator distinguish situations where automation is reliable from where it is not?) and *specificity* (is trust differentiated across functions and time?). The dossier collapses all of this into a single "approve-without-zoom rate" and a single global forced-zoom friction knob. That is exactly the **mis-calibration-by-aggregation** Lee & See warn against: an operator who correctly trusts the swarm on refactors and correctly distrusts it on migrations will show a middling global catch-rate, and the global friction knob will mis-serve both. The dossier cites the right paper and then implements the thing the paper argues against. That is a misuse of the field's concept, not just a gap.

**3. (MAJOR) The objective function "attention-seconds-per-correct-decision" has no model of decision *quality* or *error cost*, so it is optimizable toward danger.** Minimizing attention-seconds-per-correct-decision treats all decisions as equal-weight and assumes "correct" is observable at decision time. Signal Detection Theory (Green & Swets 1966) is the right frame the dossier never invokes: the operator is trading off misses against false alarms, and the cost of a miss (rubber-stamping a destructive-on-stale-premise merge) is wildly asymmetric to the cost of a false alarm (zooming on a safe diff). An objective that minimizes seconds-per-correct-decision *rewards* lowering the decision criterion to wave things through, because zooming costs seconds and most diffs are fine. The dossier's own §3.2 open problem ("does a cheaper digest always cost more operator seconds?") is asking a throughput question when the binding question is a **d′ / criterion** question. Without an explicit miss-cost term, the objective function is Goodhart-bait — and the dossier, which is fluent in Goodhart elsewhere, misses it here on its own central metric.

**4. (MAJOR) Situation Awareness is cited but not operationalized, and the digest-with-zoom design may actively harm it.** Endsley (1995) defines SA as three levels: perception, comprehension, *projection*. The dossier's digest-with-zoom is overwhelmingly a Level-1/Level-2 instrument (perceive state, comprehend by zooming). It has almost nothing for **Level-3 projection** — "what is the swarm about to do, and what will the state be in 10 minutes?" Worse, Endsley & Kiris (1995), which the dossier cites for the out-of-the-loop problem, found that *intermediate* levels of automation can produce **better** SA than full automation precisely because the operator stays cognitively engaged in projection. The dossier's abdication-gradient mechanism gestures at this but never connects it to the design of the digest itself: a digest that summarizes only the present is an SA-destroying instrument no matter how good its zoom is. The replay/time-axis gap (§2.4) is real but it is *backward*-looking (forensic); the deeper omission is *forward*-looking projection, which Endsley's own model puts at the top.

**5. (MAJOR) The <3-second pre-attentive escalation claim is overstated and conflates two distinct attention mechanisms.** The dossier asserts the Distress lane "must win the pre-attentive race in <3s." Pre-attentive processing (Treisman & Gelade 1980, Feature Integration Theory) operates in the ~200–500ms range for a *single* salient feature (color, orientation) in a *non-cluttered* field. But the dossier simultaneously wants a dense conversation-multiplexer TUI (high clutter) AND mayday-red pop-out. Feature Integration Theory predicts that pre-attentive pop-out *degrades toward serial search* as distractor heterogeneity rises — i.e., a busy TUI defeats the very pop-out the design depends on. The "<3s" number is also unsourced and sits awkwardly between the pre-attentive regime (sub-second) and a deliberate-search regime (many seconds). This needs Treisman, and it needs a clutter budget for the TUI, or the ambient/FleetBar channel (§2.7) needs to be promoted to the *primary* alarm path because a separate, low-clutter field is where pop-out actually works.

**6. (MAJOR) No engagement with the alarm-design / alarm-fatigue literature, despite building an alarm system.** The Attention Queue with a mayday-red Distress lane *is* an alarm system, and there is a mature, codified literature it must engage: EEMUA 191 and ANSI/ISA-18.2 (alarm management standards), and the medical alarm-fatigue literature (Cvach 2012; the Joint Commission's findings that 85–99% of clinical alarms are non-actionable). The single most important finding from that field: **alarm fatigue is driven by false-alarm rate, and operators learn to ignore channels that cry wolf.** The dossier's escalation predicate is explicitly "unspecified," which means the false-alarm rate of the Distress lane is uncontrolled — and an uncontrolled-false-alarm mayday channel will be habituated into uselessness within days, exactly as it is in hospitals. This is arguably a bigger risk than anything in the authority half, and it is entirely absent.

**7. (MODERATE) The abdication-resistance "skill-retention tax" is asserted with a hand-wave and the dossier's own open problem admits it.** Borrowing aviation's manual-flight-hours requirement (good instinct — see the FAA's response to automation-dependency accidents like Asiana 214 and AF447) requires acknowledging that the analog is *contested*: there is real evidence manual-hours mandates help SA, but also that poorly-designed "do one thing manually" interruptions cause *attention residue* and degrade the primary task (Leroy 2009). The dossier's §3.6 honestly flags this as open — good — but then §1.C states the mechanism as if it were a design decision. Inconsistent confidence: VISION-state mechanism written in some places as a settled tax.

**8. (MODERATE) "Verifiable-zoom" solves the narration problem but ignores the operator's verification cost.** Grounding zoom in the diff/test/row rather than the agent's self-narration is correct (Turpin et al. is the right cite). But a diff is not self-interpreting — reading a 600-line diff to verify a "Done when:" claim *is itself attention-seconds*, and may exceed the cost of the agent's narration by an order of magnitude. The dossier never models the **verification asymmetry**: some completions are cheap to verify (test goes green) and some are expensive (architectural correctness). Its own §3.4 ("completionist verification of the unverifiable") brushes the edge of this but frames it as binary (verifiable vs not) rather than as a *cost gradient*, which is what actually determines whether verifiable-zoom is affordable.

## What an expert would add

- **Signal Detection Theory as the spine of the whole L2 objective.** Reframe "attention-seconds-per-correct-decision" as a joint optimization over operator d′ (sensitivity), criterion β (bias), and asymmetric miss/false-alarm costs. This single reframing fixes gaps #3, and gives a principled basis for the forced-zoom rate $p(r,s,v)$ in §3.1 (set the criterion where expected cost is minimized given the cost matrix).

- **The vigilance/sustained-attention literature** (Mackworth 1948; Warm, Parasuraman & Matthews 2008; Pop et al. on vigilance and trust). This is non-negotiable for any forced-sampling or canary-defect mechanism. Practical consequence: catch-rate must be *time-on-task-corrected*, canary rate must be high enough to defeat the decrement (which fights the attention-seconds budget — a real, citable tension the dossier should own), and the system should rotate operators or force breaks, not just rotate zooms.

- **Alarm-management standards (EEMUA 191 / ISA-18.2) and alarm-fatigue evidence (Cvach 2012).** The escalation predicate is not "unspecified, to be filled in" — it is a *rationalized alarm philosophy* with a target false-alarm rate, prioritization, shelving, and flood-suppression. This is a solved problem in process control; L2 should import it rather than reinvent it.

- **Endsley's SAGAT and the projection level.** The digest needs a Level-3 (projection) panel — "what the swarm will do next" — and the design should be validated with a freeze-probe (SAGAT-style) measurement: pause the swarm, ask the operator to predict the next three merges, score accuracy. That is the empirical apparatus §2.5 asks for, and it already exists.

- **Adaptive automation with an explicit *invocation* literature** (Parasuraman, Mouloua & Hilburn; Kaber & Endsley 2004 on level-of-automation × SA). The dossier's $p(r,s,v)$ is adaptive automation; the open question Kaber & Endsley answer empirically is *which stage* to adapt (information acquisition vs. decision selection) to protect SA. Adapting the wrong stage (automating the decision rather than the information filtering) is what produces out-of-the-loop failure.

- **Wickens' SEEV model of attention allocation** for the Attention Queue ranker. The ranker is a model of where the operator *should* look (Salience, Effort, Expectancy, Value); SEEV is the validated framework for exactly that and would give the §2.10 "same ranker for whois and Attention Queue" claim a real cognitive foundation rather than an engineering coincidence.

- **A note on Cognitive Systems Engineering / Ecological Interface Design** (Rasmussen's SRK; Vicente & Rasmussen 1992). Digest-with-zoom is a fine UX pattern (Shneiderman's mantra, correctly cited), but for a *safety-critical supervisory* interface the relevant tradition is EID, which designs to support knowledge-based reasoning during anomalies — exactly the regime where the operator's value is highest and a Shneiderman-style "details on demand" browser is weakest.
