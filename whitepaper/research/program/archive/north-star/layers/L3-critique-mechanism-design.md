## Steelman (mechanism-design framing)

At its strongest, this is a genuinely sophisticated piece of market engineering, and it deserves credit for three moves that most "agent marketplace" pitches get wrong.

**First, it correctly locates the hard problem in identity, not in pricing.** The load-bearing claim — *"a reputation system is exactly as real as the identity it keys on"* — is the Douceur (2002) Sybil result stated as an engineering constraint, and the dossier draws the right corollary: a reputation mechanism layered on forgeable pseudonyms is not a weak mechanism, it is *no mechanism*, because the strategy space includes costless identity replacement and every punishment is escaped by respawn. Treating non-forgeable `actor_id` as the architectural bottleneck (claim #8, honestly marked DESIGNED) rather than as plumbing is exactly right. Friedman–Resnick's "newcomer policy as a *shape* not a scalar" (full work ability, reduced economic ceiling) is the precisely correct response to the whitewashing-vs-cheap-pseudonyms tension: it makes entry free but makes the *forgivable cost of a bad reputation* positive, which is the only known way to keep the market open while making churn unprofitable.

**Second, the conservation invariant as the market's consistency axiom is the right primitive.** `wallet + escrow + commons = supply`, property-tested over 10k traces, with the corollary "any L3 design that needs a second ledger has broken the contract," is a clean statement of budget-balance — and the insistence that all three sides settle through *one* escrow is the correct way to keep a multi-sided mechanism from leaking surplus through an unmonitored seam. This is the Hurwicz/Myerson discipline applied honestly: a mechanism is defined by its allocation-and-transfer rule, and a transfer rule that can be circumvented is not the mechanism you proved things about.

**Third, the incomplete-contracts framing of asset-rental is genuinely insightful.** Mapping the Arbiter jail to the Grossman–Hart (1986) / Hart–Holmström residual control right — the renter holds runtime control, the owner bears reputational consequence, and the bond bridges the *non-contractible* gap between them — is the correct economic theory for why leasing an agent is contractible *at all*. Most treatments would hand-wave "we'll use a smart contract"; this one identifies that the binding constraint is allocation of control rights over an incompletely-specifiable runtime, which is exactly Hart's point.

And the **canonical honesty** — "three-sided by design, two-sided until reputation ships" — combined with the BUILT/DESIGNED/VISION ledger, is the kind of epistemic discipline that lets a mechanism designer actually engage rather than dismiss. The dossier knows the difference between a theorem and a slogan, which is rarer than it should be.

## Gaps & inconsistencies (prioritized)

**1. The headline open problem (PoA when reputation is for sale) is under-theorized to the point of being a category error as stated.** The dossier frames "price of anarchy when reputation is itself for sale" as a bound to be proven. But *price of anarchy* is a ratio of equilibrium social welfare to optimum **for a fixed game** (Koutsoupias–Papadimitriou 1999; Roughgarden's smoothness framework, 2009). The moment reputation is a tradeable instrument, you have changed the game — the action space now includes "buy/sell reputation," which means the *type space* and the *information structure* have changed. The right object is not PoA but **a mechanism-design question with endogenous signals**: when the costly signal (reputation) can be transferred decoupled from the type it signals, you are in the world of Spence-signaling-with-a-resale-market, and the generic result is *signal-jamming / pooling collapse* — the separating equilibrium that made reputation informative is destroyed. The dossier should cite the **signaling-equilibrium literature** (Spence 1973; and for tradeable certification, the "rating shopping" / issuer-pays literature, Bolton–Freixas–Shapiro 2012, Skreta–Veldkamp 2009) and ask whether reputation-resale admits *any* informative equilibrium, not what its PoA is. As written, it's measuring the anarchy of a game it has already broken.

**2. "Reputation never ends" (no decay) is stated as a virtue but is mechanism-design-hostile, and the dossier half-knows it.** Gap #4 (no revocation path) and Open Problem #8 (surgical retraction on an append-only ledger) are *symptoms of the same root error*: a non-forgetting reputation has unbounded sensitivity to a single poisoned observation, which is precisely what makes it a high-value attack target and what makes the wash-trading attack (Gap #12) pay forever. The optimal-reputation literature is explicit that **bounded memory / geometric discounting is often welfare-improving**, not a concession — see Liu & Skrzypacz (2014, "Limited Records and Reputation Bubbles") and the broader Mailath–Samuelson reputation framework, where *unbounded* records create reputation *cycles and bubbles*, not stability. The dossier asserts the opposite ("an agent can't outrun a decay window") without engaging the result that no-decay is what *enables* the bubble. This is a direct misuse of the concept: it treats forgetting as a vulnerability when the canonical result is that forgetting is a design parameter you tune, and tuning it to ∞ is rarely optimal.

**3. Incentive-compatibility of the *grading oracle* is asserted, never proven — and it's the keystone.** Claim #17 (claim-signaling IC via repeated-game folk theorem) and the four settlement states all bottom out in "the agent picks the work, the daemon derives the grade." But the entire IC argument assumes the **grade is a truthful function of outcome**. Gap #3 (who pays the judges, judge-capture) correctly identifies that the evaluator is unpriced — but this is not a gap, it's a hole in the *core theorem*. A folk-theorem IC result is only as good as the public signal it's conditioned on (Green–Porter 1984; Abreu–Pearce–Stacchetti 1990 — imperfect monitoring folk theorems require the *signal distribution* to be common knowledge and exogenous). If the signal (the grade) is itself produced by a strategic, capturable agent, the folk theorem does not apply — you have a *nested* mechanism-design problem (mechanism design with a strategic mediator). The dossier must either (a) prove the grading oracle is itself strategy-proof, or (b) bond-and-slash the judges (which it gestures at) *and prove that recursion terminates* — i.e., that "rating the raters" converges rather than regressing infinitely. Right now the deepest theorem in the layer sits on an un-modeled foundation.

**4. The Cleanup Lower Bound + competitive underwriting may be jointly inconsistent, and the dossier flags this as "open" when it is closer to a contradiction.** The Cleanup Lower Bound requires bond ≥ worst-case cleanup cost `c`. Youle's competitive insurance lets the bond price be *market-discovered* (Open Problem #6). But a competitive insurance market prices to *expected* loss, not worst-case — that is the entire point of risk pooling. So a market-discovered premium will, in equilibrium, sit *below* the worst-case bound for any agent whose realized risk is below the worst case. Either underwriting violates the Cleanup Lower Bound (and the commons can leak in the tail), or the bound must be reframed as the *insurer's* capital-adequacy constraint (a Solvency-II / reserve-requirement framing, not a per-transaction bond). The dossier treats "does underwriting compose" as open; a mechanism designer would say the *form* of the answer is already determined — you must move from per-transaction worst-case bonds to **insurer reserve adequacy + reinsurance for the tail**, and the open question is only the reserve formula. Cite the **catastrophe-insurance / reinsurance literature** here, not just "Youle's proposal."

**5. Budget-balance vs. incentive-compatibility tension is never confronted (Myerson–Satterthwaite).** The dossier invokes Myerson (1981) for "settlement should be a direct IC mechanism" and separately insists on strict conservation (budget balance). **Myerson–Satterthwaite (1983)** is the inconvenient theorem that you cannot simultaneously have efficiency, incentive-compatibility, individual-rationality, *and* budget balance in bilateral trade under two-sided private information. A three-sided market trading labor/capital/IP under private information about quality is squarely in MS territory. The dossier's conservation invariant *guarantees* budget balance — which by MS means it must be sacrificing either efficiency or IR somewhere, and the dossier never says where. The honest move is to name the impossibility and state which corner is being given up (almost certainly efficiency, via the slashing/commons buffer acting as the budget-balancing wedge that makes the market price-of-anarchy strictly worse than first-best).

**6. Wash-trading defense (Gap #12) is named but the proposed defense is circular.** "Cost-per-settled-outcome must exceed reputation's marginal value" is the right *form*, but reputation's marginal value is *endogenous to the same market* — it's exactly the resale price from Gap #1. You cannot set the listing fee to dominate a quantity that the listing fee helps determine. This needs a fixed-point argument (the fee and the reputation price are jointly determined in equilibrium) or a structural break (make settled outcomes between a counterparty *pair* exhibit sharply diminishing reputation returns — a concavity that caps the wash-trade payoff regardless of price). The dossier has the ingredients but hasn't closed the loop.

## What an expert would add

- **A formal type/information-structure statement.** Before any IC claim, state: what is each side's private type, what is observable, what is the signal, who produces it. Half the gaps dissolve or sharpen once this is written down. Right now the mechanism is described operationally (escrow ceremony, settlement states) but not as a mechanism (type space → message space → allocation+transfer rule → equilibrium concept).

- **Explicit equilibrium concept and solution concept per claim.** The folk-theorem claims need: which equilibrium (subgame-perfect? sequential? public perfect?), what δ, what monitoring structure (perfect/imperfect-public/private). The dossier cites the folk theorem generically; with a *strategic* grader the relevant object is imperfect-private-monitoring, where folk theorems are far weaker (Ely–Hörner–Olszewski 2005).

- **The collusion analysis the dossier defers.** Cross-harbor cartels (Open #5) + correlated equilibrium (§lim-correlated) is the right frame, but the canonical tool is **Myerson's collusion-proofness / the coalition-proof / strong-implementation literature** (and for repeated settings, the *renegotiation-proofness* condition). Multi-homing + portable reputation is a recipe for a **rating cartel**; the dossier should cite the credit-rating-agency collusion literature directly, since it is the closest real-world analogue to "the harbor's universities and rating agencies."

- **A reserve/solvency framing for the bond.** As in Gap #4 — once underwriting enters, the per-transaction worst-case bond is the wrong primitive. Bring in capital-adequacy (reserve ratio, tail reinsurance) explicitly.

- **VCG/AGV as a sanity baseline.** If strict budget balance is non-negotiable, the dossier should at least position itself against **d'Aspremont–Gérard-Varet (AGV)** — the mechanism that *does* get budget balance + Bayesian-IC at the cost of dominant-strategy IC and IR. Naming why AGV doesn't directly apply (or does) would show the mechanism-design baseline has been considered, not skipped.

- **One worked adversarial equilibrium.** Pick the wash-trade or reputation-resale attack and actually solve for the attacker's best response and the resulting equilibrium. The dossier enumerates attacks beautifully but never *solves a game*. A mechanism-design referee will want to see at least one equilibrium computed, not just threat-modeled.
