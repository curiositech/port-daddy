# Exposition + Figure Review: The Anchor Protocol

`whitepaper/source/anchor-protocol-whitepaper.tex` is Chapter V of the seven-volume Port Daddy Coordination Papers, ~1171 lines, compiling to a security-protocol paper on the Anchor Protocol — the capability-token authentication/delegation/revocation scheme (Harbor Cards) that the Port Daddy daemon uses to authenticate agents to each other on `localhost`. It covers four protocol phases (symmetric HMAC pinning, Ed25519 asymmetric identity, Macaroon-style multi-hop delegation, cuckoo-filter revocation), a three-layer verification stack (ProVerif symbolic proofs, Kani bounded model checking on the deployed Rust core, runtime conformance tests), full ProVerif model listings in three appendices, and an unusually candid limitations/status apparatus. Per the series framing it is meant to sit alongside Chapter VI (*The Bonded Commons*) and Chapter VII (*The Federated Harbor*) as the protocol deep-dive, one of seven "whitepaper" volumes meant to popularize the corresponding formal research papers for a smart, non-specialist reader. As shipped, its content and rigor are excellent, but its *register* — section titles, opening moves, absence of a scene or express lane — is that of the underlying formal paper, not of a popularizing companion volume. That is this review's central finding, developed in Part A.

## Part A — Text/exposition changes

1. **Location**: Document-wide — the eight `\section` titles (lines 184, 210, 296, 342, 419, 523, 648, 693).
   **Issue**: One Path For All Readers / no popularizing register. The section titles are formal-paper section names — "Introduction: The 'Local Swarm' Problem," "The Anchor Protocol Architecture," "Related Work," "Formal Verification Strategy," "Verification Algorithms," "Implementation: Deployed Rust Core," "Security Analysis," "Conclusion" — indistinguishable from the underlying research paper's table of contents. A whitepaper meant to popularize that paper for a non-specialist should signal, in its own headings, the questions a smart lay reader actually has: what problem this solves, what it protects against, how you'd know it's right, what happens when you have to take a token back, and where the guarantees stop. None of the current titles ask a question or name a reader's concern; all of them name an academic apparatus.
   **Current text**: `\section{Introduction: The ``Local Swarm'' Problem}` … `\section{Related Work}` … `\section{Formal Verification Strategy}` … `\section{Verification Algorithms}` … `\section{Security Analysis}` … `\section{Conclusion}`
   **Proposed rewrite**: Rename (keeping `\label`s and internal structure intact, subsections unchanged):
   - §1 → **"What problem this solves"**
   - §2 → keep as **"The Anchor Protocol Architecture"** but retitle §2.4 (line 255, currently "Phase 4: Revocation via Cuckoo Filter") to **"Withdrawing a card before it expires"**
   - §3 (Related Work) → fold into a short "Where this sits" subsection or move to an appendix; a popularizing volume does not need a literature-review section at this length in the main flow
   - §4 (Formal Verification Strategy) + §5 (Verification Algorithms) → merge/retitle as **"How we know it is correct"**, with the pseudocode algorithms as a subsection ("the procedures, precisely") rather than a peer of the top-level narrative
   - §7 (Security Analysis) → split: §7.1 Threat Model → **"Threats this takes seriously"**; §7.3 Limitations → **"Where this stops"**
   - §8 (Conclusion) → **"Proofs are leverage, not decoration"** (this is already the abstract's own framing — line 151 — promote it to the closing section title it deserves)
   - New short subsections worth adding under the existing Limitations material (see items 12–13 below): **"A program is not a person"** and **"Localhost is a real network."**
   **Priority**: high.

2. **Location**: Lines 148–152, the abstract.
   **Issue**: Boundary Burial (mild) / no one-breath sentence (Rail A missing). The abstract is a single dense paragraph of qualification-heavy academic prose with no plain-English claim up front. A reader cannot extract "here is the one sentence this paper proves" without reading the whole abstract twice. The house style's express lane (one italic sentence, then "formal statement in the box below") is absent everywhere in this document, not just here.
   **Current text**: "As AI agents transition from isolated copilots to collaborative, autonomous swarms, local development environments face a crisis of coordination and trust. Existing tools designed for human-driven, static infrastructures lack the primitives required to prevent dynamic agents from corrupting shared state or squatting on ephemeral ports. This paper introduces the **Anchor Protocol**…"
   **Proposed rewrite**: Add one italic sentence before the current abstract text: *"Agents on the same machine cannot be trusted to behave, so the Anchor Protocol makes every agent prove, cryptographically, exactly which capabilities it holds and for how long — provably in the design, and checked at the level of the running Rust code."* Follow with the abstract as written, trimmed of one clause of qualification per sentence where two currently stack (e.g., the final two sentences of the abstract can merge: "These layers do not amount to a proof of the entire implementation or of hardware-level constant time, and the multi-hop proof is structurally bounded rather than session-unbounded — Section~\ref{sec:limitations} records those boundaries explicitly.")
   **Priority**: high.

   **Issue**: Definitions First / no scene.    **Priority**: high.

4. **Location**: Line 257 ("bitwise merging impossibility").

7. **Location**: Lines 687–690, the three Limitations bullets.
   **Issue**: Boundary Burial by uneven treatment. The "Delegation-chain depth" bullet (line 687) is a full paragraph naming the exact files, constants, and enforcement points (`HARD_MAX_DELEGATION_DEPTH=8`, `lib/tube-spawner-router.ts`, `lib/delegation-chain.ts`, `lib/cap-attenuation-monitor.ts`). The next two bullets — "PID Binding" and "Local Transport Security" — are one sentence each with no equivalent grounding, even though both name real, unresolved attack surfaces (a process-identity spoofing gap, and bearer-token sniffing on shared multi-user hosts). A careful reader will notice the asymmetry and reasonably wonder whether the thin bullets were rigorously scoped at all.
   **Current text**: "**PID Binding:** The ``Ghost in the Harbor'' scenario requires binding tokens to the requesting PID, which is handled at the OS level." / "**Local Transport Security:** On multi-user systems, local unix sockets or loopback TLS should be used to prevent local bearer token sniffing."
   **Proposed rewrite**: Expand both to match the delegation-depth bullet's concreteness, and consider promoting each to its own short subsection (feeding directly into the "A program is not a person" / "Localhost is a real network" titles proposed in item 1):
   > "**A program is not a person.** A Harbor Card authenticates a *capability holder*, not a human. The 'Ghost in the Harbor' scenario (§1) is really a PID-binding gap: nothing in the token cryptography stops a different process from presenting a card whose bearer process has died, if the OS-level PID check is skipped or racy. Port Daddy binds cards to the requesting PID via `pd-daemon`'s socket-credential check (`SO_PEERCRED` on Linux, equivalent on macOS) at connection time, not inside the token — this is enforced by the OS, not proved by ProVerif, and a daemon that skips the check is unprotected regardless of card validity.
   >
   > **Localhost is a real network.** `localhost` is not a trust boundary. On a shared or multi-user machine, any local process — including one belonging to a different user — can open a TCP connection to a loopback port or read a Unix socket with the wrong permissions, and a bearer token sent in the clear over either is as interceptable as one sent over the open internet. We recommend Unix domain sockets with filesystem permissions restricting access to the daemon's own user, or loopback TLS where multi-user sharing is required; the protocol does not currently enforce either, so this is an operational requirement on the deployer, not a property the Anchor Protocol itself guarantees."
   **Priority**: high.

## Part B — Existing figures/tables: clarity audit

Seven figures are actually `\input` into the compiled document (all under `figures/fig-anchor-*.tex`); three tables appear inline/in-appendix; and eight additional files matching the task's `fig-anchor-*.tex` / `diag-*.tex` glob exist on disk but are **never referenced by any `\input` or `\includegraphics`** anywhere in this whitepaper (confirmed by grep across the whole `website-v2/public/whitepaper` tree) — they are dead weight or orphaned drafts. Both groups are audited below.

### Figures actually in the compiled document

1. **`fig:anchor-four-phases`** (figures/fig-anchor-four-phases.tex, `\input` at line 215, four-card horizontal progression with a mechanization-status band).
   **What it currently shows**: Four phase cards (HS256 → Ed25519 → Macaroon delegation → Cuckoo revocation) in a left-to-right chain, each paired with a "Forecloses" threat card below it and a "Needs:" rationale pill on the connecting arrow, plus a bottom band summarizing ProVerif status per phase.
   **What the reader should take away**: Each phase exists because the previous one left a specific, named gap open, and every phase is mechanically checked.
   **Will they get it?** Yes, on the mechanism (Cleveland-McGill: this is a left-to-right ordinal sequence, position along a line — the strongest encoding — doing the real work, not color). The "Forecloses"/"Needs" pairing is a genuinely good relation-map instance (Gentner-style: arrows carry a relation, "needs X," not just a noun). Caption states the finding, not just the parts. **But**: text is set in `\sffamily` throughout (line 16 of the fig file) against the body's serif (`lmodern`) — cheap-tell #2, present in every figure in this set (see cross-cutting note below). No trust-boundary marking, but none is needed here — this figure is about protocol evolution, not an adversary interaction, so the omission is appropriate for this particular figure.
   **Verdict**: Good, with the font fix below.
   **Concrete fix**: Change `font=\sffamily\small` (and all descendant `font=\sffamily…` style keys) to the body serif, e.g. drop the `\sffamily` and keep the weight/size modifiers (`\small`, `\bfseries`, `\scriptsize`).

2. **`fig:anchor-capability-attenuation`** (figures/fig-anchor-capability-attenuation.tex, `\input` at line 219, nested "Russian doll" capability rings).
   **What it currently shows**: Three concentric rounded boxes (root/daemon cap, AgentA cap, AgentB cap) shrinking in size and TTL, principals delegating inward, and an invariants side panel plus a "forbidden grant" dashed arrow.
   **What the reader should take away**: A delegated capability is strictly smaller than its parent's, on both rights and time, and this is enforced, not just conventional.
   **Will they get it?** Mostly. Nesting communicates "subset" reasonably well (closer to a Venn/Hasse-style convention than a bar chart, so Cleveland-McGill's ranking doesn't map cleanly, but the nesting *is* the standard notation for this claim and is drawn correctly — smaller box strictly inside the larger one, matching the Hasse-diagram convention that vertical/spatial containment must encode the order). The "AgentB cannot grant net:* downstream" dashed arrow is a good preempt of the natural misread ("if AgentB has *some* rights, can't it hand out others too?"). Font is again `\sffamily` against a serif body. No trust-boundary marking — again appropriate, since every actor shown (Daemon, AgentA, AgentB) is inside the same trust domain; this figure is about attenuation, not adversary access.
   **Verdict**: Good, with the font fix.
   **Concrete fix**: Same `\sffamily` → serif fix as above.

3. **`fig:anchor-alg-confusion`** (figures/fig-anchor-alg-confusion.tex, `\input` at line 231, two-row before/after: naive verifier vs. pinned verifier).
   **What it currently shows**: Two horizontal message-sequence-style rows. Top: Daemon issues a token, an ATTACKER node rewrites `alg: HS256` to `alg: none`, a "Naive verifier" node accepts it (bad, labeled in cobalt). Bottom: same rewrite, but a "Pinned verifier" rejects it (good, labeled in teal).
   **What the reader should take away**: Trusting the token's self-declared algorithm is the vulnerability; pinning the algorithm out-of-band at issuance closes it.
   **Will they get it?** Yes — this is the strongest security figure in the set, and close to an Alice-and-Bob message-sequence chart (the community convention this document's own domain calls for). **However, this is exactly the figure the security-figure convention says needs a trust-boundary marking, and it does not have one.** The ATTACKER node sits inline in the same visual register as Daemon and Verifier — same box style, same border weight, distinguished only by an accent-color fill and the word "ATTACKER" — with no boundary line, shaded region, or "untrusted network" band separating where the daemon's authority ends and the attacker-controlled channel begins. A reader who skims (rather than reads every label) can miss that the middle of the diagram *is* the adversary's territory. Per the task brief and the figures-and-examples reference's "Community conventions differ" section: "Security — Alice-and-Bob message-sequence charts, and threat figures that mark trust boundaries explicitly. A threat diagram without a boundary marking is missing its central claim." This figure is missing that claim.
   **Verdict**: Needs work — the single highest-priority figure fix in the document.
   **Concrete fix**: Add a shaded vertical band (low-opacity `hhsand` or a dashed rectangle in `hhink`) behind the ATTACKER node and the token-rewrite arrows in both rows, labeled once "untrusted network (Dolev-Yao adversary controls everything inside this band)." Keep Daemon and Verifier nodes outside the band in both rows. This single change would also retroactively justify reusing the same band convention in figures 5 and 6 below, which have the same gap.

4. **`fig:anchor-delegation-inline`** (figures/fig-anchor-delegation-inline.tex, `\input` at line 253, three-hop chain with per-hop signature and nonce annotations).
   **What it currently shows**: origin → Hop1 → Hop2 → Hop3 → target, with a signed `hopBind` tuple above each hop and a freshness-nonce badge below, plus a "Walker rejects if" callout box listing four failure conditions.
   **What the reader should take away**: Each hop cryptographically binds to its immediate predecessor and successor, so splicing, replay, and substitution are each independently blocked.
   **Will they get it?** Reasonably — this is a good instance of a proof-structure-adjacent diagram (each rejection condition maps to a named attack), but it is denser than it needs to be: the signed-tuple notation above each hop (`sign_{id1}(hopBind(n1, ⊥, id2, h))`) duplicates, symbol-for-symbol, text already given in the body at line 251 and in Algorithm 3 (line 486). A reader who has already read the algorithm gets no new information from the figure beyond the shape of the chain; a reader who has *not* yet read the algorithm is handed dense mathematical notation with no walk-through. This risks the "figure restates the text" failure mode. `\sffamily` again against serif body. No trust boundary — appropriate here, since the diagram shows the verifier's internal walk, not an attacker interaction.
   **Verdict**: Adequate, could be sharper.
   **Concrete fix**: Simplify the per-hop annotation to plain language ("signs: I vouch for id₂, chain continues" / "fresh nonce, never seen before") and move the full symbolic tuple into the caption or a footnote for the reader who wants it — this converts the figure from a restatement into a genuine second encoding (spatial chain + plain-language annotation) of the same claim the algorithm makes symbolically.
