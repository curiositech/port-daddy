\begin{abstract}\noindent
A swarm of coding agents sharing one machine collides over the same scarce
things: ports, files, locks, and the truth about who did what. The instinct,
trained on a decade of distributed-systems literature, is to reach for consensus
--- replicate the state, run an agreement protocol. Port Daddy's kernel makes the
opposite, and we argue correct, move: it collapses the entire problem onto a
\textbf{single writer over a single local SQLite file in write-ahead-log mode},
and lets the operating system's file lock serialize every mutation. There is one
decider, so there is no agreement to reach.

This paper presents that kernel (layer \textbf{L0} of the Harbor stack) and the
\emph{built} half of the coordination substrate above it (layer \textbf{L1}:
tube, pheromones, commitments, the cryptographic \texttt{auth-chain}, the
Arbiter) as a \textbf{single-writer transactional reference monitor} in the
Lampson--Anderson sense: a small, tamper-evident mediator of every
security-relevant operation, realized locally. We state the kernel's invariants
as theorems --- mutual exclusion, idempotence, the consistency model
(serializable transactions, linearizable claim history), oracle-bound closure ---
and we are deliberate about where the promises stop. In particular we
\emph{split} durability by fault class: a returned-success write survives a
\emph{process} crash but is \emph{not guaranteed} against power loss under the
shipped pragma set; and we correct a widespread over-claim, showing that the
Arbiter is a post-commit \emph{detector}, not a pre-commit regimenter, with real
regimentation credited to the \texttt{PRIMARY KEY} constraint and the boot gate.
The kernel is solid where it is local and provisional exactly where the economy
(Paper~4) will need it to become cryptographic and continuous --- and we say so
in the same words throughout.
\end{abstract}

\vspace{0.4cm}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} reference monitor, single-writer transactions, SQLite
WAL, durability by fault class, linearizability, deontic logic, commitments,
stigmergy, local-first coordination, multi-agent systems

\section{Where this paper sits, and what it promises}
% ═════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

\input{figures/fig-swk-stack-map}

Every paper in the Harbor Volume opens with the same map
(Figure~\ref{fig:swk-stack-map}) so the volume reads as one thing rather than four
overlapping drafts. The map is a ladder. Each rung serves a different
\emph{whom}: the \textbf{machine} (L0, the kernel --- this paper's core), the
\textbf{agents} (L1, the coordination protocol --- this paper owns its
\emph{built} substrate), the \textbf{operator} (L2, legibility --- Paper~1), and
the \textbf{market between operators} (L3, economy --- Paper~4). You climb the
ladder in order, and you never buy a rung before you need it.

This paper is the load-bearing floor. The other three cite it: Paper~1 reads the
kernel's claims, bus, and Arbiter to render the swarm; Paper~3 leans the entire
notion of a \emph{person} on the kernel's continuity organs; Paper~4's bond
ledger is, mechanically, another set of tables under the same single writer, and
its cross-harbor capability transfer rides the kernel's cryptographic
\texttt{auth-chain}. If this floor is unsound, everything above it inherits the
fault. So the discipline of this paper is to state each promise precisely and,
where the promise has an edge, to draw the edge in ink.

\subsection{The one-sentence thesis}

\pullquote{Port Daddy's kernel is a single-writer transactional reference monitor
over a local SQLite/WAL file --- the one process that mediates contended
resources, and the one place where ``true'' is decided rather than asserted.}

That is the whole claim, and it has two halves, which are the kernel's entire job;
everything else is convenience.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{Durability (with an edge):} a write that returned success is
  durable across the death of any agent --- and, we will be careful, across the
  death of the \emph{daemon process}, though \emph{not} unconditionally across
  power loss.
  \item \textbf{Mediation (with a correction):} a state the kernel \emph{regiments}
  cannot come to exist; a state the kernel merely \emph{enforces} is detected and
  compensated within a bounded window. These are different guarantees and we will
  never conflate them.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Why ``reference monitor,'' and why \emph{not} consensus}

The right mental model is the \textbf{reference monitor} of Anderson and
Lampson~\cite{anderson1972,lampson1974}: a mediator that is \emph{always invoked}
(complete mediation), \emph{tamper-evident}, and \emph{small enough to verify}.
The kernel instantiates this not as an abstract security kernel but as a concrete
local daemon over one SQLite file. Complete mediation is achieved \emph{by
construction}: because every mutation routes through one writer holding one file
lock, the operating system's serialization \emph{is} the mediation primitive ---
Saltzer and Schroeder's ``economy of mechanism''~\cite{saltzer1975protection}
done with one moving part instead of $N$ hand-rolled critical sections.

\keyidea{The swarm \emph{looks} like a distributed-systems problem --- many
agents, contention, failover --- so the trained reflex is Raft, Paxos, CRDTs. The
kernel's defining move is to refuse that problem. One writer, one machine, one
file: there is nothing to \emph{agree} on, so the consensus impossibility of
Fischer, Lynch, and Paterson~\cite{flp1985} does not bite. It is sidestepped, not
solved. Distribution is an L3 concern (Paper~4); pushing Raft down into L0 would
be the wrong layer.}

This is the single most important architectural decision a local-first
coordination layer can make, and the field is littered with systems that got it
wrong by manufacturing a consensus problem they did not have. We will state the
formal payoff of the choice as a theorem in \S\ref{sec:invariants}: a single
writer over WAL gives \emph{serializable} transactions, and one decider gives
\emph{linearizable} external behavior for claims and locks --- the property that
lets the protocol layer treat ``who holds this'' as ground truth without any
agreement protocol at all.

\section{The kernel as seven organs}
\label{sec:organs}
% ═════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

We organize the kernel into seven \emph{organs}, each a contract the daemon must
hold for the stack above to be coherent (Figure~\ref{fig:swk-seven-organs}). The
flat noun-list from the project's early notes --- ``ports, claims, sessions,
tube, pheromones, commitments, Arbiter, memory'' --- is the symptom of treating
the kernel as a database. It is not a database. It is a system of record plus a
mediator, and naming the organs surfaces the connective tissue the noun-list
hides.

The \textbf{substrate organ} is the floor; the other six are, mechanically,
tables \emph{with discipline} over the same file. We treat the substrate and the
two organs that carry the paper's sharpest corrections (resource/exclusion and
obligation/enforcement) in full, and the rest with the depth their novelty
warrants.

\section{The substrate organ: one writer, one file}
\label{sec:substrate}
% ═════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

The substrate is the bedrock everything else is just a table in. Its job is to
make two promises --- durability and serialized mutation --- and to make them
honestly. We cover the single-writer discipline here and durability in
\S\ref{sec:durability}, because durability is where the most consequential
correction lives.

\subsection{The single-writer discipline}

\input{figures/fig-swk-single-writer}

The kernel's concurrency story (Figure~\ref{fig:swk-single-writer}) is: dozens of
\texttt{pd} invocations and agents funnel through one writer --- the daemon, the
sole holder of a read--write connection to the database file
(\textbf{\texttt{lib/db.ts}}, the module that opens and configures the SQLite
handle) --- and SQLite's operating-system file lock plus
\texttt{busy\_timeout=5000} serialize all mutations. We must be precise about
\emph{what} serializes writers, because there are two stories and only one is
true at a time.

\begin{definition}[Single-writer discipline]\label{def:single-writer}
All state-mutating operations route through a single daemon process holding one
SQLite write connection. Concurrency among the many clients (CLI invocations,
agents over MCP or tube, fleet sorties) is resolved by the daemon's request
queue; the SQLite file lock is the \emph{backstop} that preserves correctness if
the discipline is ever violated by a second writer (for example a stray
direct-CLI write), not the primary serialization mechanism.
\end{definition}

\keyidea{``Single-writer'' is an architectural \emph{discipline} (everything goes
through the daemon), backstopped --- not replaced --- by SQLite's file lock. This
distinction matters: SQLite permits multiple writer connections (it serializes
them, it does not reject them), so the property holds because of where writes are
routed, not because the database forbids a second writer. The same risk that
motivates the kernel's binary-drift guards (a second \emph{copy} of the daemon
running) is the risk that a second writer appears.}

\subsection{The pragma set is the durability claim}

The substrate's configuration is short and load-bearing. In WAL mode
(\texttt{journal\_mode=WAL}) with \texttt{synchronous=NORMAL},
\texttt{wal\_autocheckpoint=200}, \texttt{busy\_timeout=5000},
\texttt{foreign\_keys=ON}, and a clean-shutdown \texttt{wal\_checkpoint(TRUNCATE)},
the kernel inherits exactly the crash semantics that pragma set defines. The
claim ``a write that returned success survives a crash'' reduces \emph{entirely}
to what WAL with \texttt{synchronous=NORMAL} guarantees --- which is the subject
of \S\ref{sec:durability}, and which is not what an unguarded reading assumes.

\subsection{Schema by union, not by sequencer}

There is no migration sequencer. Every module self-initializes its tables with
\texttt{CREATE TABLE IF NOT EXISTS} plus lazy \texttt{ALTER TABLE} migrations; the
schema is the \emph{union} of what each \texttt{createFoo(db)} factory declares,
in whatever order modules load. This is elegant for greenfield --- any organ can
create its own tables independently --- and a latent hazard for renames,
backfills, and multi-column migrations, for which there is no down-migration and
no version table (open problem OP-7, \S\ref{sec:openproblems}).

\subsection{Path and ownership invariants}

The database resolves to a single canonical path with \texttt{chmod 0o600}
(the file historically carried the Ed25519 signing key in plaintext; that secret
is mid-migration to the OS keychain via \textbf{\texttt{lib/keychain.ts}}, see
\S\ref{sec:threatmodel}). A fail-closed guard, \texttt{assertNotProdInTest} ---
the analogue of Rails' \texttt{ProtectedEnvironment} --- refuses to open the live
registry from a test context. This is Saltzer--Schroeder fail-safe-defaults
applied at the one place a test could corrupt production truth.

\exercises{%
\textbf{(check)} The schema-by-union design lets any module create its tables in
any order. Give a concrete two-module example where a lazy \texttt{ALTER TABLE}
rename would be unsafe under this design.\par
\textbf{(trace)} Follow a single \texttt{pd claim} from CLI to disk. At how many
points does the single-writer discipline (Def.~\ref{def:single-writer}) hold by
\emph{routing} versus by the \emph{file lock}?\par
\textbf{(open, $\bigstar$ OP-7)} Can idempotent self-init be extended to safe
renames/backfills/down-migrations while preserving ``any module, any order''? Or
is a version table unavoidable?}

\section{Durability, split by fault class}
\label{sec:durability}
% ═════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

This is the most important correction in the paper, so it gets its own section.
The kernel's foundational promise --- ``a write that returned success survives''
--- is true, but only for the fault class a careful reader expects and not for the
one the pitch most invites.

\input{figures/fig-swk-durability-faultclass}

\subsection{Why one label is a lie here}

Gray and Reuter~\cite{grayreuter1992} draw the canonical line between
\emph{atomicity/consistency} (a transaction is all-or-nothing and never leaves the
store corrupt) and \emph{durability} (the ``D'' in ACID: a committed transaction
survives subsequent failures). Under SQLite WAL with \texttt{synchronous=NORMAL},
the WAL is \emph{not} fsync'd on every commit --- it is synced at checkpoint. Per
SQLite's own documentation, in WAL mode \texttt{NORMAL} ``commits might lose
durability following a power loss or hard reset,'' though the database remains
uncorrupted. So the honest statement splits in two.

\begin{property}[Durability by fault class]\label{prop:durability}
Let a write return success under the shipped pragma set.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{I1a (process-crash durability).} If the \emph{daemon process}
  dies (\texttt{kill -9}, a panic, an OOM), the write survives: the data is in the
  operating system's page cache, which outlives the process. \Built.
  \item \textbf{I1b (power-loss durability).} If the \emph{operating system}
  crashes or power is lost \emph{before the next checkpoint}, the most recent
  committed writes may roll back. \NotGuar{} under \texttt{synchronous=NORMAL}; it
  would require \texttt{synchronous=FULL} or a checkpoint on the commit path.
\end{itemize}
\end{property}

\pitfall{The in-code comment justifying the pragma choice
(\texttt{db.ts:388}) reads ``NORMAL sync is safe in WAL mode --- WAL already
guarantees crash safety.'' This is the standard misconception: it conflates
crash-\emph{consistency} (true: the DB is never corrupt) with \emph{durability}
(false for power loss). The comment does not merely under-document the trade; it
\emph{contradicts} the Gray--Reuter citation the dossier rests I1 on. A kernel
paper cannot ship the unqualified claim. We split it.}

\subsection{Why the trade is defensible --- and where it stops being so}

Trading power-loss durability for throughput is a reasonable default for a
local-first developer tool: the failure (lose the last few hundred milliseconds of
claims after a power cut) is benign and self-correcting --- agents re-claim,
heartbeats resume. What is \emph{not} defensible is letting the pitch imply the
stronger guarantee. The remedy for any caller who genuinely needs I1b ---
say, a future bond-ledger write at L3 where lost money is not benign --- is to
elevate that write path to \texttt{synchronous=FULL} or to force a checkpoint, and
to pay the throughput cost knowingly. The kernel must expose that choice, not bury
it.

\subsection{A recovery story, not just a durability story}

Durability is about what survives; recovery is about what happens next. On daemon
restart with a dirty WAL, SQLite replays the log to a consistent state (I1a's
mechanism). But the kernel-level questions are larger: who validates the audit
chain on boot, and what becomes of a half-applied cross-organ operation
(\S\ref{sec:atomicity}) after a crash? The hook exists --- \texttt{pd attest}'s
``refuse-to-serve on CRITICAL fail'' (\S\ref{sec:attest}) --- but boot-time WAL
recovery plus integrity check plus chain verification should be a named invariant,
and today it is closer to \BuiltWeak{} than \Built.

\exercises{%
\textbf{(check)} Classify each fault as I1a-survivable or I1b-exposed:
(a) \texttt{kill -9} of the daemon; (b) \texttt{sudo shutdown -r now}; (c) yanking
the power cord; (d) an unhandled exception in a route handler.\par
\textbf{(trace)} A claim commits at $t_0$; the next \texttt{wal\_autocheckpoint}

\section{The resource organ: claims, locks, and fair exclusion}
\label{sec:resource}
% ═════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

Ports are the namesake and the original sin. The resource organ
(\textbf{\texttt{lib/services.ts}} for ports, \textbf{\texttt{lib/locks.ts}} for
generic mutexes, the \texttt{session\_files} table for edit-surface claims) is the
exclusion primitive every higher layer's ownership reduces to.

\subsection{Claim granularity is richer than ``claims''}

The early notes collapsed three primitives into one word. The kernel actually
distinguishes \textbf{whole-file}, \textbf{line-range}, and \textbf{symbol-path}
claims (the \texttt{session\_files} columns \texttt{file\_path},
\texttt{start\_line}, \texttt{end\_line}, \texttt{symbol},
\texttt{symbol\_path}). This is the substrate for the project's ``reserve regions,
not whole files'' direction and for semantic-conflict prediction over a
tree-sitter symbol index --- but at L0 it is just exclusion at three granularities.

\subsection{The claim lifecycle as a finite-state machine}

\input{figures/fig-swk-claim-lifecycle}

Figure~\ref{fig:swk-claim-lifecycle} states the four properties L1 builds typed
ownership on, as a state machine. We give the central one as a theorem.

\begin{theorem}[Atomic mutual exclusion]\label{thm:exclusion}
At most one holder exists per resource key (port $\mid$ file-region $\mid$ named
lock) at any instant. The acquire path is a bare \texttt{INSERT} against a
\texttt{PRIMARY KEY}; a concurrent acquirer's insert fails with
\texttt{SQLITE\_CONSTRAINT}, and the loser is handed the current holder.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Acquire is a single atomic \texttt{INSERT} on the resource key, which is the
table's primary key. SQLite guarantees at most one row per primary-key value;
the second concurrent insert violates the constraint and is rejected atomically.
Because the operation is a single statement, there is no interleaving window
\emph{within} the insert. Re-claim by the current holder is an idempotent upsert
(refresh \texttt{last\_seen}); release is idempotent (delete-if-exists). Expiry is
swept lazily on the next read (\texttt{DELETE WHERE expires\_at < now}). The four
behaviors are exactly properties I2--I4.\qed
\end{proof}

\pitfall{Theorem~\ref{thm:exclusion} is sound for the \emph{fresh-contention}
case. There is a subtler hazard the dossier elided: the lock path runs
\texttt{releaseExpired} (a \texttt{DELETE}) and \emph{then} \texttt{acquire} (the
\texttt{INSERT}) as two separate statements, not one transaction
(\texttt{lib/locks.ts}). On the single daemon connection this is serialized by the
connection itself; \emph{across} connections (the scenario the single-writer prose
sometimes invokes), the default \texttt{DEFERRED} transaction opens a window for
\texttt{SQLITE\_BUSY} mid-sequence rather than clean serialization. The fix is
\texttt{BEGIN IMMEDIATE} (or wrapping acquire in a transaction); it is
\emph{absent} from that path today. So Theorem~\ref{thm:exclusion} holds because
all writes route through the one daemon connection
(Def.~\ref{def:single-writer}), \emph{not} because the expire-then-acquire
sequence is itself atomic. State which story you mean; do not tell both.}

\subsection{The missing state: fairness}

The conspicuous gap in Figure~\ref{fig:swk-claim-lifecycle} is the absence of a
\textsc{queued} state. Locks have a TTL but \emph{no} wait-list and \emph{no}
fairness: acquisition is racy first-come, so a fast agent churning a hot file can
starve a slow agent indefinitely. There is no priority, no FIFO wait-list, no
anti-starvation guarantee. This is open problem OP-1, and it has teeth: adding
bounded-wait may force a real scheduler into the kernel, which would push L0
toward L1.

\exercises{%
\textbf{(check)} Why does releasing a lock you do not hold return success rather
than an error? Which property (I2--I4) is this, and what would break if it raised?\par
\textbf{(trace)} Two agents call \texttt{acquire} on the same fresh key at the
same instant. Walk the \texttt{PRIMARY KEY} insert for both and show exactly where
the loser learns the holder's identity.\par
\textbf{(open, $\bigstar$ OP-1)} Can L0 add a bounded-wait fairness guarantee to
claims/locks while keeping the single-writer, no-background-scheduler simplicity?
Is a SQLite-backed FIFO wait-list with TTL'd reservations enough, or does fairness
force a scheduler (and thus blur the L0/L1 boundary)?}

\section{The communication organ: the bus, stigmergy, and two delegation chains}
\label{sec:comm}
% ═════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

\input{figures/fig-swk-comm-organ}

The communication organ (Figure~\ref{fig:swk-comm-organ}) is where L0 stops being
a lockbox and becomes a substrate for conversation. It is \Built; the
\emph{semantics} that ride on top of it (typed performatives, protocol FSMs) are
Paper~1's L1 chapter. Three pieces matter here.

\subsection{Tube: the carrier envelope}

\textbf{Tube} (\textbf{\texttt{lib/tube.ts}}) is a durable, ordered,
\emph{at-least-once} pub/sub bus over a \texttt{messages} table, with a versioned
envelope (\texttt{\{ v:1, kind, body, inReplyTo \}}), a history cursor, and message
TTL. The L1 typed performative (\texttt{performative}, \texttt{conversationId},
\texttt{delegation-trace}, \texttt{reply-by}) is layered \emph{inside} this
envelope --- the kernel ships the carrier, Paper~1 ships what it carries.

\keyidea{At-least-once delivery means a \emph{healthy} bus routinely redelivers
and reorders. This collides with the volume's loud-fail honesty discipline
(ADR-0045): if ``every anomaly is loud,'' benign redelivery drowns the operator in
false alarms. The volume's resolution (stitch C8, specified in Paper~1 and assumed
here) is that benign transport non-determinism must be \emph{deduped silently};
only genuine protocol-FSM violations surface loudly. That requires an idempotency
key in the envelope --- currently absent --- and is the cheapest high-leverage L1
fix. The kernel provides the bus; making every performative effect idempotent is
L1's debt, flagged here so the reader does not mistake silent dedup for dishonesty.}

\subsection{Pheromones: decay as a provided guarantee}

\textbf{Pheromones} (\textbf{\texttt{lib/pheromone.ts}}) are stigmergic floats
(Grass\'e~\cite{grasse1959}; the Linda tuple-space lineage of
Gelernter~\cite{gelernter1985} is the sibling primitive in \texttt{lib/tuples.ts})
stored in entity metadata, with per-kind exponential decay $w \cdot r^{\Delta t}$
computed on a background evaporation tick \emph{and} at read time (so a reader sees
the correct decayed value without waiting for the tick), pruning below a threshold
of $0.01$. The decay is the structural defense against ``blackboard-rot'': stale
coordination signals evaporate on their own. The calibration of the decay rate is
genuinely open (OP-8): too slow and signals rot, too fast and they evaporate
before they coordinate.

\subsection{Two delegation chains, one dangerous name}

The volume's most dangerous naming collision (stitch C4) lives in this organ.
``Delegation chain'' names \emph{two different objects}:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{\texttt{auth-chain}} --- the \emph{cryptographic} object
  (\textbf{\texttt{lib/delegation-chain.ts}}, \Built, ProVerif-verified): an
  Ed25519 hop-bound, attenuation-monotonic, anti-replay/anti-splice chain that
  proves \emph{who authorized whom}. This is what Paper~4's cross-harbor capability
  transfer rides.
  \item \textbf{\texttt{delegation-trace}} --- the \emph{coordination} object
  (\Designed, Paper~1): the task-lineage over which loop-detection and
  upward-block run, preventing ping-pong.
\end{itemize}

These are distinct objects, and the relationship is that the
\texttt{delegation-trace} is carried \emph{inside} the \texttt{auth-chain} ---
loop-detection runs over a lineage whose authenticity the crypto chain guarantees.
We never again write bare ``delegation chain.''

\pitfall{The ``trace inside chain'' relationship is a slogan until the task-shape
field is in the \emph{signed} payload at each hop --- and that collides with the
project's no-keyword-NLP rule, because loop-detection needs a semantic
task-shape equivalence (embeddings or a structural hash), which is not
deterministically signable. This tension is a real open problem; we flag it (it is
formalized in Paper~1 and as OP-2's cousin) rather than assert it solved.}

\subsection{A second transport}

The kernel ships \emph{two} transports, not one: HTTP-over-Unix-socket
\emph{and} a binary IPC framing over a Unix domain socket
(\textbf{\texttt{lib/ipc-*.ts}}) with peer-credential authentication and
backpressure/draining --- the high-frequency path for tight-loop coordination. We
note in \S\ref{sec:threatmodel} that the peer-credential check is a software
handshake, not kernel-enforced \texttt{SO\_PEERCRED} (a real trust-boundary
caveat).

\exercises{%
\textbf{(check)} Why does read-time pheromone decay make the system more honest
than a background tick alone? Give a scenario where tick-only decay would report a
stale signal as fresh.\par
\textbf{(trace)} A \texttt{request} performative is sent over tube and redelivered
once by the at-least-once bus. Trace what the operator should see under the C8
resolution, and what an envelope idempotency key would change.\par
\textbf{(open, $\bigstar$ OP-8)} What per-kind pheromone decay rate keeps
stigmergic signals neither rotten nor prematurely evaporated? Frame this as an
empirical-systems measurement, not a constant to guess.}

\section{The obligation \& enforcement organ: the sovereign's two arms}
\label{sec:enforcement}
% ═════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

This organ is the deontic core, and it is genuinely two distinct mechanisms
(Jones \& Sergot~\cite{jonessergot1993}): \emph{prohibition} (the Arbiter) and
\emph{obligation} (commitments). Getting the distinction right --- and being
honest about how strong each arm actually is --- is the security heart of the
paper.

\input{figures/fig-swk-deontic-split}

\subsection{The deontic split}

\begin{definition}[The kernel's deontic theorem]\label{def:deontic}
The kernel realizes three deontic modalities with three mechanisms:
\emph{prohibition} is regimented (pre-commit) or enforced (post-commit, detected);
\emph{obligation} is enforced by oracle-bound commitments; \emph{permission} is a
recorded capability. L1's deontic binding (ADR-0047) is a direct lift of this
split.
\end{definition}

We keep capability (\emph{ability} --- what the jail makes possible) distinct from
permission (\emph{normative status} --- what the deontic layer allows), per stitch
C9, so that ``able but forbidden'' stays expressible. The canonical example is the
project's own \texttt{--allow-main-worktree} flag: it \emph{exists} (an agent is
\emph{capable} of invoking it) but \emph{must not be used} (it is \emph{forbidden}).
Collapsing capability into permission makes that state inexpressible, which is
exactly the bug a guardrail-bypass discipline cannot afford.

\subsection{The Arbiter is a detector, not a regimenter}
\label{sec:arbiter-correction}

\input{figures/fig-swk-reference-monitor}

Here is the correction the whole volume must adopt (stitch C1). The most-repeated
claim about the Arbiter --- that it ``makes forbidden coordination states
physically unreachable'' --- is false as stated, and falsely inherited by all four
papers. The code is decisive: \texttt{arbiter.ts:328} is
\texttt{activityLog.subscribe((entry) => \{\ldots\})}. The Arbiter is a
\emph{subscriber to an append-only event stream that has already committed}. By the
time it observes a \texttt{security.violation}, the offending write is durable in
the WAL. Even in strict mode, its strongest response is a \emph{compensating}
``man-overboard'' salvage --- an undo, not a prevention.

\begin{theorem}[Schneider bound on the Arbiter]\label{thm:schneider}
A monitor that observes the activity log strictly after commit enforces no safety
property; it can only detect and compensate. Therefore every Arbiter
\emph{subscription} rule (\texttt{HEARTBEAT\_FRESHNESS}, \texttt{NOTE\_MONOTONICITY},
\texttt{CAP\_ESCALATION}, \texttt{LOCK\_OWNER\_VALID}, \ldots) is, architecturally,
detect-and-compensate --- not regimentation.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Schneider's theory of enforceable security policies via execution
monitors~\cite{schneider2000} establishes that an execution monitor can enforce a
property only by \emph{truncating the execution before the bad state is entered}.
A monitor invoked strictly after the state transition has committed cannot
truncate before it; the bad state already exists. Hence it can detect the
violation and trigger a compensating transition, but it cannot have prevented the
state. Subscription to a post-commit log places every subscribed rule in this
regime. \qed
\end{proof}

\keyidea{The real, inline regimentation is credited to the right mechanism: the
\texttt{PRIMARY KEY} constraint (double-claim, PID-squat-as-PK) and the
fail-closed boot gate (prod-DB-in-test, \texttt{attest} refuse-to-serve). Those
reject \emph{before} the commit line and are \emph{truly} unreachable. The Arbiter
notices everything else \emph{afterward} and compensates. So: ``physically
unreachable'' for the regimented set; ``detected within a bounded window, then
halted/compensated'' for the rest. Crediting double-claim prevention to the
Arbiter \emph{double-counts} --- the PK already did it; the Arbiter just logs it.}

The Arbiter is honest about its own coverage, which is a genuine security
property: \texttt{ArbiterStatus} reports each rule as \texttt{enforced},
\texttt{degraded}, or \texttt{stubbed}, and one rule (\texttt{CAP\_ESCALATION}) is
backed by a Rust FFI core. We note in \S\ref{sec:threatmodel} that the FFI
enforcement and its temp-file materialization are the weakest-evidenced claim in
the organ and carry a time-of-check/time-of-use surface.

\subsection{Commitments: obligation with oracle-bound closure}

\input{figures/fig-swk-commitment-oracle}

Commitments (\textbf{\texttt{lib/commitments.ts}},
\textbf{\texttt{lib/obligation-monitor.ts}}) are durable, \emph{violable} promises
--- Cohen \& Levesque's ``intention as persistent goal''~\cite{cohenlevesque1990},
with single-minded and open-minded drop strategies. Two of five hardening laws are
shipped, and they are the load-bearing ones (Figure~\ref{fig:swk-commitment-oracle}).

\begin{property}[Goodhart-resistance, Law 1]\label{prop:law1}
A commitment's \texttt{due\_at} is derived by the daemon from a scope policy and is
\emph{never} agent-supplied. The agent picks the work; the daemon picks the
deadline. (Invariant I5.)
\end{property}

\begin{property}[Oracle-bound closure, Law 2]\label{prop:law2}
\texttt{close('done')} is refused unless the agent supplies a non-empty
\texttt{closed\_by\_oracle\_ref} drawn from a finite vocabulary: a released claim,
a merged SHA, a passing test id, or a satisfied Arbiter sub-check. Free-text notes
cannot close a commitment. (Invariant I6.)
\end{property}

\keyidea{That finite oracle vocabulary \emph{is} the kernel's honesty boundary. It
can mechanically verify ``the test you named passed,'' never ``the refactor is
good.'' Naming the oracle set is naming the limit of what L0 can verify --- and
open problem OP-5 asks which real ``done'' conditions it fails to capture, and
whether the set can be extended without re-opening the Goodhart hole that free-text
closure would.}

\exercises{%
\textbf{(check)} For each Arbiter rule in Theorem~\ref{thm:schneider}, say whether
it \emph{could in principle} be moved to a pre-commit hook (a \texttt{BEFORE
INSERT} trigger). Which genuinely cannot, and why?
(Hint: \texttt{HEARTBEAT\_FRESHNESS} and Chandra--Toueg~\cite{chandratoueg1996}.)\par
\textbf{(trace)} An agent calls \texttt{close('done', oracle\_ref="")}. Walk the
Law-2 gate and show exactly where and why the transition is refused.\par
\textbf{(open, $\bigstar$ OP-2)} Formalize the regimentation-vs-enforcement
boundary: which of the Arbiter's rules are truly regimentable (rejectable at
insert) versus only enforceable (detectable after)? A clean theorem here is the
deontic heart of the layer.\par
\textbf{(open, $\bigstar$ OP-5)} Extend the Law-2 oracle vocabulary to capture a
``done'' condition it currently misses, without re-admitting free text. State your
new oracle and prove it is Goodhart-resistant.}

\section{The continuity organ: memory, checkpoint, and the foundation of the economy}
\label{sec:continuity}
% ═════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

The through-line of the whole volume --- memory $\to$ checkpoint $\to$ continuity
$\to$ person $\to$ reputation $\to$ market --- \emph{bottoms out here}. If L0's
continuity is weak, L3 is built on sand. This is the section Paper~3 and Paper~4
lean on hardest, so it carries the volume's canonical statement of its own softest
link.

\input{figures/fig-swk-continuity-organs}

\subsection{Three organs, one weak link}

Figure~\ref{fig:swk-continuity-organs} names the three continuity organs and
states the canonical C7 sentence verbatim. We repeat it in prose because the
volume requires it identically in Papers~2 and 3:

\pullquote{The economy rests on three continuity organs --- memory (\textsf{BUILT}),
checkpoint (\textsf{BUILT-WEAK}: notes, not execution state), and the
witnessed-outcome ledger (\textsf{DESIGNED}) --- and reputation keys on the third,
which does not yet exist; the checkpoint organ is the weakest \emph{built} link,
and ``resurrection with teeth'' (a real execution-state checkpoint) is the literal
foundation of L3.}

\textbf{Memory} (\textbf{\texttt{lib/sessions.ts}}, the largest module, plus
\textbf{\texttt{lib/episodic-memory.ts}} and the activity log) is \Built, and the
kernel even forbids amnesia: the Arbiter's \texttt{NOTE\_MONOTONICITY} rule
guards that an active session's durable note count never regresses
(detect-and-compensate, per Theorem~\ref{thm:schneider}, but real).

\textbf{Checkpoint} (\textbf{\texttt{lib/resurrection.ts}}) is \BuiltWeak, and the
weakness is precise: resurrection runs heartbeat-watchdog $\to$ stale/dead
detection $\to$ resurrection queue $\to$ man-overboard salvage, and it \emph{passes
notes}; it does not checkpoint execution state. ``Resurrection with teeth'' ---
the gap between passing notes and restoring work --- is open problem OP-4, the most
important unbuilt thing at L0 because it is the literal foundation of the economy.

\textbf{The witnessed-outcome ledger}, the durable record of what an agent actually
delivered, is the organ reputation keys on. It is \Designed{} only; Paper~3 builds
it. OP-4 (here) and the outcome-ledger gap (Paper~3) are the \emph{same} finding
viewed from two ends.

\subsection{The activity log and tamper-evidence}

The append-only activity log (\textbf{\texttt{lib/activity.ts}}) is the kernel's
audit organ --- the stream the Arbiter subscribes to and the thing that makes
``what actually happened'' answerable. Hash-chained tamper-evidence over it
(\textbf{\texttt{lib/merkle-chain.ts}}, the Haber--Stornetta~\cite{haberstornetta1991}
pattern that predates blockchain) exists but is re-scoped --- see
\S\ref{sec:merkle}.

\textbf{(trace)} Follow \texttt{NOTE\_MONOTONICITY} from an agent appending a note

\textbf{(open, $\bigstar$ OP-4)} What is the minimal durable artifact that turns

\section{The self-attestation organ: honest green}
\label{sec:attest}
% ═════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

ADR-0045 (loud-fail invariants and honest attestation) lives at L0, because the
kernel is the one component that can honestly report its own integrity: it is
always-on and owns the only writable truth. \textbf{\texttt{pd attest}}
(\textbf{\texttt{lib/attest.ts}}, \textbf{\texttt{lib/attest-invariants.ts}})
evaluates an invariant registry where each invariant returns
\texttt{PASS}, \texttt{FAIL}, \texttt{SKIPPED(reason)}, or \texttt{UNKNOWN}. The
report is \emph{scoped and conjunctive}: ``all good'' is printed only when every
checked invariant passes, and the report always enumerates what was \emph{not}
checkable.

\begin{property}[Honest self-report, I10]\label{prop:attest}
\texttt{pd attest} reports ``all good'' only if every checked CRITICAL invariant
passed, and it always lists the unchecked set. There are three triggers: a boot
regimentation gate (refuse-to-serve on CRITICAL fail), a CLI pre-flight (loud
refusal naming the fix), and a continuous watchdog (a PASS$\to$FAIL transition
emits a pheromone and a notification). \Built.
\end{property}

This is the ADR-0045 honest-label discipline turned on the kernel itself, and it
is the right posture for a trusted computing base: when integrity is unknown, deny.
The drift/liveness guards (\textbf{\texttt{lib/binary-drift-detector.ts}},
\textbf{\texttt{lib/cli-liveness.ts}}, \textbf{\texttt{lib/git-origin-check.ts}})
exist precisely because a second copy of the daemon \emph{can} run --- the homebrew
install lagging the repo is a live, recurring hazard --- and the kernel must know
which copy of itself is the real one.

\subsection{The consistency-model theorem}

The dossier never said ``serializable'' or ``linearizable.'' It should have: those
words are the formal payoff of the single-writer choice, and they are what make
L1's typed ownership trustworthy.

\input{figures/fig-swk-consistency-model}

\begin{theorem}[Consistency model]\label{thm:consistency}
Under one writer over a single SQLite database in WAL mode, every transaction is
\textbf{serializable}. Because the daemon is the sole decider, the externally
observable history of claim/lock operations is \textbf{linearizable}: each
operation appears to take effect atomically at its commit (its linearization
point), consistent with real-time order.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
SQLite under a single writer provides serializable isolation: there is one stream
of write transactions, totally ordered by commit, and readers see a consistent
snapshot. For linearizability (Herlihy \& Wing~\cite{herlihywing1990}) we need a
single point per operation, between its invocation and response, at which it
appears to take effect, with these points consistent with real time. The commit of
each claim/lock operation is exactly such a point: it is atomic
(Theorem~\ref{thm:exclusion}), it lies between the client's request and the
daemon's response, and because the sole daemon connection commits operations in a
total order that respects the order in which it received them, the linearization
order coincides with real time. Hence the observable claim/lock history is
linearizable. \qed
\end{proof}

\keyidea{This is why the kernel needs no agreement protocol. Linearizability is the
strongest single-object consistency guarantee, and the single-writer architecture
gives it \emph{for free} --- not despite refusing consensus, but \emph{because} it
refused consensus. One decider, one order, one truth.}

\subsection{The dual-runtime hazard, and what would make I11 a theorem}

\input{figures/fig-swk-dual-runtime}

Invariant I11 (runtime parity) is the one place the formal spine is held together
by a shim rather than a proof. Truth is \emph{computed} under \texttt{bun:sqlite}
(the compiled single-binary daemon) but \emph{verified} under
\texttt{better-sqlite3} (jest); a pragma/option shim
(\textbf{\texttt{lib/sqlite-runtime.ts}}) bridges them
(Figure~\ref{fig:swk-dual-runtime}). An invariant green under test can be a 500 in
production if the bindings diverge in lock or pragma semantics --- the recurring
``green-in-jest, 500-in-bun'' failure. CI must \emph{boot the bun daemon} and smoke
its routes, not merely build it. Open problem OP-3 asks for the differential-test
harness that would turn I11 from a hope into evidence.

\exercises{%
\textbf{(check)} Theorem~\ref{thm:consistency} gives linearizability ``for free.''
What does ``free'' cost? (Hint: \texttt{busy\_timeout} and throughput under
contention --- the single writer is also a scalability ceiling.)\par
\textbf{(trace)} Construct a three-operation claim history and give its unique
linearization. Then show why no second linearization is consistent with real
time.\par
\textbf{(open, $\bigstar$ OP-3)} Specify the minimal differential-test suite that
would make I11 a theorem: what operation sequences, what observable-state
assertions, run against both runtimes?}

\section{Cross-organ atomicity: a buildable defect, not a frontier}
\label{sec:atomicity}
% ═════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

``Claim this port \emph{and} open this session \emph{and} record this commitment''
is three writes. Nothing at the kernel's API boundary wraps them in one SQLite
transaction, so partial-failure semantics across organs are undefined. The dossier
labeled this \Vision. That is wrong, and the correction matters because it changes
the engineering posture from ``research'' to ``do it.''

\keyidea{This is the Saga problem (Garcia-Molina \& Salem~\cite{garciamolina1987})
--- but with a \emph{cheap local fix}. These are all local writes to one file;
\texttt{db.transaction()} already exists in the codebase (it is used in
\texttt{bonds.ts} and \texttt{symbol-index.ts}). Wrapping a multi-organ operation
in one local transaction (or \texttt{BEGIN IMMEDIATE}) gives all-or-nothing for
free. Calling it \Vision{} understates how cheap the correct answer is. It is a
\textbf{buildable defect}, re-labeled here from \Vision{} to ``buildable now.''}

The reason it is not yet built is simply that no one wrapped the multi-organ API
endpoints; the primitive is present. This is the kind of finding the honest-label
discipline exists to surface: a gap mis-labeled as a frontier hides a one-line fix
behind the word ``VISION.''

\exercises{%
\textbf{(check)} Give a concrete partial-failure: port claimed, session opened,
then the commitment write fails. What inconsistent state is left, and which agent
sees it?\par
\textbf{(open, $\bigstar$ C5)} Sketch the API change that wraps the three-organ
``begin'' operation in one \texttt{db.transaction()}. What is the compensating
action if you instead chose Sagas, and why is the transaction strictly simpler
here?}

\section{Threat model and the limits of mediation}
\label{sec:threatmodel}
% ═════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

A kernel paper lives or dies on its threat model. The kernel deliberately shrinks
its trusted computing base (one writer, one file), which is good security hygiene
--- but the shrinking pushes several adversaries explicitly out of scope, and the
honest move is to draw the boundary, not gesture at it.

\subsection{Advisory claims are not sandboxing}

The sharpest category error to avoid: \texttt{session\_files} claims are
\emph{advisory coordination locks between cooperating agents}, not OS-enforced
filesystem isolation. There is no seccomp-bpf, no \texttt{pledge}/\texttt{unveil},
no macOS Seatbelt, no Landlock. A malicious agent ignores a claim row and writes
the file. The kernel provides \emph{advisory} coordination; real OS sandboxing of
agent processes is out of scope at L0 (it is the L1 ``jail,'' \Designed{} in
Paper~1, and would need to be grounded in a real OS primitive). We say so plainly
rather than let advisory claims be read as isolation.

\subsection{Self-asserted identity bounds every other invariant}

Today \texttt{owner\_actor\_id} is whatever the session claims (I12 is \Designed).
This silently bounds the security weight of I7, I8, and I9: every rule that keys on
identity (\texttt{LOCK\_OWNER\_VALID}, \texttt{CAP\_ESCALATION},
\texttt{NOTE\_MONOTONICITY}, owner-scoped release) is unforgeable only up to
impersonation. A malicious agent simply claims to be another actor. The Rust-FFI
\texttt{CAP\_ESCALATION} enforcement is theater if the capability set it checks
hangs off a forgeable identity. This dependency --- I12 gates the soundness of
I7/I8/I9 --- is a hard precondition, stated here so the reader does not over-trust
the enforcement organ.

\subsection{Merkle tamper-evidence, re-scoped to L3 provisioning}
\label{sec:merkle}

The Merkle chain (I9) is built, but under the \emph{stated} threat model it defends
against nobody: its only adversary is someone who can edit the audit log, which is
exactly the same-user process the model excludes (OP-9). Tamper-evidence that
nothing on the read path verifies is a data structure, not a control. We therefore
re-scope I9 as \textbf{L3 provisioning}: it matters only against cross-machine sync
or a non-same-user tamperer. Its load-bearing treatment moves to Paper~4; here it
is a one-line pointer: \emph{provisioned at L0, activated at L3}.

\exercises{%
\textbf{(check)} The Merkle chain defends against nobody in scope. Name the
\emph{out-of-scope} adversary it does defend against, and the layer (L0/L3) where
that adversary becomes real.\par
\textbf{(trace)} An agent forges \texttt{owner\_actor\_id} to release another
agent's lock. Walk \texttt{LOCK\_OWNER\_VALID} and show exactly where I12's absence
lets the forgery through.\par
\textbf{(open, $\bigstar$ OP-9)} What is the minimal hardening that closes the
same-machine adversary without a TPM dependency --- OS keychain for the signing
key, sealed memory? Where does each stop?}

% ═════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
\section{Adjacency contract: what the kernel assumes and provides}
\label{sec:adjacency}
% ═════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

The kernel's coherence with the layers around it is a contract: what it assumes
from the machine below, what it provides to the protocol above, and --- just as
important --- what it explicitly does \emph{not} provide so higher layers do not
over-assume.

\subsection{What L0 assumes from the machine}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item A POSIX filesystem with working advisory locking (\texttt{flock}/\texttt{fcntl}).
  \textbf{This is a correctness precondition for I2}: networked filesystems break
  advisory locks, and a broken lock lets two writers both win --- destroying mutual
  exclusion, not just performance.
  \item A local wall clock (\texttt{Date.now()}). Single-machine, so no shared-clock
  assumption is needed --- but note that \texttt{Date.now()} is \emph{not monotonic}
  (it steps on NTP adjustments), an intra-node hazard for every \texttt{expires\_at
  < now} sweep that the Lamport~\cite{lamport1978} inter-node citation does not
  cover.
  \item Unix domain sockets with peer-credential auth (the IPC and HTTP transports).
  \item An OS keychain for signing keys; \texttt{chmod} honored on the DB path.
  \item A supervisor (launchd) to keep the daemon always-on and to resurrect
  \emph{the daemon itself} --- L0 makes other things always-on but cannot make
  \emph{itself} always-on; that is delegated downward.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{What L0 provides to L1 (the kernel API contract)}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{Atomic, idempotent, TTL'd claims} (I2--I4) over ports, file-regions,
  symbols, and named locks --- L1's typed ownership reduces to L0 claim release.
  \item \textbf{A durable, versioned, history-cursored pub/sub bus} (tube) + tuple
  space + decaying pheromones --- L1's performative envelope is carried \emph{in}
  L0's tube envelope; the anti-blackboard-rot property is \emph{provided} by L0
  decay.
  \item \textbf{The deontic split as a primitive} (Def.~\ref{def:deontic}):
  prohibition $\to$ Arbiter (detect/regiment), obligation $\to$ commitment, permission
  $\to$ capability. L1 must not re-implement enforcement.
  \item \textbf{Daemon-owned deadlines and oracle-bound closure} (I5, I6).
  \item \textbf{An append-only audit log} (Merkle-provisioned) the protocol and the
  operator read.
  \item \textbf{Self-attestation} (I10) --- higher layers may \emph{assume the kernel
  is honest about its own health}.
  \item \textbf{The cryptographic \texttt{auth-chain}} --- L1's
  \texttt{delegation-trace} rides inside it.
  \item \textbf{Linearizable claim/lock semantics} (Theorem~\ref{thm:consistency})
  --- the property that lets L1 treat ``who holds this'' as ground truth.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The explicit non-provisions}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item L0 does \emph{not} provide non-forgeable identity yet (I12, \Designed).
  \item L0 does \emph{not} provide fair/queued exclusion (OP-1), cross-organ
  transactional atomicity by default (\S\ref{sec:atomicity}), real
  execution-checkpoint (OP-4), or any cross-machine guarantee (L3 --- different
  theorems, a shared-clock and Byzantine-membership problem L0 deliberately never
  touches).
  \item L0 does \emph{not} decide \emph{what to do} (no orchestration). It enforces
  what \emph{cannot} be done and records what \emph{did} happen --- the ``building
  department, not architect'' model.
\end{itemize}

\section{Open problems}
\label{sec:openproblems}
% ═════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

These nine open problems are the layer's research frontier; they appear as starred
exercises throughout and are collected here. OP-4 is co-owned with Paper~3 (it owns
the kernel mechanism; Paper~3 owns the personhood-and-reputation argument).

\begin{description}[leftmargin=2.2em,style=nextline]
\item[$\bigstar$ OP-1 --- Fair exclusion without a scheduler.] Add bounded-wait to
claims/locks while keeping the single-writer, no-background-scheduler simplicity.
Is a SQLite-backed FIFO wait-list with TTL'd reservations enough?
\item[$\bigstar$ OP-2 --- Regimentation vs.\ enforcement boundary.] Formalize which
Arbiter rules are truly regimentable (rejected at insert) versus only enforceable
(detected after). The deontic heart of the layer (Theorem~\ref{thm:schneider}).
\item[$\bigstar$ OP-3 --- Cross-runtime soundness as a proof obligation.] Prove,
not test, that the bun:sqlite and better-sqlite3 paths are observationally
equivalent for the invariant set (I11).
\item[$\bigstar$ OP-4 --- Checkpoint with teeth.] The minimal durable artifact that
turns ``resurrection passes notes'' into ``resurrection restores work.'' The
literal foundation of the L3 economy.
\item[$\bigstar$ OP-5 --- Oracle completeness.] Extend the finite Law-2 oracle set
without re-admitting free-text (without re-opening the Goodhart hole).
\item[$\bigstar$ OP-6 --- Tamper-evidence on the read path.] Where should Merkle
verification gate? A sampling/checkpoint regime with a provable detection-probability
bound (couples to \S\ref{sec:merkle}'s L3 re-scope).
\item[$\bigstar$ OP-7 --- Schema evolution without a sequencer.] Safe
renames/backfills/down-migrations preserving ``any module, any order.''
\item[$\bigstar$ OP-8 --- Pheromone decay calibration.] The right per-kind decay so
stigmergic signals neither rot nor evaporate before they coordinate.
\item[$\bigstar$ OP-9 --- Same-machine adversary.] Minimal hardening (OS keychain,
sealed memory) that closes the same-user process adversary without a TPM.
\end{description}

\section{Conclusion: solid where local, provisional where it must grow}
% ═════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

The kernel is a single-writer transactional reference monitor over a local
SQLite/WAL file, and its two jobs --- durable record and mediated exclusion --- are
real and, mostly, proven. It earns its strongest claims (mutual exclusion,
serializable/linearizable consistency, oracle-bound closure) precisely by refusing
the distributed-systems problem it superficially resembles: one writer, one file,
one decider, no consensus.

Where it stops, it stops honestly. Durability is split: process-crash durable
(I1a), \emph{not} guaranteed against power loss (I1b). The Arbiter detects and
compensates; it does not regiment, and we credit the real inline regimentation to
the PK constraint and boot gate. Cross-organ atomicity is a buildable defect, not a
frontier. Merkle tamper-evidence is re-scoped to L3 provisioning, where it actually
defends someone. And the two genuinely soft spots --- non-forgeable identity (I12)
and checkpoint-with-teeth (OP-4) --- are exactly the two things the economy
(Paper~4) and the person (Paper~3) lean on hardest.

\pullquote{The kernel is solid where it is local and provisional exactly where the
economy will need it to be cryptographic and continuous. That is not a flaw to
hide; it is the layer boundary, stated truthfully.}

Every ``single-machine / one-writer / one-clock / self-asserted-identity''
simplification that makes this layer clean is precisely the assumption a later
layer must pay to relax. The Anchor Protocol \emph{proper} --- cross-harbor
capability transfer over the \texttt{auth-chain} this kernel ships --- is where
that payment begins, and it is Paper~4, not here. Build the harbor first. The
cryptography earns its keep when the ships sail out to trade.
