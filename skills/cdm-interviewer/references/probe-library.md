# Probe Library

Probes organized by purpose. Use this as a menu, not a checklist. The art of CDM is choosing the right probe for the moment, not running them all in sequence.

Each family includes (a) the canonical question, (b) variants for when the canonical fails, (c) what the probe is *for* (so you know whether it succeeded).

## Family 1 — Cues

**For**: surfacing the perceptual triggers experts notice but novices miss. The single highest-value L3 content for skill authoring.

Canonical: *"What were you noticing right then? What was telling you something was off?"*

Variants:
- "If a video camera had been on the room, what would it have shown that mattered?"
- "What were you looking at? Listening for? Smelling? Feeling?"
- "Walk me through what was on your screen / your dashboard / in your visual field."
- "Was there anything that was *missing* that should have been there?"
- "Was there anything that was *there* that shouldn't have been?"
- "Imagine I was standing next to you. What would I have seen but not understood?"

Success signal: a specific, observable cue ("the secondary pressure was flat when it should have been climbing") rather than a generalization ("things looked wrong").

## Family 2 — Knowledge

**For**: surfacing the L1/L2 anchors that make a cue meaningful. Why does *that* cue mean *that*?

Canonical: *"What did you know that made you read it that way?"*

Variants:
- "What would someone without that knowledge have seen?"
- "When did you learn that this pattern means what it means?"
- "If a smart person from a different specialty walked in, what would you have to explain to them first?"
- "What's the underlying mechanism — why does X cause Y here?"

Success signal: an actual causal explanation, not just "experience." If the answer is "experience," push: "experience teaching you what specifically?"

## Family 3 — Analogues

**For**: surfacing pattern matching. Experts often act on case-based reasoning rather than first-principles deduction.

Canonical: *"Did this remind you of any other situation? Have you seen this pattern before?"*

Variants:
- "What's the closest case you'd seen before?"
- "How was this similar to / different from that earlier case?"
- "Is this a pattern you have a name for, even informally?"
- "If you had a war story you tell new hires, is this it — or is the war story the one this reminded you of?"

Success signal: the expert names a prior incident or pattern. Bonus signal: they describe a *family* of cases.

## Family 4 — Options

**For**: surfacing discrimination criteria. Why this action and not the alternatives?

Canonical: *"What other options did you consider? Why not those?"*

Variants:
- "What would have been the textbook answer here? Why didn't you do that?"
- "What was the second-best option, and what would have triggered you to pick it?"
- "Was there an option that's obviously wrong only in retrospect?"
- "What was the safest option? Why didn't you take it?"
- "What was the riskiest option? Why didn't you take it?"

Success signal: at least one rejected alternative with the reason for rejection. Two is better.

## Family 5 — Experience

**For**: tying the current decision to past learning, especially failure-driven learning.

Canonical: *"How did your past experience guide you here?"*

Variants:
- "Has anyone you trained ever gotten this wrong? What did you tell them?"
- "Was there a time earlier in your career when you would have made a different call here? What changed?"
- "What's a mistake you've made that this decision was avoiding?"

Success signal: a specific prior incident referenced, especially a personal failure. Failure-anchored heuristics are the densest L3.

## Family 6 — Time pressure

**For**: understanding decision economics — what changes when time is short.

Canonical: *"How much time did you have to decide?"*

Variants:
- "What if you'd had ten times more time?"
- "What if you'd had half the time?"
- "Was there a point where waiting was the wrong move? How would you know?"
- "Were you racing the clock against something specific?"

Success signal: you understand both the time budget and what the expert traded away to fit it.

## Family 7 — Confidence

**For**: probing the uncertainty handling that distinguishes experts from rule-followers.

Canonical: *"How confident were you? What would have changed your mind?"*

Variants:
- "On a scale of 1–10, how sure were you?"
- "What was the doubt that almost made you do something else?"
- "What would have been a sign that you were wrong?"
- "Were you ever close to changing your mind? When?"

Success signal: a *specific disconfirming signal* the expert was monitoring. "I was watching for X — if I'd seen X, I'd have switched." This is rich L3.

## Family 8 — Novice errors (Sweep 4 power tool)

**For**: surfacing the discrimination that makes someone an expert. The most generative L3 question in the entire protocol.

Canonical: *"Where would a novice have gone wrong here?"*

Variants:
- "If you'd had less experience, what would have tripped you up?"
- "What's the obvious-looking mistake here?"
- "What's the trap that catches people who only know the textbook?"
- "If I gave this incident as a case study to a junior, where would they go off-track?"

Success signal: the expert describes a specific error mode and the *cue* that should prevent it. That cue is the heuristic trigger.

## Family 9 — Counterfactuals

**For**: testing the boundaries of the heuristic. When does it not apply?

Canonical: *"What if [key cue] had been different?"*

Variants:
- "Imagine the situation but with X changed. Same call?"
- "What's a situation where this would have looked the same but you'd have done the opposite?"
- "What would have to be true for you to do nothing here?"

Success signal: the expert names the *boundary conditions* of their heuristic — the "unless" clause.

## Family 10 — Wished-for information

**For**: identifying the information environment gaps that constrain even expert performance.

Canonical: *"Was there information you wished you had at that moment?"*

Variants:
- "If you could have had one tool / dashboard / piece of data, what would it have been?"
- "What did you have to infer that you wish was just shown to you?"
- "What did you want to ask, and who would you have asked?"

Success signal: a specific information item, often pointing at observability or tooling gaps that affect more than this incident.

## Family 11 — Closing probes

**For**: catching the last 10% of yield. Always run at least one of these before ending the session.

Canonical: *"Is there anything about this incident you've never told anyone, or that you assume everyone knows but might not?"*

Variants:
- "What would your senior peers find surprising about how you handled this?"
- "What would you have written in the postmortem if there were no political consequences?"
- "If this comes up again next year, what's the one thing you want future-you to remember?"

Success signal: surprise — yours or theirs. If nothing surprises you in the closing probes, either you got everything (rare) or you missed something (more likely — flag for follow-up).

## Probe selection cheat sheet

| Situation | Try this family first |
|---|---|
| Expert just paused dramatically in the timeline | Cues |
| Expert says "I just knew" | Knowledge → "novice" probe |
| Expert says "I always do this" | Options ("what's the alternative you're rejecting?") |
| Expert describes a workaround | Experience ("how did you learn this was needed?") |
| Expert is confidently wrong about something | Confidence ("what would have changed your mind?") |
| Sweep 3 stalling | Novice errors (jump to Sweep 4 probe early) |
| Want to extract a heuristic | Cues + Counterfactuals (cue + boundary) |

## Anti-probes (don't use)

- "Why did you do that?" — invites post-hoc rationalization. Use cue-based probes instead.
- "What's your decision-making framework for X?" — invites textbook recitation.
- "Was that the right call?" — invites defensiveness. CDM is not a postmortem.
- "Have you ever read about [theory] in [book]?" — invites citation, not recall.
- Leading questions ("So you must have been thinking about Y, right?") — contaminates the protocol.
