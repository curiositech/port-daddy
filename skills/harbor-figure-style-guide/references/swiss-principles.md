# Swiss Modernist Principles for Systems Diagrams

This reference documents the art direction and visual philosophy governing diagrams in *The Harbor, the Person, and the Economy*. Rooted in the International Typographic Style (Zürich and Basel schools, 1950s–1970s), this approach rejects decorative illustration in favor of **geometry that makes its own structural logic visible**.

---

## The Ten Canon Laws

### 1. Content Determines Form (Ernst Keller, 1918–1956)
There is no generic template. Ernst Keller's teaching at the Kunstgewerbeschule Zürich emphasized that the internal logic of the subject must dictate the physical layout. If you remove the diagram's text, the remaining geometric architecture should still uniquely reflect the specific systems problem being solved.

### 2. The Modular Field Grid (Josef Müller-Brockmann)
Every diagram is anchored to an explicit modular grid of horizontal and vertical coordinates. Elements do not float arbitrarily; bounding boxes, lifelines, axes, and labels must begin and end on grid module lines. Gutters between modules must be mathematically consistent.

### 3. Asymmetric Balance
Symmetrical arrangements create static, decorative compositions that conceal underlying structural relationships. Asymmetry exposes the coordinate module to the reader, drawing immediate visual attention to the causal flow or structural asymmetry in the protocol.

### 4. Mathematical Composition & Stateable Series
The proportions, spacing, and progression of geometric marks must be generated by a stateable numerical rule, not placed "by eye." Whether stepping time along an explicit arithmetic module ($\Delta t = 1.2\,\text{cm}$) or scaling weights in a 1 : 1.8 : 3.2 ratio, the viewer should be able to deduce the underlying rule from the visual spacing.

### 5. Color as a Structured Category System (Richard Paul Lohse)
Color is not mood or decoration; it is a discrete category coordinate. Hues are assigned strictly according to conceptual identity (`pd truth`, `pd breach`, `pd value`), never to make a diagram "look lively." A diagram uses 2 to 4 hues at most.

### 6. The Single Grotesk & Typographic Restraint (Emil Ruder, Massimo Vignelli)
Use a single sans-serif grotesk typeface (TeX Gyre Heros / Source Sans 3). The entire diagram must adhere to the Typographic Law: one base size (`\footnotesize`, ~8.7 pt) with variations strictly limited to weight (bold for titles) and posture (italic for singular notes). Text is set flush-left, ragged-right, never justified, never centered inside wide rectangles.

### 7. Form and Counter-Form (Emil Ruder)
The white space surrounding ink is not empty void; it is an active design element ("counter-form"). Text blocks function visually as textured grey planes. Balance the visual density of text blocks against the negative space of the canvas.

### 8. Graphic Reduction to Tonal Signs (Armin Hofmann)
Complex physical entities or abstract processes are reduced to clean, high-contrast graphic signs. Avoid figurative depictions, cartoon laptops, server racks, or anthropomorphic agent icons. Represent systems entities as clean geometric enclosures with precise boundary edges.

### 9. Orthogonal & 45° Angular Discipline (Massimo Vignelli, Otl Aicher)
All connection paths, state transitions, and lifeline communications bend exclusively at 90° (Manhattan routing) or 45°. Freehand curves, jaunty angles, and arbitrary diagonal vectors are banned.

### 10. Negative Space Under Tension
Negative space must feel intentional and pressurized, holding the graphical marks in equilibrium. If an extraneous element can be inserted into the diagram without disrupting the composition, the layout is underconstrained and must be tightened.
