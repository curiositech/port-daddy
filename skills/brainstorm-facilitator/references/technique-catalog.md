# Technique Catalog

A menu of divergence techniques. Pick by situation, not by familiarity. Switch when one runs dry — a second technique on the same problem typically yields 30–50% more ideas because it forces a new angle.

## Selection cheat sheet

| Situation | First-choice technique |
|---|---|
| Generic ideation, no specific frame | How Might We → Crazy Eights |
| Stuck on the obvious answer | Reverse Brainstorming or Worst Possible Idea |
| Need quantity fast, group of 4+ | 6-3-5 Brainwriting |
| Mental block on what's possible | Random Stimulus |
| Need to explore a system or feature space exhaustively | SCAMPER or Lotus Blossom |
| Group has dominant voices | Brainwriting (any silent-first variant) |
| Solo session | Crazy Eights + Random Stimulus + Worst Possible Idea rotation |
| Need to explore tensions between perspectives | Six Thinking Hats |
| Naming, metaphor, creative framing | Random Stimulus + analogical (cross-domain) |
| Strategic options under uncertainty | Pre-mortem-style "imagine it failed, why?" |

## Techniques

### 1. How Might We (HMW)

**For**: framing the question itself. Should precede most other techniques.

**How**: take the problem and rewrite it as "How might we [verb] [outcome] for [user/context]?" Then generate variants of the HMW at multiple altitudes (broad/narrow), and pick 1–3 to brainstorm against.

**Example**: 
- Problem: "users abandon onboarding"
- HMW (broad): "How might we make new users feel successful in their first session?"
- HMW (narrow): "How might we cut steps from current onboarding from 7 to 3?"
- HMW (lateral): "How might users teach each other to onboard?"

The technique is most powerful as a *framing* exercise: spending 10 minutes generating 10–20 HMW variants often reveals the original framing was suboptimal. Pick the HMW that opens the most option space without being so broad it's vacuous.

### 2. Crazy Eights

**For**: fast individual ideation in a small group. Excellent warm-up.

**How**: each person folds a paper into 8 panels (or uses 8 sticky notes / 8 doc cells). Each person sketches or writes 8 distinct ideas in 8 minutes — one per minute. Then share.

**Why it works**: enforced quantity (8 in 8 min) bypasses the "this isn't good enough" filter. The first 3–4 ideas are usually obvious; ideas 5–8 are where the interesting ones live.

**Variants**: Crazy 4s (faster, lower-stakes). Crazy 16s (longer session, deeper dive). Pair sharing after individual round to halve sharing time.

### 3. 6-3-5 Brainwriting

**For**: groups of 6 (adjustable to 4–8), high quantity, low production-blocking.

**How**: 6 people, 3 ideas each, 5 minutes per round. Round 1: everyone writes 3 ideas on a sheet. Pass right. Round 2: read what's there, write 3 new ideas (often building on what you read). Repeat 6 times. Output: 108 ideas in 30 minutes.

**Why it works**: silent generation eliminates production blocking. "Building on what you see" creates idea cross-pollination without verbal interruption. Equal airtime (everyone contributes the same volume).

**Adaptations**: adjust to team size; use a shared doc with sections instead of paper; allow drawings.

### 4. SCAMPER

**For**: exploring variations on an existing thing (feature, product, process). Systematic, exhaustive.

**Mnemonic**: 
- **S**ubstitute — what can be replaced?
- **C**ombine — what can be merged with something else?
- **A**dapt — what can be borrowed from another domain?
- **M**odify (or Magnify/Minify) — what can be made bigger/smaller/different?
- **P**ut to other use — who else could use this differently?
- **E**liminate — what can be removed entirely?
- **R**everse (or Rearrange) — what can be flipped or reordered?

**How**: take the target thing, walk each letter, generate 3–5 ideas per letter. 21–35 ideas in 20 minutes.

**Best for**: product feature exploration, process redesign, business model variants. Less useful for blue-sky / brand-new framing (use HMW first).

### 5. Reverse Brainstorming

**For**: breaking out of "how do we make X better" when stuck.

**How**: invert the question. Instead of "how do we increase signups," ask "how do we GUARANTEE no one signs up?" Generate ideas. Then invert each idea back to its positive form.

**Why it works**: the inverted question is often easier to answer because it activates failure-mode knowledge (which is more salient than success-mode). Inversion produces ideas that the original framing wouldn't have surfaced.

**Variant — Worst Possible Idea**: just generate the worst possible solutions, on purpose, with energy. The exercise is liberating (no pressure to be smart), and many "worst" ideas have a kernel that, slightly tweaked, becomes brilliant.

### 6. Random Stimulus

**For**: breaking fixation. When the well runs dry on a topic.

**How**: pick a random word, image, or object unrelated to the problem. Force a connection between it and the question. Generate ideas from the forced association.

**Sources**: random Wikipedia article, dictionary page, photo from camera roll, object visible in the room, random card from Oblique Strategies, random emoji.

**Example**: brainstorming pricing models. Random stimulus: "lighthouse." Forced associations: visibility (transparent pricing); guidance (pricing helps customers choose tier); danger warning (price as commitment signal); periodic (subscription with check-ins); operated by humans (human-curated tiers).

**Why it works**: forces new neural pathways. The forced unrelated input bypasses the constraints your existing frame is imposing.

### 7. Lotus Blossom

**For**: exhaustive exploration of a topic with subtopics. Visual structure.

**How**: start with the topic in the center of a 3×3 grid. The 8 surrounding cells get 8 sub-topics. Each sub-topic then becomes the center of its own 3×3 grid. Result: 64 ideas organized hierarchically.

**Best for**: feature spaces with natural sub-categories; product taxonomies; design system option spaces. Less useful for free-form ideation.

### 8. Six Thinking Hats (de Bono)

**For**: exploring the same idea through forced perspective shifts. Especially good for groups where everyone defaults to the same role (all critics, all optimists).

**Hats**:
- **White**: facts and information only
- **Red**: emotions and intuition (no justification needed)
- **Black**: caution, criticism, what could go wrong
- **Yellow**: optimism, benefits, what could go right
- **Green**: creativity, alternatives, "what else"
- **Blue**: process, meta — am I running this well?

**How**: pick a hat, everyone wears it for 3–5 minutes on the same topic. Then switch. Cycle through all six.

**Best for**: convergence-adjacent exploration. Pure divergence sessions usually don't need this — it's better when you have a candidate and want to stress-test from multiple angles before committing.

### 9. "Yes, And" Improv

**For**: building on ideas. Less a standalone technique than a mode discipline that powers other techniques.

**How**: any idea offered must be responded to with "yes, and..." which adds to it rather than evaluating it. The conversation builds vertically (adding) rather than rejecting.

**Best for**: small group verbal sessions, especially as a warmup to break critical-mode habits. Run for 10 minutes before any other technique.

### 10. Pre-mortem ideation

**For**: surfacing risk-aware options. Especially good for strategic/high-stakes decisions.

**How**: imagine it's 12 months from now and the project failed catastrophically. Each person writes the post-mortem: what went wrong? Then translate failure modes back into ideas: "what option would have prevented X?"

**Best for**: long-horizon decisions, multi-stakeholder projects, anything where "what could go wrong" is a real input. The imagination of failure unlocks options the optimistic framing would miss.

## Switching mid-session

Signs to switch technique:
- 3+ minutes with no new ideas
- New ideas are obvious variants of existing ones
- The room is producing only one "type" of idea (e.g., all UI tweaks, no business model)
- Energy has dropped

Switching pattern: pair complementary techniques. SCAMPER (systematic) + Random Stimulus (chaotic). 6-3-5 (silent) + Yes-And (verbal). HMW (framing) + Reverse (inversion). The contrast between paired techniques is the engine.
