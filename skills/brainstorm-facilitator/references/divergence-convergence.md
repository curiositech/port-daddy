# Divergence and Convergence: The Core Discipline

The single most important rule in brainstorming: **never mix divergence and convergence in the same phase.** This file explains the principle, the failure modes when violated, and the moves to enforce it.

## Why separation matters

The two modes use different cognition and reward different behaviors:

| Aspect | Divergence | Convergence |
|---|---|---|
| Goal | Maximize option count and variance | Select best option(s) |
| Stance | Generative, accepting | Evaluative, critical |
| Quality bar | Quantity, including wild | Realistic and feasible |
| Time pressure | Helpful (forces flow) | Harmful (forces premature commit) |
| Group dynamic | Building, "yes and" | Challenging, debating |
| Speed | Fast (1–2 min per idea max) | Slow (deliberation rewards quality) |
| Best mood | Playful, slightly silly | Sober, focused |

When you mix them:
- Divergence collapses (people self-censor anything that won't "win")
- Convergence collapses (no real options to choose from)
- Anchoring locks in the first surviving idea
- Wild ideas (the substrate for novel solutions) never appear

The illusion that "we can do both at once" is the single most common reason brainstorming sessions produce mediocre output.

## The failure mode in slow motion

A typical session that mixes the modes:

1. Facilitator: "Let's brainstorm features for X."
2. Person A: "We could do feature P."
3. Person B: "Yeah but legal won't allow that."
4. Person A retreats. Stops offering ideas.
5. Person C: "What about Q? It's safer."
6. Person B: "Q is fine, that's what we should do."
7. Total ideas: 2. Time elapsed: 4 minutes. Decision made: Q.

The room never learned that R, S, T, U, and V exist. Q won by being the first non-rejected idea, not by being the best idea. This is the default of unfacilitated brainstorming.

## Enforcing separation: the moves

### Pre-session

Set the rule explicitly before you start:

> "We're going to do this in two phases. Phase one is divergence — we generate as many ideas as we can without judgment. No 'yes but,' no 'we tried that,' no 'won't work because.' Phase two is convergence — that's where we evaluate, and you can object as much as you want. But not until we get to phase two. Sound okay?"

Naming the rule and getting verbal assent gives you permission to interrupt later. Without this, interruption feels rude.

### During divergence — interventions

When someone violates the rule, intervene immediately and consistently:

| Violation | Intervention |
|---|---|
| "Yes but..." | "Save it for phase two. For now: yes, and what else?" |
| "We tried that already." | "Note it. Add three new ones." |
| "Legal won't allow that." | "Add it anyway, we'll address constraints later." |
| Someone visibly skeptical | Don't call them out. Redirect: "What's an idea you like, even if it's wild?" |
| Side conversation about feasibility | "Pause that. We're still in divergence. Hold the thought." |
| Person stops contributing | Switch technique or mode (often signals fatigue or fixation, not absence of ideas) |

The intervention should be matter-of-fact, not punitive. The rule applies to everyone including the most senior person in the room. If you let the senior person break it once, the rule is dead.

### The "yes, and" reflex

Train it explicitly. When someone offers an idea, the next two responses (from anyone, including the facilitator) should be:
1. An additive build ("yes, and we could also...")
2. A wilder version ("what's the extreme version of that?")

This actively counteracts the evaluative reflex and produces idea cascades.

### Knowing when divergence is "done"

Three signals:
1. **Diminishing returns**: 3+ minutes go by without a new idea, even after technique switching.
2. **Repetition**: new ideas are obvious variants of existing ones.
3. **Energy drop**: room is visibly tired and the ideas are getting half-hearted.

Two of three signals = done. One signal = try one more technique first.

Common mistake: stopping at 8–10 ideas because "that feels like enough." It isn't. Real divergence usually produces 30–100 in a 30-minute session. If you stop at 10, you're 3–10x undershooting.

## Convergence as its own protocol

Convergence is not "now we just pick the best." It has structure.

### Sequence

1. **Cluster**: Group near-duplicates. Done collaboratively, not by the facilitator alone — surfaces hidden disagreement about what's actually similar.
2. **Set criteria**: Agree explicit evaluation criteria *before* looking at the ideas. Defaults: NUF (Novel/Useful/Feasible), Impact-vs-Effort, or domain-specific. Without criteria, convergence selects by social dynamics.
3. **Independent scoring**: Everyone scores or votes *silently* against criteria before any discussion. Prevents the first speaker from anchoring everyone else.
4. **Reveal and discuss**: Show the votes. Discuss the surprises (high-variance options) and high-scorers. Let advocates argue for preserving wild cards.
5. **Shortlist**: 3–5 options for further work. Plus 1–2 rejected-but-interesting (preserved with rationale).
6. **Handoff**: Owner, action, deadline.

### Voting mechanisms

| Method | When | Mechanics |
|---|---|---|
| Dot voting | Quick read on group preference, 20+ ideas | Each person gets N dots (often √(ideas)/2), places on favorites |
| Multi-criteria scoring | When trade-offs matter | Score each option 1–5 on each criterion; sum or weighted sum |
| Forced ranking | Small option set, must pick one | Each person ranks top 3, points-based aggregation |
| Buy-a-feature | Resource-constrained shortlisting | Each person has $100, allocates across options |

Default to dot voting for speed. Use multi-criteria when the trade-offs are real and the team disagrees about which criterion matters most (the disagreement itself is information).

### The advocacy round

After voting, before finalizing: ask if anyone wants to advocate for an option that didn't make it. Not as a re-vote — as a chance to surface dissent and capture rationale. If the advocate persuades the room, re-vote. If not, the option goes on the rejected-but-interesting list with the advocate's rationale.

This prevents the post-meeting regret of "we should have considered that more."

### The rejected-but-interesting list

The single highest-leverage convergence artifact, almost always omitted. Each entry:

> **[Option name]**: rejected because [reason]. Would become viable if [condition]. Revisit when [trigger]. Champion: [name].

Reasons to preserve:
- Constraints change. The "we can't because budget" idea may be the right call next quarter.
- The reason for rejection gets lost. Without the list, in 6 months someone proposes the same idea and gets a vague "we tried that" instead of usable context.
- It validates dissent. People who lost the vote feel heard; their judgment is preserved as future option-space.

## Re-opening divergence

Sometimes during convergence you realize the option set is wrong — too narrow, missing a class of solutions. When this happens:

1. Acknowledge it openly: "I think we're missing a category. Let's go back to divergence for 5 minutes."
2. Set a tight time box.
3. Run a *different* technique than the one you used originally (forces a new angle).
4. Return to convergence with the expanded set.

Resist the urge to "just discuss it for a minute" — that's mode-mixing. Either you're in divergence or you're in convergence. Naming the switch protects both modes.

## Solo discipline

When working alone, the same separation applies, but the failure mode is different. Solo brainstormers tend to:
- Edit ideas in their head before writing them down (silent self-censorship)
- Stop too early because "I'm running out of ideas" (you aren't; you're tired or fixated)
- Pick the first idea that "feels right" (anchoring on yourself)

Counters:
- Write *everything* down, including bad ideas. The mediocre ones are seeds for better ones.
- When stuck, switch technique (random stimulus, reverse, worst-possible) — this is the solo equivalent of group facilitation.
- Force a minimum count before evaluating ("I will not start judging until I have 30 options").
- Sleep on it before convergence whenever possible. Overnight breaks dramatically improve the quality of evaluation.
