# Facilitation Moves

Group-dynamics interventions for keeping divergence productive and convergence honest. The L3 of facilitation lives in *cues* (what tells you to intervene) and *moves* (what to do).

## Reading the room: cues and what they mean

| Cue | Likely meaning | Move |
|---|---|---|
| One person has spoken 3x in a row | Production blocking forming | Switch to silent generation for 5 min |
| Quiet person hasn't spoken | Evaluation apprehension; status hierarchy | Direct invitation: "X, what's a wild one you've thought of?" |
| All ideas are variants of one theme | Fixation | Switch technique; force lateral move |
| Long silence after question | Question is wrong, or anchoring is locking room | Reframe; or do silent-write 2 min then share |
| Laughter | Genuinely playful (good) OR nervous (bad) | If genuine: great, keep going. If nervous: name the awkwardness |
| Body language closed (arms crossed, looking at phone) | Disengagement; perceived political theater | Pause and ask: "Is this useful right now? What would make it more useful?" |
| Senior person waits, others defer | HiPPO forming | Senior person writes silently first; share last |
| Someone says "we tried that" | Premature closure attempt | "Note it. What was different then? What's different now?" Add the original to the list anyway |
| Energy crashing 25 min in | Cognitive fatigue | 5-minute break; switch to physical movement |
| One person dominating verbally | Production blocking + airtime imbalance | Token-passing rule; or move to silent format |

## Drawing out quiet voices

Quiet voices often have the most diverse ideas (less anchored to group consensus). The cost of leaving them unspoken is high. Moves in increasing intensity:

1. **Direct invitation, low-stakes**: "X, anything to add?"
2. **Direct invitation, specific**: "X, you were taking notes — anything jumped out?"
3. **Format change**: switch to silent-write; quiet voices contribute equally in writing.
4. **Round-robin**: go around the room, each person says one idea.
5. **One-on-one prompt during break**: "Hey X, I noticed you didn't share — was there something on your mind you didn't get a chance to say?"

Don't:
- Shame ("we haven't heard from X yet!" — embarrassing)
- Wait too long (silence becomes structural)
- Assume silence = no ideas (almost always wrong)

## Handling dominant voices

A dominant voice creates two problems: (1) suppresses others, (2) anchors the room on their first ideas. Moves in increasing intensity:

1. **Token mechanism**: "Let's pass a token; only the holder speaks."
2. **Format switch**: silent generation rounds shift the participation equilibrium.
3. **Reflective ask**: "Y, you've offered a lot — what do you think we're missing?" (engages them as a meta-thinker rather than continuing volume).
4. **Direct private**: at break, "Y, can you hold space for the others by writing instead of speaking for the next round?"
5. **Last resort**: explicit norm-setting: "We're going to do a round where everyone except [Y] speaks first."

If the dominant voice is the most senior person, the moves still apply but require more diplomatic framing. Don't capitulate — silent formats remove the issue without confrontation.

## Handling the HiPPO (Highest Paid Person's Opinion)

The HiPPO failure mode: senior person says something early, room aligns, divergence collapses. Moves:

1. **Senior person commits silently first**: ask the senior person to write their initial ideas privately *before* discussion. Reduces the anchoring effect of their verbal lead.
2. **Senior person speaks last**: explicit rule. "Let's hear from least senior to most senior in the share-back."
3. **Devil's advocate role**: assign someone explicitly to challenge the senior person's ideas. Makes pushback safe.
4. **Cover for dissent**: facilitator privately notes ideas that contradict the senior person's view and surfaces them: "I want to make sure we considered the other framing — Z mentioned earlier..."

If the senior person is *also* the facilitator, brainstorming is structurally compromised. Either get an external facilitator or have the senior person agree explicitly: "I'm setting aside my preference until convergence."

## Surfacing dissent during convergence

Convergence selects the loudest, safest, or highest-status option by default. Moves to counteract:

1. **Independent voting before discussion**: silent dot voting, then reveal. Anchoring-free.
2. **Steel-manning round**: before voting, ask one person to make the strongest case for an underdog option. Anyone can be assigned this; rotates.
3. **Devil's advocate for the leading option**: "Before we commit, what's the strongest argument against option A?"
4. **Five-second silence after each option discussion**: forces space for second thoughts to surface.
5. **Anonymous dissent capture**: small slips of paper, each person writes any objection they didn't voice. Read aloud anonymously.

The goal is not to override the group's preference — it's to make sure the preference is informed and not just polite.

## Energy management

A 90-minute brainstorm has natural arcs. Plan for:

- **First 10 min**: warm-up energy, often shallow
- **10–30 min**: peak generative
- **30–45 min**: first dip — switch technique here
- **45–60 min**: second peak after technique switch
- **60–75 min**: convergence
- **75–90 min**: shortlist, handoff, close

Take a real break at 45 min. Coffee, movement, off-topic chat. Returning groups consistently outperform groups that push through.

## Remote / distributed sessions

Remote sessions amplify some failure modes (production blocking, dominant voices) and reduce others (status display). Adjustments:

- Default to silent / written generation more than in-person
- Use shared docs (Miro, Figma, Google Docs) for visibility
- Camera on for facilitator at minimum; cameras on for participants if culturally accepted
- Shorter sessions (60 min instead of 90); attention degrades faster online
- Break every 30 min, not every 45 min
- Use chat for parallel quiet contribution alongside verbal main thread
- Anonymous voting tools (Mentimeter, Polly) help with dissent surfacing

The facilitator's job remote is harder — body language cues are weaker. Lean on explicit format protocols rather than reading the room.

## Mid-session recovery moves

When the session is going wrong, three reset moves help:

1. **Name it**: "I'm noticing we're all converging on one type of idea — let's deliberately push for the opposite for 5 minutes." Public naming of the failure mode loosens it.
2. **Switch mode**: verbal → silent, group → pairs, technique A → technique B. The change itself unsticks.
3. **Take a real break**: 10 minutes physically away. Cognitive reset. Often the best ideas come from the "in between" mental state.

## Closing the session well

A good close has three elements:
1. **Recap the shortlist + rejected-but-interesting** explicitly. Confirm everyone heard the same outcomes.
2. **Confirm the handoff**: who, what, when. If you can't name these, the session didn't actually end.
3. **Ask for meta-feedback**: "What would make the next session better?" 30 seconds, gathers facilitation L3 you can apply.

A bad close: "Great session everyone, let's circle back."
