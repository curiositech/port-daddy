# What Makes a Deck Feel Premium

Load when the brief says "polished" / "investor-ready" / "premium" / "expensive" / "the deck looks cheap."

**The throughline:** premium decks *subtract* until only the argument remains. Cheap decks fill space; expensive decks resist it. The visual confidence is the signal.

## Reputed premium decks to study

1. **Airbnb seed deck (2008)** — 10 slides, one idea per slide, single-color accents, generous air. Every slide passes the "squint test": one message visible from across the room.
2. **Sequoia Capital investment memos** — Tiempos serif body, single-column layout, near-zero chartjunk. Reads like a private letter, not a presentation; the format itself signals "we don't perform."
3. **Stripe Sessions keynote decks** — full-bleed off-white (#FAFAFA), Söhne for headlines, monospaced metadata in muted gray, screenshots cropped to a single UI detail. The restraint mirrors Stripe's API aesthetic.
4. **Apple Keynote (Jobs era)** — pure black backgrounds, Helvetica Neue Ultra Light at huge sizes, one number per slide ("$0.99"). The black became the brand; the math was the drama.
5. **Linear marketing decks** — Inter Display, hairline 1px dividers, a single purple accent against neutral charcoal, monospace for version numbers and changelog dates. Feels engineered, not designed.
6. **IDEO method cards / case studies** — warm off-white paper stock (#FAF7F0), serif callouts, hand-cropped photography of real people working. The "paper" tactility reads as craft.
7. **Notion Series C deck (2021)** — single accent, lots of breathing room, screenshots presented as artifacts rather than features.

Find each via search. Study one slide deeply rather than skimming all of them.

## What premium decks DO

### Margins
- Outer margins minimum **8–10% of slide width** on each side.
- Top/bottom never less than **6% of slide height**.
- McKinsey/BCG decks routinely use 80–100px outer margins on a 1920px canvas.

### White space
- Target **40–60% of the slide as negative space**.
- If a slide is more than 60% filled, cut content. The slide is too dense for live; reset to slidedoc mode if you really need that much.

### Typography
- **Foundry-licensed type** (Söhne, Tiempos, GT Sectra, Inter Display, Berkeley Mono, Söhne Mono) for client-facing or investor work — the unfamiliarity itself signals investment.
- **System-safe stack** (Aptos, Helvetica Neue, SF Pro, Georgia) is appropriate for internal docs, engineering reviews, anything where pretending to be designed would be worse than being plain.
- **One display + one body + optional mono** is the ceiling. No more.

### Background
- **Warm off-white** (#FAF7F0, #FAFAF7) — decks meant to feel considered and human.
- **Pure white** (#FFFFFF) — clinical / data-forward work.
- **Near-black** (#0A0A0A — never pure #000) — product launches and stage keynotes.

### Color discipline
- **Three colors total**: one neutral (charcoal or warm gray), one ink (near-black for type), and **one** accent.
- Charts may add **muted tints of the accent** — never new hues.
- The "one-accent-color" rule: every chart, callout, divider, and CTA uses the same accent. Visual unity without coordination cost.

### Photography
- Commissioned or documentary-style only.
- Unsplash editorial works **if it doesn't look like Unsplash** (no "handshake," "diverse team laughing at laptop," "arrow hitting a target").
- **Real product screenshots cropped tight** read more premium than any photograph.

### Monospace for metadata
- Page numbers, dates, version, footnotes, figure labels in a mono (Söhne Mono, JetBrains Mono, Berkeley Mono, Geist Mono).
- Signals "this is a system, not a slide."

### Optical alignment, not pixel alignment
- Text baselines align across columns, not bounding boxes.
- Visual weight of a serif period is different from a sans period — the rule is about what the eye sees, not what the grid measures.

## What premium decks DON'T DO

### AP-1. Rainbow palette
6+ chart colors, gradient backgrounds, color-coded everything. Says "I'm afraid to make a choice."

### AP-2. Stock photo of a handshake / diverse team laughing at laptop / arrow hitting a target
Or any photograph that looks staged for a stock library. Even one of these in a deck cancels the rest of the design effort.

### AP-3. Skeuomorphs older than 2012
Drop shadows on text, beveled buttons, 3D pie charts, faux paper-clip pinning. Signals "I learned design before flat became standard and didn't update."

### AP-4. Center-aligned body paragraphs longer than one line
Center alignment is for single lines and titles. Multi-line center-aligned body is a wedding-invitation pattern; reads as amateur in a deck.

### AP-5. Default PowerPoint / Keynote template
Aptos at 18pt, title-and-bullets, the giveaway corporate beige. The single most-common signal that nobody designed the deck.

### AP-6. Filling every slide to the edges
Fear of white space is the loudest tell of cheapness. Premium decks resist filling space; designers who lack confidence add more elements.

### AP-7. Mixing 3+ typefaces
Or pairing a display serif with Comic Sans-adjacent script fonts for "warmth." Two faces max. Three only if the third is mono and used strictly for metadata.

### AP-8. Stretching photos / logos off-aspect
Hard fail. Looks broken, not designed.

### AP-9. Inline icon clipart
The little envelope icon next to "email," the gear icon next to "settings," the lightbulb next to "idea." Reads as 1998. Use type alone or commissioned icons.

### AP-10. Watermarks, page numbers shouting at the audience
Page number at 16pt in bold across the footer. Should be ~9pt mono in soft gray, never the visual focus.

## The "squint test"

Take a screenshot of every slide. Apply 50% blur. Each slide should still communicate its core message — one assertion, one image, one number, one quote. If a slide becomes meaningless under blur, it had too many competing visual elements.

## The "across the room" test

For live mode, view a printed slide from across the room (or hold your phone at arm's length and zoom to 50%). The hero element should still read. If you have to lean in, the slide isn't for projection.

## The "scroll past at 2x" test

For slidedocs that may be read on phones, scroll through at 2x speed. The action title of each slide should communicate the conclusion. If you have to slow down to understand a slide's point, the title isn't doing the job — fix it before designing more pages.
