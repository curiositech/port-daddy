## 1. How chapters open

### 1.1 Feynman: an objection, a question, a scene, an honest picture

The first page of "Atoms in Motion" has a chapter number and title, a section heading ("1–1 Introduction"), and prose addressed to "you" from its first sentence: "This two-year course in physics is presented from the point of view that you, the reader, are going to be a physicist. This is not necessarily the case of course, but that is what every professor in every subject assumes!" There is no abstract, keyword list, or objectives block; the Caltech web edition prepends a short editorial "Summary" box ("Science: We are not concerned with where a new idea comes from – the sole test of its validity is experiment …") whose provenance the page does not state and which could not be checked against the 1963 print layout.

He voices the reader's objection before teaching anything: "You might ask why we cannot teach physics by just giving the basic laws on page one and then showing how they work in all possible circumstances, as we do in Euclidean geometry … We cannot do it in this way for two reasons. First, we do not yet know all the basic laws: there is an expanding frontier of ignorance." He then fixes the epistemic stance of the whole course: "at each stage it is worth learning what is now known, how accurate it is, how it fits into everything else, and how it may be changed when we learn more."

Section 1–2 opens with the compression question — "If, in some cataclysm, all of scientific knowledge were to be destroyed, and only one sentence passed on to the next generations of creatures, what statement would contain the most information in the fewest words?" — and turns the answer into a scene: a drop of water "a quarter of an inch on the side," magnified two thousand times ("about as big as a large room"), two thousand times again ("about fifteen miles across"), and two hundred fifty times more, at which point Figure 1–1 enters the prose with its idealizations named on the spot: "This is a picture of water magnified a billion times, but idealized in several ways. In the first place, the particles are drawn in a simple manner with sharp edges, which is inaccurate." Later figures get the same treatment (Fig. 1–4 "is wrong because it is in two dimensions, but it is right qualitatively"). Scale anchors replace formulas ("if an apple is magnified to the size of the earth, then the atoms in the apple are approximately the size of the original apple"), and each stretch of theory lands on something the reader can test that evening: "Hence, if you wish to evaporate water turn on the fan!" Asides ride in parentheses and one footnote. The chapter ends not with a summary but with the question enlarged: "how much more is possible?"

### 1.2 SICP: epigraph, a one-sentence subject, then the sorcerer

Chapter 1 opens with "1 Building Abstractions with Procedures" and an epigraph from Locke's *Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (1690) enumerating the three "acts of the mind": combining simple ideas, "bringing two ideas … together … so as to take a view of them at once," and "separating them from all other ideas that accompany them in their real existence: this is called abstraction." The epigraph is the chapter's table of contents in seventeenth-century prose. The first sentence names the subject — "We are about to study the idea of a *computational process*" — and the first paragraph ends on the governing metaphor: "In effect, we conjure the spirits of the computer with our spells." Three paragraphs later the metaphor pays a bill ("A small bug in a computer-aided design program, for example, can lead to the catastrophic collapse of an airplane or a dam"), and all history is pushed into footnotes.

Section 1.1.1 reaches the concrete at once ("If you present Lisp with a number," `486`, then `(+ 137 349)`). Exercises are interleaved, not banked: 1.1–1.5 close §1.1.6 and 1.6–1.8 close §1.1.7, each right after the idea it tests; §1.1 alone carries 27 footnotes. The book says when a teaching model is only a model: "The purpose of the substitution is to help us think about procedure application, not to provide a description of how the interpreter really works."

The pattern is book-wide: Chapter 2 opens on Weyl ("we forget about what the symbols stand for"); Chapter 3 pairs Heraclitus ("Even while it changes, it stands still") with Karr ("Plus ça change, plus c'est la même chose"). The first-edition preface begins "A computer is like a violin" and holds the sentence the book is remembered for: "programs must be written for people to read, and only incidentally for machines to execute." Perlis's Foreword opens: "Educators, generals, dieticians, psychologists, and parents program. Armies, students, and some societies are programmed."

### 1.3 Knuth: two epigraphs and a graded ladder of exercises

*The Art of Computer Programming*, Vol. 1, opens Chapter 1 with Ada, Countess of Lovelace ("Many persons who are not conversant with mathematical studies imagine that because the business of [Babbage's Analytical Engine] is to give its results in numerical notation, the nature of its processes must consequently be arithmetical and numerical, rather than algebraical and analytical …") and Epictetus ("Practice yourself, for heaven's sake, in little things; and thence proceed to greater"). One frames the subject, the other the reader's work. Section 1.1 then opens with the history of the word "algorithm" and works Euclid's algorithm before stating the five formal features *[ordering from memory; unverified here]*. Every exercise carries a rating from 00 to 50, an "M"/"HM" tag for mathematical content, and an arrow for the especially instructive; the "Notes on the Exercises" ask that "each reader should at least make an attempt to solve all of the problems whose rating is 10 or less." *The TeXbook* marks optional depth with single and double "dangerous bend" signs in the margin. No published statement by Knuth on *how* he chooses epigraphs was found; none should be attributed to him.

### 1.4 Axler: results before machinery, definitions with motives

The uploaded preface is the third edition of *Linear Algebra Done Right* (Springer UTM, 2015): it reports "561 exercises, including 337 new exercises" and names the Fundamental Theorem of Linear Maps as 3.22. It opens on the teacher's situation — "You are about to teach a course that will probably give students their second exposure to linear algebra" — then justifies the title with a diagnosis: "Determinants are difficult, nonintuitive, and often defined without motivation. … This tortuous (torturous?) path gives students little feeling for why eigenvalues exist." The remedy is structural: "Once determinants have been banished to the end of the book, a new route opens to the main goal of linear algebra—understanding the structure of linear operators."

Design decisions are stated as decisions: "Each theorem now has a descriptive name"; "Exercises now appear at the end of each section, rather than at the end of each chapter"; "Many new examples have been added to illustrate the key ideas"; "As a visual aid, definitions are in beige boxes and theorems are in blue boxes"; optional material is named ("these topics can be skipped without running into problems elsewhere in the book"). The creed: "Mathematics can be learned only by doing." The "Preface for the Student" (Tier B) opens: "You cannot read mathematics the way you read a novel. If you zip through a page in less than an hour, you are probably going too fast."

### 1.5 Spivak, Strang, Sipser, CLRS, Pierce

*Spivak:* "Every aspect of this book was influenced by the desire to present calculus not merely as a prelude to but as the first real encounter with mathematics" (preface, Tier B); Lamport takes his own epigraph from the same preface — "precision and rigor are neither deterrents to intuition, nor ends in themselves, but the natural medium in which to formulate and think about mathematical questions." *Strang:* every section ends with "Review of the Key Ideas," "Worked Examples," a problem set, and "Challenge Problems"; topic headings are set in blue (Tier B). *Sipser:* "Proofs are presented with a 'proof idea' component to reveal the concepts underpinning the formalism" (publisher, Tier B). *CLRS:* "A starred section is not necessarily more difficult than an unstarred one, but it may require an understanding of more advanced mathematics"; sections end with short exercises, chapters with "problems" that "often introduce new material" and "chapter notes that give historical details and references" (preface, Tier B). *Pierce:* "The approach is pragmatic and operational; each new concept is motivated by programming examples … Each chapter is accompanied by numerous exercises and solutions" (MIT Press, Tier B).

### 1.6 Economics and game theory

*Osborne and Rubinstein* (MIT Press, 1994) open Chapter 1 with a definition that is also a scope warning: "Game theory is a bag of analytical tools designed to help us understand the phenomena that we observe when decision-makers interact." Their preface has a section no other book here has, "Disagreements Between the Authors": "We see no reason why a jointly authored book should reflect a uniform view." They decline completeness explicitly, not pretending "to provide a complete reference book" (Tier B paraphrase). *Myerson* (Harvard, 1991) begins: "Game theory can be defined as the study of mathematical models of conflict and cooperation between intelligent rational decision-makers." *Kreps* (Princeton, 1990) is deliberately informal, with few theorems (Tier B). *Varian* allocates pages by the reader's confusion — more consumer theory "because I have found that this is the material that students find the most mysterious" (Tier B). *Tadelis* (Princeton, 2013): "Concepts are first introduced using simple motivating examples, then developed carefully and precisely to present their general formulation, and finally used in a variety of applications" (Tier B). *Binmore's Playing for Real* (OUP, 2007) "retains the entertaining examples" of *Fun and Games* (1991) "but changes the organization to match how game theory courses are actually taught" (Tier B). *Fudenberg and Tirole* (MIT Press, 1991) grade sections with one to three daggers for audience level and exercises with one to three asterisks (Tier B). *Mas-Colell, Whinston, Green* (Oxford, 1995) open each chapter with a lettered "Introduction" section *[lettering and A/B/C exercise grades from memory; unverified]*. *Shoham and Leyton-Brown* (Cambridge, 2009) give the PDF away and end every chapter with "History and references." *Roth and Sotomayor* (1990) carry Aumann's foreword: "This book chronicles one of the outstanding success stories of the theory of games." *Milgrom* (Cambridge, 2004) announces its origin in lecture courses; *Acemoglu* (Princeton, 2009) could not be verified beyond its course-route preface. *Mankiw* states ten principles in three groups and refers back to them by number; his apparatus is publisher-driven and heavy — "Case Study," "In the News," "FYI," "Quick Quiz," "Chapter in a Nutshell," "Problems and Applications" (Tier B).

### 1.7 What is on the first page — and what is not

Across all of these books the first page of a chapter contains a subset of five things: number and title; an epigraph (SICP, Knuth); a question or scene (Feynman, Mankiw, Binmore); a one-sentence statement of the subject or claim (SICP, Myerson, Osborne–Rubinstein, Axler's descriptive theorem names); and a short map of what follows (Feynman's "the first three chapters will therefore outline …"; Strang's chapter prefaces).

Three items appear on none of them — an abstract, a keyword list, a reading-time estimate — with the single, telling exception of the web edition's editorial box on Feynman's page, an editor's addition to a web page rather than part of the chapter. The conventions live elsewhere:

- Chapter abstracts and keywords are *publisher metadata for online discovery*. MIT Press asks for a 3–6 sentence abstract (about 120 words, at most 150) and 5–10 keywords per chapter because "keywords increase the likelihood that your audience will be able to find your book through search on a retail website"; Cambridge says "chapter-level abstracts considerably increase the likelihood of books being surfaced in searches, and chapter keywords display on Cambridge Core." They are delivered with the manuscript and displayed on the platform, not printed as the chapter's opening.
- Reading-time estimates are a web-platform convention: Medium has shown them since its 2013 launch (about 265 words per minute); no book examined prints one.
- Learning-objective bullet lists are an open-textbook convention (the Queen's authoring guide recommends them "at the start of chapters and/or sections"); none of the advanced texts above uses them.

On epigraphs the *Chicago Manual of Style* (¶1.37) is the standing rule: an epigraph "may appear at the head of a chapter," "typically follows the chapter number and any chapter title," is set as a block quotation without quotation marks, and its source "is usually given on a line following the quotation, sometimes preceded by a dash" — "only the author's name and usually the title of the work"; "it is not customary to give a full citation."

## 2. Scholastic exposition: question first, objections, resolution

The medieval university taught by three linked acts: *lectio*, line-by-line reading of an authoritative text; *quaestio*, raising the questions where the text was puzzling or contradictory; and *disputatio*, the formal debate that resolved a disputed question with arguments for and against (Encyclopedia.com, "Scholastic Method"; Novikoff, *The Medieval Culture of Disputation*, 2013). Aquinas's *Summa Theologiae* is the condensed written form. Every article has one shape: a yes/no question ("Whether …"); numbered objections against the position to be defended ("It seems that …"); a *sed contra* ("On the contrary …"); the *respondeo* ("I answer that …"); and replies indexed to each objection. In I q.2 a.3 the pivots read: "On the contrary, It is said in the person of God: 'I am Who am.' (Exodus 3:14)" and "I answer that, The existence of God can be proved in five ways." Borrow three features: the question precedes any answer; the strongest counter-arguments are stated by the author, first, in their best form; the replies are indexed to the objections.

Euclid's *Elements*, Book I — 23 definitions, 5 postulates, 5 common notions, 47 propositions (Joyce's Clark University edition) — is the other ancient template, and its weakness as pedagogy is what Lakatos attacks in *Proofs and Refutations* (1976), Appendix 2: "In deductivist style, all propositions are true and all inferences valid. … Counterexamples, refutations, criticism cannot possibly enter." And: "Deductivist style hides the struggle, hides the adventure. The whole story vanishes, the successive tentative formulations of the theorem in the course of the proof-procedure are doomed to oblivion while the end result is exalted into sacred infallibility." Lakatos's own book is a classroom dialogue about Euler's polyhedron formula in which "monster-barring" and "exception-barring" are the students' answers to counterexamples. Pólya's *How to Solve It* (1945) gives the reader-side rhythm: understand the problem, devise a plan, carry it out, look back.

The modern books echo all of this. Feynman opens with an objection; Sipser's "proof idea" is the *respondeo* in plain words before the formal body; Osborne and Rubinstein print their disagreements; Lamport requires "that the last step of every proof be a statement of what it is that the proof is trying to prove," written Q.E.D., so question and answer are visibly matched.

## 3. What the writers say: actionable rules with sources

### 3.1 Halmos (1970), read in full

Halmos disowns his title on page one — "a more honest title might be HOW I WRITE MATHEMATICS" (p. 123) — and compresses the thesis into one paragraph (p. 124): "The basic problem in writing mathematics is the same as in writing biology, writing a novel, or writing directions for assembling a harpsichord: the problem is to communicate an idea. To do so, and to do it clearly, you must have something to say, and you must have someone to say it to, you must organize what you want to say, and you must arrange it in the order you want it said in, you must write it, rewrite it, and re-rewrite it several times, and you must be willing to think hard about and work hard on mechanical details such as diction, notation, and punctuation. That's all there is to it."

1. **Say one thing.** "there are two ways for a piece of writing not to have a subject (no ideas or too many)"; calculus books are bad because calculus "is not a subject because it is many subjects" (p. 125).
2. **Speak to someone specific.** "It helps me to think of a person, perhaps someone I discussed the subject with two years ago, or perhaps a deliberately obtuse, friendly colleague, and then to keep him in mind as I write" (p. 126). "The writer must anticipate and avoid the reader's difficulties" (p. 127).
3. **Organize first.** "The reader's attention is held because he was told early what to expect, and, at the same time and in apparent contradiction, pleasant surprises keep happening" (p. 128); the organization "is a graph, very likely a tree, but almost certainly not a chain" (p. 128).
4. **Organize around examples; leave things out.** "The heart of mathematics consists of concrete examples and concrete problems. Big general theories are usually afterthoughts based on small but profound insights; the insights themselves come from concrete special cases. The moral is that it's best to organize your work around the central, crucial examples and counterexamples" (p. 129). The reader most needs guidance "in the discovery of the things the proof does not prove" (p. 129).
5. **Design the alphabet first** — notation decided "mid-sentence in the heat of composition" will be bad (p. 130).
6. **Write in spirals; rewrite every word.** Chapters "get written and re-written in the order 1, 2, 1, 2, 3, 1, 2, 3, 4, etc." (p. 131); "Rewrite means write again—every word" (p. 132); "if you cannot find a title, the reason may be that the section doesn't have a single unified subject" (p. 133).
7. **Motivate, define, exemplify, counter-exemplify — then review from the new vantage.** "do right by it: motivate it, define it, give examples, and give counterexamples. That's Section 1. In Section 2 introduce the first related concept … and then, this is the important point, review Section 1, as nearly completely as possible, from the point of view of Section 2" (p. 133). Plant clues: a word "first mentioned several chapters earlier than its definition" (p. 134).
8. **Tell the reader where every statement stands.** "Is it obvious? (Say so.) Will a counterexample be supplied later? (Promise it now.) … do you merely mean that you have tried to derive q from p, you failed, and you don't in fact know whether p implies q? (Confess immediately!) In any event: take the reader into your confidence" (p. 137). "Complete honesty makes for greatest clarity" (p. 137).
9. **State the theorem first, in one short sentence.** "my answer is: first" (p. 138). "All that comes first; the statement comes next; and the proof comes last" (p. 139). "A list of eight hypotheses … and a list of six conclusions do not a theorem make; they are a badly expounded theory" (p. 139).
10. **Repeat exactly when the point is a small difference; never repeat to drive it home.** Use "the same words in the same order for as long as possible, and then, with a proper roll of drums, emphasize the difference" (p. 139); otherwise the reader "will wonder if what he is now learning is exactly the same … or just similar but different" (p. 140). "never repeat a proof" — find the lemma (p. 140).
11. **Words and symbols.** "Let 'we' mean 'the author and the reader'" (p. 141). "never use 'any' in mathematical writing" (p. 142). "The best notation is no notation … Pretend that you are explaining the subject to a friend on a long walk in the woods, with no paper available; fall back on symbolism only when it is really necessary" (p. 144). Prove "a typical (say 3 × 3) special case" (p. 146); write proofs forward (p. 147); "never start a sentence with a symbol" (p. 148).
12. **The page is an instrument.** Solid prose "will have a forbidding, sermony aspect"; computational hash "a frightening, complicated aspect"; "The golden mean is golden" (p. 148).
13. **Omit and stop.** "Half the art of good writing is the art of omission" (p. 149); let the manuscript "ripen"; "begin at the beginning, go on till you come to the end, and then, with no further ado, stop" (p. 152).

### 3.2 The others

- **Steenrod** (AMS, *How to Write Mathematics*, 1973) separates "the formal or logical structure consisting of definitions, theorems, and proofs" from "the complementary informal or introductory material" (Tier B). **Knuth, Larrabee, Roberts** (Stanford CS 209, 1989) could not be held; the rules overlapping Halmos — separate symbols with words, never open a sentence with a symbol, one notation per thing — are confirmed by derivative notes. **Krantz** (AMS, 1997; arXiv:1612.04888) and **Higham** (SIAM, 3rd ed. 2020) are handbooks of mechanics; Higham devotes "a whole chapter … to worked examples showing how writing can be improved" (Tier B).
- **Lamport** (Tier A). Diagnosis: "proofs are still written in prose pretty much the way they were in the 17th century … proofs are unnecessarily hard to understand, and they encourage sloppiness that leads to errors." Cure: "structure and naming" — "Adding structure makes it clear what the function of each assertion is … Naming all facts makes it easy to tell the reader exactly which ones are being used." Method: hierarchical numbering, a proof per step, a Q.E.D. step restating the goal, "about 4 to 10 steps" per level, so that "It is easy to ignore the subproofs and read just the five steps." Explanation slot: "a proof sketch that comes between a statement and its proof." Rigor test: "imagine that there is a curious child sitting next to us. Every time we write an assertion, the child asks: Why?" Limit: "Structured proofs make it possible, not inevitable."
- **Tao** (Tier A index; linked posts Tier B). "Use the introduction to 'sell' the key points of your paper; the results should be described accurately"; "invest some effort in both organising and motivating the paper, and in particular in selecting good notation and giving appropriate amounts of detail. But one should not over-optimise"; "making plenty of lemmas"; "writing a rapid prototype first"; "write professionally, and in one's own voice; it can also help to make your past self your target audience"; "take advantage of the English language, and not just rely purely on mathematical symbols." From the linked posts: dwell "on the most important, innovative, and crucial components" and be brief on the routine; from the career page, "Learn the limitations of your tools."
- **Gowers**, "My favourite pedagogical principle: examples first!" (19 October 2007): put the example before the general definition; do not define a thing before saying why you need it (Tier B paraphrase). **Chow** (arXiv:0712.1320, 2008): "Solving an open exposition problem means explaining a mathematical subject in a way that renders it totally perspicuous. Every step should be motivated and clear; ideally, students should feel that they could have arrived at the results themselves."
- **McCloskey**, *Economical Writing* (3rd ed., 2019): thirty-five rules — "A Paragraph Should Have a Point," "Be Concrete," "Avoid Boilerplate," and footnotes are "nests for pedants" (Tier B). **Thomson**, "The Young Person's Guide to Writing Economic Theory," *JEL* 37(1), 1999: "make your argument appear simple, and even trivial"; "summarize each [paragraph] in one sentence" (Tier B).
- **Tufte**, *Beautiful Evidence* (2006): show comparisons; show causality and mechanism; "completely integrate words, numbers, images, diagrams"; document the evidence (Tier B). On apparatus: "Sidenotes are much better than footnotes because you never know what the footnote holds. Sidenotes are best when the reader can tell, out of the corner of her eye, whether they are worth examining" (edwardtufte.com notebook, Tier B). Tufte-LaTeX codifies the style as "extensive use of sidenotes, tight integration of graphics with text, and well-set typography," with "a wide margin to provide ample room for sidenotes and small figures."
- **Zinsser**: "Clutter is the disease of American writing"; "Clear thinking becomes clear writing; one can't exist without the other." **Pinker**, *The Sense of Style* (2014), after Thomas and Turner: "The writer can see something that the reader has not yet noticed, and he orients the reader's gaze so that she can see it for herself" (Tier B).

### 3.3 Two specimen papers: introduction first, definitions when needed

The book cites both of these; they show how a first-rate economics paper and a first-rate formal-security paper place a model, its assumptions, and its results.

*Lizzeri (1994).* Pages of prose precede any symbol (four were read here; the roadmap runs on). The introduction opens on the literature ("Since Akerlof's (1970) work on the market for lemons …"), grounds the institution in everyday instances before naming it — "mechanics that test the quality of second hand cars, debt rating agencies in financial markets, schools in their rating of abilities through grades, department stores that have a reputation for carrying only the highest quality goods" — and poses the question in words ("one has to deal again with the question of information revelation, only one step back"). Then comes a section-by-section roadmap in which *each section's assumption and result are stated in plain English*: "It is assumed that tests are costless and perfectly precise and that the seller pays the fee to the intermediary. Under some conditions on the distribution of types there is a unique equilibrium outcome of the game. Perhaps surprisingly, this equilibrium involves no information revelation and the intermediary extracting all the surplus in the market." Footnotes carry the real-world asides (a Wall Street Journal advertisement; "Of course, there are other explanations, such as reputation"). The formal model appears only where it is first used.

*van der Meyden (2007).* The abstract and introduction state the contribution as an argument, not a definition ("we present a new argument against Haigh and Young's … definition … showing that it is too weak for the intuitions it seeks to capture. We present an example …"). Section 2 introduces the machine model at the moment it is needed ("We work here with the state-observed machine model used by Rushby [Rus92], but similar results would be obtained for other models"), gives Definition 1 in the middle of running prose, and builds only the apparatus the counterexample requires, announcing it as such: "We may now present our example illustrating a weakness of IP-security." Example 1 is worked by hand on the page (the reader can check `viewD1(α1) = viewD1(α2)` line by line), and Section 3 opens "As a response to Example 1, we consider several alternative definitions." The example is the pivot on which the new definitions turn; the theorems follow it.

The shared pattern is Halmos's: motivation and examples first, statement next, proof last; assumptions named in the same breath as the result; formal machinery rationed to what the argument uses.

## 4. Pedagogical structure inside chapters

**Examples before definitions is evidence, not taste.** Sweller and Cooper (1985) found that algebra students who studied worked examples, with study time held fixed, later solved problems faster and with far fewer errors than students who only solved problems — the worked-example effect. Kirschner, Sweller, and Clark (2006) generalize: for novices, guided instruction beats minimally guided discovery. Two refinements matter for a book whose readers range from newcomers to specialists: the *expertise reversal effect* (Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler, Sweller, 2003) — techniques that help novices can hurt experts, hence express lanes — and *fading* (Renkl and Atkinson, 2003): remove worked solution steps one at a time so the reader supplies the missing step, a "now you try" ladder rather than a cliff. Halmos said it from the inside ("organize your work around the central, crucial examples and counterexamples," p. 129); Feynman opens with a drop of water; SICP types `486` before it defines anything.

**Concrete before abstract, with a caveat.** Fyfe, McNeil, Son, and Goldstone (2014) review "concreteness fading": begin concrete and fade explicitly toward the abstract form. Kaminski, Sloutsky, and Heckler (2008) reported a transfer advantage for a single abstract presentation; a 2020 replication narrowed that claim. The defensible position: examples must be simple, motivating, and illustrative, and a stripped abstract example can be all three.

**Retrieval, not rereading.** Roediger and Karpicke (2006) — "Taking a memory test not only assesses what one knows, but also enhances later retention, a phenomenon known as the testing effect" — showed that after a week, tested students retained more than restudiers. Karpicke and Blunt (2011) found retrieval practice beat concept mapping even when the final test *was* concept mapping. Dunlosky et al. (2013) rated practice testing and distributed practice the two "high utility" techniques; rereading and highlighting fared poorly. The consequences: short retrieval prompts at section ends with answers available (the reader is unsupervised), and spaced re-asking of earlier chapters' questions later — which Halmos's spiral already does structurally.

**How the admired books implement this.** Exercises at the end of *every section* (Axler; SICP; CLRS). Ratings and flags for triage: Knuth's 00–50 with arrows; MacKay's 1–5 rating "similar to that used by Knuth (1968)," the recommended ones "marked by a marginal encouraging rat," and the page number of each solution printed beside the rating; Bishop's one to three stars; Fudenberg–Tirole's daggers. Solutions within reach: MacKay prints answers to many exercises; Nielsen and Chuang separate in-text exercises that "solidify understanding of basic material" from end-of-chapter problems that "introduce … new and interesting material." Fixed apparatus at section ends (Strang's "Review of the Key Ideas," "Worked Examples," "Challenge Problems") and chapter ends (Nielsen and Chuang's "summary of the main results" plus "History and further reading"; CLRS's chapter notes; Shoham–Leyton-Brown's "History and references"). Signals of essential versus optional: CLRS's stars, Knuth's dangerous bends, Axler's "can be skipped." Asides in the margin or footnote, never the main line: SICP's 27 footnotes in one section; Tufte's sidenotes; MacKay's margins. Feynman's asides are household experiments in parentheses, so the aside is itself a retrieval cue ("blow on soup to cool it!").

### 4.1 Provenance: letting the reader leap back to the original

The author's working rule — that every derived claim should let a reader jump back to the passage it came from, "so you can always spot the original context" — has a long pedigree in the books above, and it separates the ones that age well from the ones that do not.

- Knuth copies quotations "verbatim from the original sources," and TAOCP carries "History and Bibliography" sections with dense citations *[section titles from memory]*, so a claim about an algorithm's origin can be checked against the paper it came from. CLRS's chapter notes and Nielsen–Chuang's "History and further reading" (which gives "citations and references for the whole chapter") do the same at chapter grain.
- SICP's footnotes are locators: the Lisp history footnote points at McCarthy 1960 and the 1962 manual; the chaotic-Solar-System aside points at Abelson et al. 1992 and Sussman and Wisdom 1992. The aside carries its own source.
- MacKay's margins are pointers in the literal sense — the page number of a solution is printed beside the exercise.
- Tufte's case for sidenotes is a provenance case: a citation the reader can see "out of the corner of her eye" costs nothing to ignore and nothing to follow.
- Lamport's "Naming all facts makes it easy to tell the reader exactly which ones are being used" extends to citations: "If that result is stated as a numbered theorem, surely its use should be mentioned." Halmos's catechism includes the same duty: "Is it a standard but for present purposes irrelevant part of the literature? (Give a reference.)" (p. 137).
- Feynman is the honest counterexample: the *Lectures* carry no references, because they are lectures. A textbook that wants to be checked cannot imitate that.

The book's own apparatus already points this way. *The Harbor After the Harbor* asks that a digest be "a projection of a claim-evidence graph" whose claim object carries `derivation`, `evidence-refs`, and `verifier`, so the operator can ask "Why is this claim present? Where did its inputs come from?" — database why-, where-, and why-not provenance. *The Full Wheel*'s honesty bar requires "citation-required compaction" and that the record of what a thing should be "always lives on the artifact itself … where the next reader will actually look." The uploaded screenshot of relay.portdaddy.dev shows the pattern in the product: each step is a one-sentence bold summary with a timestamp offset, an indented quotation of the underlying text, and a collapsed "raw step data" disclosure — Lamport's top-level-then-subproof reading order rendered as an interface. The textbook consequence is in §7.4: every derived claim carries a result ID *and* a locator into its source, set in the margin.

## 5. Typography and design conventions

The admired books are restrained, and restraint is stated as policy. Axler uses color for exactly two things — "definitions are in beige boxes and theorems are in blue boxes" — and calls the result "an unusually pleasant appearance in both print and electronic versions." Strang uses blue for topic headings. Springer's Graduate Texts in Mathematics are "yellow books of a standard size," distinguished from the older all-yellow Undergraduate Texts only "by a white band at the top" — a series identity carried by color and format, with no chapter art. Tufte's books, and the Tufte-LaTeX classes that imitate them, use a wide outer margin for sidenotes and small figures, full-width figures where needed, captions beside the figure, and two typefaces (Bembo and Gill Sans). MacKay typeset his Cambridge book in that idiom, with marginal exercise marks and front-matter roadmaps for three courses. SICP's chapter opener is number, title, epigraph, first paragraph — nothing else; Knuth's is number, title, two epigraphs.

Books that *do* put art or dialogue at chapter boundaries make it do argumentative work. Hofstadter alternates every chapter of *Gödel, Escher, Bach* with a dialogue modeled on Carroll's "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles," its form enacting the idea the next chapter treats (the "Crab Canon" reads the same backward); Petzold's *Code* opens on two children signalling with flashlights before any electronics; Shiffman's *The Nature of Code* ends every chapter with a project prompt that builds on the previous one; Manning's "Grokking" series is illustrated on every spread. In each case the picture carries a mechanism; none uses a decorative plate. Feynman's captions are the model for figure honesty: the idealizations are named in the sentence that introduces the figure. Chicago's epigraph rule and Halmos's page rule ("the golden mean is golden") cover the rest.

## 6. Voice: confidence without overclaiming

The best authors state their limits in the same register as their results, and at the same location.

- Feynman: "everything we know is only some kind of approximation, because we know that we do not know all the laws as yet. Therefore, things must be learned only to be unlearned again or, more likely, to be corrected." Of his own course, in the 1963 preface: "I don't think I did very well by the students" (Tier B).
- Axler: "You probably cannot cover everything in this book in one semester"; whole chapters are marked skippable; the determinant-free route is argued for, not asserted.
- Lamport: "Structured proofs make it possible, not inevitable." "Another mistake I made was giving the impression that I know the best way of writing proofs. … I am sure my way of writing proofs can be improved, and I encourage mathematicians to improve it."
- Halmos: "Whenever you tell him something, tell him where it stands: this has been proved, that hasn't, this will be proved, that won't. Emphasize the important and minimize the trivial" (p. 137); and the essay's last words: "Do, please, as I say, and not as I do, and you'll do better. Then rewrite this essay and tell the next generation how to do better still" (p. 152).
- Osborne and Rubinstein: game theory is "a bag of analytical tools"; the authors print their own disagreements; they "do not pretend to provide a complete reference book."
- Kreps points out "both strengths and weaknesses" of every technique; Jaynes declares his "finite sets policy" up front as a self-imposed limit; Sipser labels intuition as intuition before the proof that carries the weight (all Tier B).

The common move: name the epistemic status of each claim where the claim is made, and name the scope of the whole at the front. That is the Harbor book's existing rule (`proved`, `verified-framework`, `model-checked`, `empirical`, `designed`, `running`; `[verified]` versus `[internal, script, seed]`), which is why the rule belongs on the page and not only in the manifest.

The project's reviewers have already ruled on the voice, and the ruling agrees with the books. *The Harbor After the Harbor* asks that "every important statement" be labelled one of four things — "1. Theorem: follows from a formal model whose assumptions are explicit. 2. Design invariant: intended to hold; backed by implementation and tests. 3. Model-checked property: proved only for a bounded or abstract program model. 4. Empirical hypothesis: needs measurement, calibration, or an experiment" — with a two-sided warning: "Do not call a type check or uniqueness constraint a theorem about the world. Conversely, do not undersell properties that can be proved once the boundary is clean." Its "What cannot honestly be promised" list ("Literal deletion of information a party already observed"; "Availability if either key owner refuses or aborts") is a model boundary section, as is the *Provable-Results Portfolio*'s closing list of "what I decline to claim, in the volume's own spirit." *Revising the Treatise* adjudicates the tone fight between the product reviews and the rigor review: "prose-level apologetic tics ('honestly not built yet,' repeated mid-sentence) induce fatigue and read as fragility — cut those"; "keep the four-valued status tags and add a clinical 'Limitations & Boundaries' section per chapter; delete the in-prose apologies. State what the system does with confidence; state what it does not do with precision; stop apologizing for the gap between them." Its summary line belongs above the editor's desk: "Keep the honesty structure, cut the honesty tone." That is Halmos's "where it stands" and Lamport's "possible, not inevitable" in one sentence.

## 7. What this means for the Harbor book

The seven propositions are already sentences ("Legibility is a budget, not a mood"; "Delegated authority must only narrow") and each already has a governing question ("what must remain visible?"; "how may authority travel without silently growing?"). The reviewers propose a different spine — *The Harbor After the Harbor*'s eight chapters from "The Evidence-Bearing Work Unit" to "Federation," whose order "makes the causal dependency honest: first define and secure the work; then preserve and share it; only then price it," adopted by *Revising the Treatise* as Move 1, with the seven papers surviving "as essays or interludes." The template below is spine-agnostic: it governs how any chapter opens and closes, whichever order the propositions finally take. Two of the reviewers' cuts bear on it directly: the estimate that the volume "could lose roughly forty percent without losing a single core idea" by cutting "Repeated reader maps, estimator catalogs, tutorial-level token material, ornamental theorem labels, and the recurring Hobbes/Locke/Parfit/Sen detours" (Halmos's omission rule, applied), and the refinement that one philosophical frame per chapter may stay "as an explicitly-labelled interlude" — the GEB dialogue slot, not the argument's premise. That is the Axler/Myerson move done right, and the existing exposition rule (opening names the failure, the question, and the result IDs; handoff names what may be assumed, what was not solved, and the next uncertainty) is sound. What the evidence adds is the *order and form* of those elements on the first two pages, and four apparatus decisions.

### 7.1 The chapter opener: exact sequence for pages 1–2

**Page 1 (recto).**

1. **Number and proposition-title.** The proposition sentence is the title, as in the architecture note.
2. **One epigraph, at most two, set per Chicago ¶1.37** (block quotation, source line with a dash, author and work only). Admission test: it must say, in someone else's older words, the thing the chapter will make precise — Locke's "acts of the mind" for abstraction, Lovelace for symbolic computation. If the editor cannot state in one sentence what the chapter does to the epigraph, cut it. Prefer a primary-source sentence from the chapter's own lineage (Ostrom, Lampson, Hume, Coase, Aumann, Lamport) over aphorisms.
3. **The failure, as a scene with a number in it** (three to eight sentences), on the model of Feynman's drop of water and SICP's dam: a concrete operator, swarm, and loss at a stated scale ("four hundred summaries an hour, one of which hides the write that mattered"). No terminology yet.
4. **The question, in one italic sentence**, in the *utrum* form the architecture note already uses: *What must remain visible?*
5. **The claim in one breath, boxed, with its epistemic kind.** Halmos: state the theorem "first," in one short sentence. The box carries the proposition in words, the governing result IDs, and their manifest kinds (`proved` / `model-checked` / `empirical`) — the house style's "boxed theorem" move, promoted to the first page.

**Page 2 (verso).**

6. **Objections** (three numbered sentences): the strongest reasons a competent reader will think the claim false or trivial, in their best form, as in the *Summa* and Feynman's "You might ask why we cannot …", each with a forward pointer to the section that answers it.
7. **The route and the express lane** (one paragraph). Say what the chapter will do in order, and mark which sections an expert may skip and which a newcomer must not — CLRS's starred-section sentence is the template.
8. **First worked example, hand-checkable, before any definition** (Sweller; Halmos's 3 × 3 case; Feynman's apple): one instance of the mechanism with numbers the reader can redo on paper. Only then the definitions, each followed at once by an example and a non-example (Halmos rule 7).

Nothing else appears on these two pages. No abstract, no keyword list, no reading-time estimate, no learning-objective bullets, no result table.

### 7.2 Where the meta-information goes

Chapter abstracts (120–150 words) and 5–10 keywords per chapter are still written — publishers require them — but they live in `mega-volume-epistemic-manifest.yaml`, in the publisher's metadata sheet, and, if the book wants them visible, in a front-matter "Guide to the chapters" (one paragraph per chapter, as MacKay's roadmap does for courses). Reading-time estimates do not appear in print. The result-ID governance line belongs in the page-1 claim box and again in the handoff, not in a header block.

### 7.3 Proofs, proof ideas, and structure

Adopt Sipser's convention and Lamport's discipline together. Every theorem is preceded by a labelled **Proof idea** in plain English (Lamport's "proof sketch that comes between a statement and its proof"). Every proof longer than about ten lines is a hierarchical structured proof with named steps, a final Q.E.D. step restating the goal, and 4–10 steps per level, readable at the top level with the subproofs ignored. Mechanized results (ProVerif, Kani, TLC) cite the machine-checked artifact by ID in the proof's first line, as Lamport's appendix cites the TLAPS-checked module, and the prose proof states only what the machine did *not* check. Halmos's rule applies directly: the ε-ledger and no-mint results should each be statable in one short sentence with the standing assumptions named once above the box.

### 7.4 Worked examples, exercises, recitation, boundaries

- **Worked examples:** at least one per section, always before the general statement, with numbers that can be redone by hand; fade them (Renkl–Atkinson) so that the third example in a chapter leaves a step for the reader.
- **Exercises:** at the end of every section (Axler, SICP); rated on a small scale with the recommended ones flagged (Knuth's arrow, MacKay's rat); every core exercise has a solution in the back matter, its page printed beside the exercise (MacKay), because the reader is unsupervised and the testing effect requires feedback. Longer "problems" that introduce new material (CLRS, Nielsen–Chuang) go at the chapter end. Keep the three kinds the review corpus already uses — *Check* (short, closed), *Trace* (walk a mechanism through a concrete run), *Open* (a design or proof obligation) — and adopt the solution key's two rules: "Where an exercise depends on a false premise in the manuscript, the solution repairs the premise instead of manufacturing the requested conclusion," and "Open problems receive a defensible design or proof obligation rather than a fictional closed-form solution." Never park load-bearing content in a starred exercise; the digest-poisoning threat, as the Coordination Review says, "belongs in Chapter II's threat model as a named section, not in a starred exercise."
- **Provenance:** every derived claim carries its result ID and a locator into its source — page and section for a book, theorem number for a paper, script and seed for a number — set as a sidenote, so the reader can leap from the book's sentence to the original passage without a search (Knuth's notes, MacKay's page pointers, SICP's footnotes; §4.1).
- **Recitation:** three retrieval prompts at the end of each section ("Without looking: what is the one thing the local writer can refuse before commit?"), answers in the back; and in each chapter's route paragraph, one or two prompts that re-ask an earlier chapter's question from the new vantage — Halmos's spiral made explicit, the "roll of drums" reserved for the one thing that changed.
- **Summary and further reading:** end each chapter with Strang's "Review of the Key Ideas" as a list of *what remains coupled*, then a "History and references" section in the Nielsen–Chuang/CLRS manner, then the handoff.
- **Boundaries:** the handoff's "what this chapter deliberately did not solve" is written in Halmos's imperative — for each open item, say whether it is proved elsewhere, promised later, out of scope with a reference, or unknown ("Confess immediately!") — and Lamport's "possible, not inevitable" is the model sentence for every mechanism whose guarantee depends on operation rather than on the theorem.

### 7.5 Design

Color for navigation, status, and semantic contrast only, exactly as the architecture note says and as the evidence agrees: two box colors (Axler), one accent for headings (Strang), a series color for part openers (Springer). Wide outer margins carrying sidenotes for asides, result-ID citations, and small regime figures (Tufte, MacKay); captions that name the idealization in the introducing sentence (Feynman); part-title pages may carry art, chapter openers do not (SICP, Knuth, Axler). Boxes only for theorems, protocols, and worked examples; "ordinary argument remains ordinary prose."

For the part openers the house art direction already exists: the cut-paper prompts blessed 2026-07-04 specify a "Handcrafted cut-paper diorama photographed macro with shallow depth of field," a fixed nine-color palette, "Swiss-modern composition: one hero subject, generous negative space, objects aligned to an invisible grid," and — decisive for a book — "All labels and signage rendered as blank color blocks and plain paper rectangles, keeping the scene entirely wordless," in light and dark variants. Wordless, one-subject, palette-disciplined plates are the right kind of art for a part page and the wrong kind for a chapter opener, where the epigraph and first sentence must do the work. The relay screenshot (summary sentence, indented quotation, collapsed "raw step data") is the model for how a web edition should render a theorem: statement visible, proof idea one tap away, machine artifact one more.

### 7.6 Voice rules to enforce in editing

State each claim's status where it is made (Halmos p. 137; the manifest kinds). Prefer "the author and the reader" *we*, declarative sentences, and English over symbols until a symbol is really necessary (Halmos pp. 141–144; Tao). Repeat definitions word for word across chapters and drum only the difference (Halmos p. 139). Sell the introduction accurately (Tao). Say what the tools cannot do (Tao; Lamport). Hold the prose to the project's own honesty bar: "every 'done' carries evidence a stranger can check" (*The Full Wheel*, §12). Keep Feynman's stance in Chapter I's opening: the book teaches what is now known, how accurate it is, how it fits, and how it may change.
