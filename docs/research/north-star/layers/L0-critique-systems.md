## Steelman (distributed-systems / local-first framing)

The dossier's strongest claim — and it is a genuinely good one — is that **Port Daddy correctly identified that it does not have a distributed systems problem, and refused to manufacture one.** This is the single most important architectural decision a local-first coordination layer can make, and the field is littered with corpses that got it wrong.

Stated in my terms: the swarm-coordination problem *looks* like it wants consensus. Many agents, concurrent contention for shared resources, liveness detection, failover — every instinct trained on Raft/Paxos/CRDTs says "replicate the state, run an agreement protocol." The dossier's L0 instead collapses the entire problem onto a **single-writer, single-machine, single-file substrate** and lets SQLite's OS-level file locking plus `busy_timeout` serialize all mutations. This is exactly the Kleppmann "single-leader is the right default; you adopt consensus only when forced" argument, and the dossier cites it correctly. By doing so it sidesteps FLP (1985) entirely — there is no agreement to reach because there is one decider — and it earns the right to make linearizable claims about claims/locks for free, because SQLite under WAL gives you serializable transactions on a single writer. The FLP citation is used *correctly and for the right reason*: consensus is hard, so don't have a consensus problem. That is a mature, field-aware move, and most "agent coordination frameworks" never reach it.

Second steelman: the **regimentation-vs-enforcement split** (Jones & Sergot via Schneider's enforceable-policies lens) is the right formal frame, and the dossier is unusually honest that a runtime monitor (the Arbiter) is a *detector*, not a type system. The recognition that `HEARTBEAT_FRESHNESS` is "intrinsically observe-only" because you cannot distinguish slow from dead is a direct, correct application of Chandra & Toueg (1996) failure-detector theory. Most systems claim "we prevent X" when they mean "we notice X and yell." The dossier refuses that lie in I8. That is the behavior of someone who has actually read the canon.

Third: the **dual-runtime soundness hazard** (truth computed under `bun:sqlite`, verified under `better-sqlite3`) is named as a first-class kernel property rather than buried as an impl detail. From a reliability standpoint this is the correct paranoia — it is a *differential-implementation* problem, and naming it as I11 (with the honest "CI parity is the recurring failure" caveat) is exactly right.

I verified the substrate claims against the tree: `lib/db.ts` sets `journal_mode=WAL`, `synchronous=NORMAL`, `wal_autocheckpoint=200`, `busy_timeout=5000`, `foreign_keys=ON`, and `wal_checkpoint(TRUNCATE)` on clean shutdown — exactly as stated. The lock acquire path (`lib/locks.ts:183-213`) is a bare `INSERT` against a `PRIMARY KEY`, catching `SQLITE_CONSTRAINT` and returning the holder. So I2 (mutual exclusion via atomic PK insert) is **genuinely sound** — the dossier earns that theorem. Good.

## Gaps & inconsistencies (prioritized)

**1. I1 (Durability) is stated too strongly and the in-code justification is wrong. (Critical — this is the keystone theorem.)**
The dossier says I1 = "a write that returns success survives process crash," labeled `BUILT`, mechanism "WAL + `synchronous=NORMAL`." The code comment at `lib/db.ts:388` asserts: *"NORMAL sync is safe in WAL mode — WAL already guarantees crash safety."* This is the standard misconception. Under SQLite WAL with `synchronous=NORMAL`, the guarantee is **crash-consistency, not durability**. The WAL is *not* fsync'd on every commit; it is synced only at checkpoint. Per SQLite's own documentation, in WAL mode `NORMAL` "commits might lose durability following a power loss or hard reset" — committed transactions that have not yet reached a checkpoint can roll back after an OS crash or power failure, though the database remains uncorrupted. So the honest statement of I1 is:

> *A returned-success write survives a **process** crash (the OS buffer persists), but may **not** survive an **OS crash or power loss** until the next checkpoint.*

This is precisely the distinction Gray & Reuter (1992) draw between **atomicity/consistency** and **durability**, the "D" in ACID. The dossier cites Gray & Reuter as "the formal ground for I1" — but the cited ground actually *refutes* the unqualified claim. A reliability expert reads "survives crash · BUILT · WAL" and assumes power-loss durability, which the configuration deliberately trades away for throughput. **I1 must be split into I1a (process-crash durable, BUILT) and I1b (power-loss durable, NOT GUARANTEED under NORMAL — would require `synchronous=FULL` or `wal_checkpoint` on the commit path).** This is the most important correction in the whole review: the kernel's foundational promise is overstated by exactly one fault class, and that fault class (power loss) is the one a "the file survives every agent's death" pitch most invites the reader to assume.

**2. I2's theorem is sound but the lock critical section has a check-then-act race the dossier elides. (High.)**
`lib/locks.ts:164` runs `releaseExpired` (a `DELETE … WHERE expires_at < now`) and *then* `acquire` (the `INSERT`) as **two separate statements, not one transaction.** I grepped: there is no `db.transaction(...)` wrapping the lock acquire path (transactions exist elsewhere — `bonds.ts`, `symbol-index.ts` — but not here). The PK insert is individually atomic, so two *fresh* acquirers can't both win (good, I2 holds for the contended-fresh case). But the **expire-then-acquire sequence is not atomic**: between the `DELETE` of an expired lock and the `INSERT`, another writer's interleaving is possible. On a single SQLite connection inside the daemon this is serialized by the connection; but the dossier's whole §1.1 premise is that *"concurrency across many CLI invocations + the daemon is serialized by SQLite's file lock,"* i.e. multiple processes/connections. Across connections, `busy_timeout` retries but SQLite's default `DEFERRED` transaction acquires the write lock lazily on first write, opening a window for `SQLITE_BUSY` mid-sequence rather than clean serialization. The fix is `BEGIN IMMEDIATE` (or wrapping acquire in `db.transaction`), which I confirmed is **absent** from this path. The dossier should either (a) state that all kernel writes go through the single daemon connection (making cross-process write contention a non-issue, but then §1.1's "many CLI invocations serialized by the file lock" is misleading — they're serialized by the *daemon's HTTP queue*, not the file lock), or (b) add `BEGIN IMMEDIATE` and make I2 a real cross-connection theorem. Right now the two stories contradict.

**3. §3.11 "no cross-organ transactional atomicity" is correctly identified but mislabeled VISION when it should be a named hazard with a known pattern. (Medium.)**
The dossier flags that "claim port AND open session AND record commitment" is three unwrapped writes. Correct. But this is not a research frontier — it is the **Saga problem** (Garcia-Molina & Salem, 1987), and the absence of either a wrapping transaction *or* compensating actions is a defect, not "VISION." On a single SQLite file the cheap fix is real: these are local writes; `db.transaction()` already exists in the codebase and gives you all-or-nothing for free. The dossier should cite Sagas and state the choice explicitly: *either* wrap multi-organ operations in one local transaction (trivially available, single file) *or* define compensations. Calling it VISION understates how cheap the correct answer is here.

**4. The single-writer invariant (the steelman's crown jewel) is asserted but never pinned to a mechanism that the code actually guarantees. (Medium-High.)**
The dossier says the single-writer property "holds by construction on one machine." But "by construction" is doing enormous work. SQLite allows *multiple* writer connections (serialized, not rejected). What actually makes Port Daddy single-writer is the **daemon being the sole holder of a write connection** — yet the CLI is documented (MEMORY, §1.1) to be invokable directly, and `assertNotProdInTest` plus `0600` perms don't prevent a second `pd` process from opening the same file read-write. So the invariant is **a convention enforced by "everything goes through the daemon," not a mechanism.** This matters because §5's adjacency contract leans on it. The honest version: *single-writer is an architectural discipline (all mutations route through the daemon process), backstopped — not guaranteed — by SQLite's file lock if the discipline is violated.* The dossier's binary-drift/install-path guards exist precisely because a second copy of the daemon *can* run; that same risk applies to a second writer. State it.

**5. The clock-trust gap (§3.12 / Lamport cite) is right but doesn't go far enough even at L0.** Even on one machine, `Date.now()` is **not monotonic** — it moves backward on NTP step adjustments, which silently corrupts every `expires_at < now` sweep and every TTL. The single-machine assumption removes the *distributed* clock problem but not the *wall-clock-vs-monotonic* problem. The fix (`process.hrtime`/monotonic clock for intervals, wall clock only for display) is a real L0 hardening, not an L3 deferral. The Lamport citation is for ordering across nodes; the *intra-node* hazard is the more immediate bug and is uncited.

**6. WAL + networked filesystem caveat is in §5 but the dossier doesn't connect it to the durability claim.** §5 correctly notes "networked filesystems void the warranty" (SQLite over NFS = broken advisory locks). But that caveat *also* destroys I2 (mutual exclusion) entirely, not just performance — if `flock` is unreliable, two writers can both win. The adjacency contract should mark this as a **correctness precondition for I2**, not a footnote.

**7. Merkle tamper-evidence (I9) — the threat model is incoherent as stated.** §3.8/OP-9 admit the DB is `0600` plaintext and a same-user process is out of scope. But a Merkle audit chain's *only* adversary is someone who can edit the DB — which, given `0600` and same-user exclusion, is **exactly the adversary you've declared out of scope.** So against the stated threat model, the Merkle chain defends against *nobody*. It's meaningful only against (a) future cross-machine sync (L3) or (b) a non-same-user tamperer. The dossier should state that I9 is **provisioning for L3**, not an L0-active defense — otherwise it's tamper-evidence theater, exactly the failure OP-6 warns about, applied to the whole organ.

## What an expert would add

- **A precise durability table by fault class.** Reliability work lives or dies on the fault taxonomy: process crash / OS crash / power loss / disk fault / fs corruption. State which faults each invariant survives. Right now "survives crash" collapses four distinct fault classes. ARIES (Mohan et al. 1992) — already cited — is built entirely around this taxonomy; use it.

- **A recovery story, not just a durability story.** The dossier proves writes are durable (mostly) but says almost nothing about **what happens on daemon restart with a dirty WAL**: who replays, who validates the Merkle chain on boot, what happens to half-applied cross-organ operations (§3.11) after a crash. "Resurrection" is about *agents* dying; the kernel needs a story for the *kernel* dying mid-write. `pd attest`'s "refuse-to-serve on CRITICAL fail" is the hook, but boot-time WAL recovery + integrity check + chain verification should be a named invariant.

- **An explicit consistency-model statement.** The dossier never says the word "linearizable" or "serializable." It should: single-writer SQLite gives **serializable** transactions and, because there's one decider, **linearizable** external behavior for claims/locks. That's the formal payoff of the single-writer choice and it should be stated as a theorem (it's the thing that makes L1's "typed ownership" trustworthy). Right now the strongest formal property of the layer is left implicit.

- **`busy_timeout` is a liveness hazard, not just a convenience.** A 5-second busy timeout under sustained write contention means **unbounded latency and `SQLITE_BUSY` errors under load** — there is no fairness (the dossier's own OP-1) *and* no backpressure signal to callers. Nygard's *Release It!* (in your skill set) would flag this as a missing bulkhead/timeout-budget. At swarm scale (dozens of agents tight-looping), the single writer is a throughput ceiling the dossier never quantifies. "One writer, no consensus" is correct *and* is a scalability bound that should be stated honestly alongside its benefits.

- **Differential testing as the I11 discharge.** OP-3 asks for it; an expert would specify it: a property-based / differential test harness that runs the same operation sequence against both runtimes and asserts identical observable state. This is standard practice for differential-implementation soundness and is the only thing that turns I11 from "hope" into evidence. It belongs in CI as a gate, not as an open problem.

## Verdict

**Not yet paper-ready, but close, and honest about most of its seams.** This is a genuinely sophisticated L0 treatment — the single-writer/no-consensus thesis is correct and well-defended, the regimentation/enforcement split is the right formal frame, and the ADR-0045 honest-labeling discipline is applied with rare integrity. The FLP, Chandra-Toueg, and Kleppmann citations are used for the right reasons.

What must change before it's paper-ready:

1. **Fix I1 (blocking).** Split durability by fault class. "WAL + `synchronous=NORMAL` ⇒ survives crash" is false for power loss; the code's own justifying comment is wrong. This is the kernel's foundational promise and it is overstated by exactly the fault class the pitch most invites readers to assume. Either re-label I1 honestly or change the pragma — but the paper cannot ship the current claim, because it contradicts its own Gray & Reuter citation.

2. **Reconcile the I2 mechanism story (blocking).** Either (a) state plainly that all writes route through the single daemon connection (and then fix §1.1, which currently credits the *file lock* for serializing many CLI processes — it's the daemon queue), or (b) add `BEGIN IMMEDIATE` to the expire-then-acquire critical section in `lib/locks.ts` and make I2 a true cross-connection theorem. As written, §1.1 and I2 tell two incompatible stories about *what* serializes writers.

3. **Add the consistency-model theorem (serializable/linearizable).** It's the formal payoff of the whole layer and it's missing.

4. **Re-class three items honestly:** cross-organ atomicity (§3.11) is the Saga problem with a cheap local fix, not VISION; the Merkle chain (I9) defends against nobody under the *stated* threat model and should be re-scoped as L3 provisioning; the clock hazard is intra-node (monotonicity), not just inter-node.

5. **Add a kernel-recovery / boot-integrity invariant.** The dossier covers agent death exhaustively and kernel death barely.

The through-line sentence — *"the kernel is solid where it is local and provisional exactly where the economy will need it cryptographic and continuous"* — is rhetorically excellent and substantially true. But "solid where it is local" currently overstates power-loss durability and cross-process exclusion. Tighten those two and this is a strong L0 paper.
