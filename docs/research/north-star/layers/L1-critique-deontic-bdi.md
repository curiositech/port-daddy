## Steelman (deontic logic & protocol design framing)

The strongest version of this dossier's argument is genuinely strong, and I want to state it at full power before I attack it.

**The core thesis is correct and well-grounded: coordination is not transport.** L0 moves bytes; L1 is the layer where bytes acquire *illocutionary force*. This is the right Austin/Searle move — a `request` is not data, it is a speech act that creates an obligation-to-respond in a felicitous context. The dossier understands that the daemon's job is to record the *rational effect* (FIPA's term for the recordable institutional consequence) rather than the agent's private intention, and it correctly locates dishonesty in the gap between perlocution and rational effect. That is the single most sophisticated observation in the document, and most protocol designs never reach it.

**The deontic triad is mapped to the right enforcement mechanisms.** Prohibition→regimentation (Arbiter), obligation→enforcement-with-sanction (commitment monitor), permission→capability is exactly the Jones & Sergot (1993) distinction between *regimentation* (making the violation physically impossible — "you cannot enter the state") and *enforcement* (allowing the violation but attaching a sanction — "you may break it but you will pay"). A normative system that conflates these two is broken from the start; this dossier keeps them separate, and that is the load-bearing correctness property of the whole layer. The robot-caretaker pattern from the normative-BDI canon — that a violated prohibition must remain *representable and explainable*, not silently impossible — is implicitly respected by the choice to enforce rather than regiment obligations.

**The completionist move from A–D to A–H is intellectually honest and substantively right.** The seed (ADR-0047) gave a spanning subtree; the dossier names what a spanning *tree* of the actual problem space requires. Two of its additions are not decoration — they are mandatory and the field would refuse the paper without them:
- **The directory layer (G)** is genuinely missing from the seed and genuinely required: Contract-Net (Smith 1980) is *defined* over a known bidder set, and the bidder set comes from FIPA's Directory Facilitator (yellow pages) / AMS (white pages). You cannot specify `cfp` without specifying who hears it. This is not a nice-to-have; it is a precondition of the keystone protocol.
- **Distributed quiescence (D2/gap 6)** is the deepest and most correct catch in the document. "Terminate when no pending commitments remain in the branch" is a *global stable-property predicate over a distributed computation*, and the dossier correctly names Dijkstra-Scholten (diffusing computations) and Chandy-Lamport (consistent snapshots) as the actual algorithms, and — crucially — bounds the whole thing with FLP (1985): under asynchrony + one crash you cannot have *perfect* termination detection, so quiescence must be *eventually-correct atop a failure detector*. That is exactly the right theory chain, in the right order, with the right impossibility result as the ceiling. A protocol-theory reviewer reads that paragraph and relaxes.

**The honest-state ledger is the document's strongest discipline.** Distinguishing BUILT substrate from DESIGNED semantics, and flagging that *every L1 honesty claim is conditional on the unbuilt non-forgeable identity (ADR-0040)*, is precisely the right epistemic posture. In deontic terms: **you cannot have accountability without attributability.** A norm violation you cannot attribute to a principal is not a violation, it is noise. Naming identity as the keystone-below-the-keystone is the most important sentence in the dossier.

That is the steelman. It is a serious, well-read document. Now the attack.

## Gaps & inconsistencies (prioritized)

**1. (Critical, foundational) The deontic logic is named but never actually *done*. The dossier cites the canon and then commits the very fallacy the canon warns against.**
It invokes Jones & Sergot, Chisholm, deontic binding — but it treats obligations as if they compose monotonically, which is the classic deontic error. Standard Deontic Logic (SDL) is notoriously paradox-ridden: Ross's paradox, the Good Samaritan paradox, the gentle-murderer paradox, and — the one the dossier *names but does not resolve* — Chisholm's contrary-to-duty paradox. The dossier says "if you breach, you owe a salvage note" is a CTD structure (gap 11), which is correct, but then does nothing with it. CTD obligations are *exactly* what SDL cannot represent without contradiction — that is the whole point of Chisholm's paper. You need a defeasible or dyadic deontic logic (Hansson's dyadic O(A/B), or input/output logic à la Makinson & van der Torre 2000, or a non-monotonic / defeasible deontic system) to even *write down* "you ought not breach; but given that you have breached, you ought to file a salvage note." Citing Chisholm as a "gap" while using monotonic obligation-stacking everywhere else is using the field's concept as a label, not a tool. **This is the single biggest deontic-logic weakness: the paper claims a deontic layer but specifies no deontic *logic* — no semantics, no consequence relation, no treatment of obligation conflict or override.** The normative-BDI canon I just reviewed is explicit that adopted norms must compete through deliberation with consequence-ranking and minimax — there is *zero* conflict-resolution machinery in this dossier. What happens when an obligation and a prohibition bind the same action? The dossier has no answer. That is not a gap in the seed; it is a gap in the dossier itself.

**2. (Critical) "Permission → capability" silently collapses the regimentation/enforcement distinction the document elsewhere gets right.**
D1 maps permission to capability (a hard, regimented boundary — the jail in E). But deontic *permission* is not a capability. Permission is the dual of prohibition: P(a) ≡ ¬O(¬a). A capability is an *ability* (you can), permission is a *normative status* (you may). Conflating them means the system cannot represent the most ordinary normative situation in software: **"you are able to but not permitted to"** (you *can* force-push to main; you may not). The dossier's own canon — the guardrails-never-advertise-bypass rule, the `--allow-main-worktree` flag that exists but must not be used — is *precisely* a case of capability-present-permission-absent. By mapping permission to capability and capability to the jail, the dossier makes "able but forbidden" *inexpressible*, and then quietly relies on it everywhere. This is an internal inconsistency between the deontic model (D) and the enforcement model (E), and it is exactly the kind of misuse a deontic-logic reviewer is trained to catch.

**3. (High) The default-closure question (gap 12) is raised and abandoned — and it has a known wrong answer the dossier is drifting toward.** The dossier asks "default-permit or default-deny?" and says it must be stated. Good. But it then proposes a *tool*-level default-deny (the jail, E1) while leaving the *deontic/coordination* layer apparently default-permit (anything not Arbiter-forbidden is allowed). These are two different closure rules at two layers and the document never reconciles them. In deontic terms this is the difference between a *closed* normative system (von Wright: what is not permitted is forbidden) and an *open* one (what is not forbidden is permitted). A safe agent OS almost certainly wants closed-on-tools, open-on-speech-acts — but the dossier must *say* that and prove the two closures compose. Right now it has two implicit, unstated, possibly-contradictory closure rules.

**4. (High) Felicity conditions are correctly demanded (A1, gap 1) but the dossier doesn't notice they re-introduce the FIPA *semantic verification problem* — a known dead end it must explicitly route around.** FIPA SL's feasibility preconditions are defined over the *sender's mental state* (Bᵢ, the agent believes; ¬Iⱼ, the recipient does not already intend). Wooldridge's central critique of FIPA ACL (in the very textbook the dossier cites) is that **mental-state semantics are unverifiable**: you cannot, from outside, check whether an agent "believes" the precondition of its `inform`. The dossier wants felicity conditions *and* wants the daemon to record rational effect *and* wants loud-fail on insincerity (gap 14) — but you cannot loud-fail on a precondition you cannot observe. The way out is exactly what Singh (1998, "Agent communication languages: rethinking the principles") and the commitment-based-ACL tradition (Singh's social semantics, Fornara & Colombetti's commitment-based ACL) proposed: **ground the semantics in observable social commitments, not private mental states.** The dossier already *has* a commitment substrate (C) — so the obviously correct move is to give the performatives a *commitment-based operational semantics* rather than a FIPA mentalistic one. The dossier never connects A (performatives) to C (commitments) at the semantic level; it leaves them as separate families when Singh's tradition says they are the *same thing*. This is the most important missing synthesis in the document.

**5. (High) The seven protocol patterns are asserted to be FSMs (B1) but no liveness/safety properties are stated, which is the entire point of specifying a protocol formally.** Saying "Contract-Net is an FSM with terminal states" is necessary but not sufficient. A protocol-design reviewer wants the two standard property classes: **safety** (no agent reaches an inconsistent local state — e.g., no agent believes it won an award that was never made) and **liveness/progress** (every conversation reaches a terminal state — *deadlock-freedom* and *termination*). The dossier gestures at termination (good) but never mentions deadlock — and nested protocols (B2) are a *classic* deadlock source (parent waits on child commitment, child waits on parent capability grant). Hoare's CSP is cited; the natural follow-through is to actually state the protocols in a process calculus or as TLA+ and model-check deadlock-freedom. The dossier cites CSP as lineage but does no process-calculus work. Given this project ships ProVerif proofs already, the *expected* standard here is a TLA+ or CSP/FDR model of at least Contract-Net-with-nesting showing deadlock-freedom. Its absence is conspicuous.

**6. (Medium) The two-meanings-of-delegation-chain catch (F, gap 5) is excellent but the proposed resolution is hand-waved and may be unsound.** The dossier says F2 (loop-detection) "is carried inside" F1 (crypto chain). Fine — but loop detection requires comparing *task shapes* across hops (open-problem 2), and the crypto chain authenticates *hops*, not *task semantic equivalence*. Carrying F2 inside F1 buys you *authenticated* loop detection only if the task-shape field is part of the signed payload at each hop — which means every re-delegation must commit to a canonical task representation *before* knowing whether it loops. The dossier doesn't specify what is signed, so "F2 inside F1" is currently a slogan, not a mechanism. And open-problem 2 correctly notes the NO-KEYWORD-NLP constraint forbids substring task-matching — but then the task-shape equivalence relation must be embeddings-or-structural-hash, which is *not* deterministically signable (two semantically-equal tasks hash differently). There's an unacknowledged tension between "signed task shape" (F) and "semantic task equivalence" (open-problem 2). These two threads contradict and the dossier doesn't notice.

**7. (Medium) Decommitment penalty (C5) is treated as an L1→L3 hinge but its deontic status is wrong.** Sandholm's leveled-commitment contracts are correctly cited, but a *priced* decommitment is precisely the move from **regimentation to enforcement-with-sanction** — i.e., it converts a commitment from "must" to "may, at a price." That is the Jones & Sergot enforcement quadrant *exactly*. The dossier files it under "obligation layer C" and again under "L3 economy," but never says the structurally important thing: **adding a decommitment penalty changes the deontic operator from O (obligation) to a CTD-conditional (¬breach is ought; given breach, pay-penalty is ought).** So C5 and gap 11 (CTD) are *the same phenomenon* and the dossier treats them as unrelated entries in two different families. A deontic reviewer sees these collapse into one and will dock points for not seeing it.

**8. (Medium) "Loud-fail on protocol violation" (gap 14) needs a runtime-verification frame it never invokes.** Detecting "an unanswered `request` past `reply-by`," "a malformed performative," "a counterparty gone silent" against a protocol FSM *is* runtime verification of a temporal property — this is monitor synthesis from LTL/regex over the performative stream (Bauer, Leucker, Schallhart's three-valued LTL runtime verification; Havelund's work). The dossier specifies *what* to detect but not *that this is a monitor-synthesis problem* with a known theory (and a known hard part: the *anticipation* / true-false-unknown verdict for liveness properties you can't yet refute). Without that frame, "loud-fail on protocol violation" is a wish, not a spec.

## What an expert would add

- **A concrete deontic logic, named and bounded.** Pick one: defeasible deontic logic (Nute), dyadic deontic logic (Hansson/Lewis), or — best fit for this architecture — **input/output logic (Makinson & van der Torre)** which was built for exactly "given these conditional norms and these facts, what is obligatory?" State the consequence relation. State how O/F/P conflicts resolve (priority? specificity? consequence-ranking à la the normative-BDI canon's minimax?). Without this the "norm layer" is a label.
- **Commitment-based operational semantics for the performatives**, à la Singh 1998 and Fornara-Colombetti, replacing FIPA mentalism — and explicitly *unifying families A and C*. This is the synthesis that makes the whole layer verifiable and dissolves the unverifiability critique (#4). It is also the move that makes "loud-fail on insincerity" *possible*, because social-commitment violation is observable where mental-state violation is not.
- **The full deontic square / interdefinability constraints**: O(a) ≡ ¬P(¬a), F(a) ≡ ¬P(a), and the "ought-implies-can" bridge between the deontic layer (D) and the capability layer (E) — you may not be *obliged* to do what you are not *capable* of (Kant's principle, and it's load-bearing: the jail's capability set bounds the legitimate obligation set). The dossier has D and E as separate families; ought-implies-can is the *theorem connecting them* and it's absent.
- **Safety + liveness statements per protocol, and at least one model-checked composition.** Given the repo already does formal proofs (ProVerif), a TLA+ model of nested Contract-Net + supervisor-worker showing deadlock-freedom and termination is the bar. Cite **van der Aalst's workflow patterns** (already in the skill registry) for the composition/exception edges — soundness of a workflow net is the established notion of "this protocol can always complete," and it's exactly B1/B2's missing property.
- **Sybil / mechanism-design honesty (open-problem 7).** Contract-Net with self-interested bidders is an auction, and an auction without a strategyproof mechanism invites strategic mis-bidding even before Sybils. The dossier should cite Nisan et al. (already in the registry) and state plainly: *single-player local Contract-Net has no incentive-compatibility guarantee; bond-to-bid is a deposit, not a mechanism.* Right now open-problem 7 understates this — it frames Sybil as the threat when *strategic bidding by honest-identity agents* is the prior threat.
- **An explicit "ought-implies-can" failure mode**: what does the system do when an agent is *obliged* (commitment) to perform an action its *capability set* (jail) forbids? This is a guaranteed-to-occur conflict between C and E and the dossier has no handler for it.

## Verdict

**Not yet complete; not yet fully consistent; close to paper-ready as a *map*, far from paper-ready as a *specification of a deontic protocol*.**

As a **completionist survey of the idea-space**, it is strong — A–H is a defensible spanning tree, the prior-art table is well-chosen and correctly attributed, and the honest BUILT/DESIGNED ledger is exemplary. If the paper's claim is "here is the territory L1 must cover," it largely succeeds.

But the dossier's *title* promises a coordination *protocol* with *obligations, permissions, and loop/termination guarantees* — and judged as that, it has four disqualifying holes that a deontic-logic / protocol-design reviewer will not wave through:

1. **It names a deontic layer but specifies no deontic logic** — no semantics, no consequence relation, no conflict resolution. Chisholm/CTD is cited as a gap while monotonic obligation-stacking is used throughout. (Critique #1, #7.)
2. **It conflates permission with capability**, making "able but forbidden" — the most common real normative state, and one its own canon depends on — inexpressible, and creating an internal D-vs-E inconsistency. (#2.)
3. **It demands felicity conditions without confronting the FIPA unverifiability dead-end**, when its own commitment substrate hands it the Singh-style social-semantics escape it never takes. Families A and C should be unified and aren't. (#4.)
4. **It asserts protocols-as-FSMs without a single safety or liveness property**, never mentions deadlock (the canonical hazard of its own nesting proposal), and does no model-checking despite the repo already shipping formal proofs. (#5.)

**What must change before paper-ready:** (a) commit to a named, bounded deontic logic and show O/F/P conflict resolution + ought-implies-can bridging D and E; (b) give performatives a commitment-based operational semantics and explicitly merge families A and C; (c) fix permission≠capability and state both closure rules and prove they compose; (d) collapse CTD (gap 11) and decommitment-penalty (C5) into one enforcement-quadrant treatment; (e) state safety+liveness per protocol and model-check at least one nested composition for deadlock-freedom (TLA+ or CSP/FDR, matching the repo's existing proof bar); (f) reframe loud-fail as runtime monitor synthesis (Bauer/Leucker/Havelund) and Contract-Net as a mechanism-design problem (cite Nisan, state the no-strategyproofness honestly).

Do those six and this becomes a genuinely strong L1 chapter. Until then it is an excellent annotated map of a protocol that has not yet been given a logic.",
