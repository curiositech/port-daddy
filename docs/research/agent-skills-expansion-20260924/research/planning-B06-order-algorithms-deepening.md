## Primary sources actually checked

- R. P. Dilworth, “A Decomposition Theorem for Partially Ordered Sets,” *Annals of Mathematics* 51(1), 161–166 (1950), DOI [10.2307/1969503](https://doi.org/10.2307/1969503). The original paper body was read from the [UCSD-hosted scan](https://mathweb.ucsd.edu/~asuk/dilworth.pdf). Its §1 defines chains and independent sets (antichains); Theorem 1.1 states the decomposition result. For a finite poset, minimum disjoint chain partition size equals maximum antichain size. Dilworth proves the theorem more generally, but finite DAG reachability posets are the relevant case here.
- D. R. Fulkerson, “Note on Dilworth’s Decomposition Theorem for Partially Ordered Sets,” *Proceedings of the AMS* 7(4), 701–702 (1956), [full scan](https://resources.mpi-inf.mpg.de/departments/d1/teaching/ss11/graph_theory/DilworthFulkerson.pdf). The paper gives the bipartite-matching proof: make left and right copies of each poset element, and put an edge for each strict order relation. A maximum matching of size (m) yields a minimum chain partition of size (n-m); the relation must be poset comparability (reachability for a DAG), not only original direct arcs.
- Yangjun Chen, “Decomposing DAGs into Disjoint Chains,” DEXA 2007, LNCS 4653, 243–253, DOI [10.1007/978-3-540-74469-6_25](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-540-74469-6_25). I read the publisher’s bibliographic record and abstract, not the paywalled full body: it identifies the chain-decomposition method and reports an (O(n^2+bn\sqrt b))-style bound (publisher extraction formats the radical poorly), (O(bn)) storage and (O(\log b)) reachability query. Treat those as the abstract’s claims, not independently checked theorem proofs. “Chen stratification” is an insufficient citation by itself: give author, title, year, version and the exact algorithm relied on.
- Yangjun Chen, “Directed Acyclic Graphs and Disjoint Chains,” ICEIS 2009, 17–24, [publisher PDF](https://www.scitepress.org/PublishedPapers/2009/18583/18583.pdf), [publisher landing/PDF index](https://www.scitepress.org/PublishedPapers/2009/18583/pdf/index.html). The primary PDF text was searchable and exposed the relevant body sections. It says the *Y. Chen and Y. Chen (2008)* algorithm can fail to return a minimum set in some cases, then presents a revised algorithm using DAG stratification, chain generation, and virtual-node resolution with a combined alternating graph, still bounded by (O(n^2+bn\sqrt b)). Do not silently equate that 2008 target with Chen’s distinct 2007 DEXA paper; the exact version/relationship needs source attribution in any retained method claim.
- Giorgos Kritikakis and Ioannis G. Tollis, “Fast Reachability Using DAG Decomposition,” SEA 2023, DOI [10.4230/LIPIcs.SEA.2023.2](https://doi.org/10.4230/LIPIcs.SEA.2023.2), [full primary paper](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/opus/volltexte/2023/18352/pdf/LIPIcs-SEA-2023-2.pdf), plus its [2022 full version](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.03945). I read the full 17-page published paper. Useful sections: §1 definitions and objectives; §2 decomposition heuristics and graph-generator setup; §3 transitive-edge bound/filter; §4 indexing and Alg. 1; §5 experimental results; Appendix A.1 proof of indexing correctness.
- The fast paper’s algorithm assumes a static DAG and a supplied vertex-disjoint path/chain decomposition. It sorts adjacency lists by topological order, then constructs arrays with one entry per chain per vertex. A query takes the target’s chain/position and checks whether the source array’s lowest reachable position for that chain is at or before the target’s position. The body gives (O(|E_{tr}|+k_c|E_{red}|)) indexing construction and (O(k_c|V|)) space; the query is (O(1)) after construction in its stated RAM/array model. Sorting is (O(|V|+|E|)); the chain-decomposition method is separately (O(|E|+c\ell)) for its cited heuristic. End-to-end cost therefore includes the selected decomposition and sorting. If adjacency lists are not sorted, the paper says the relevant indexing work becomes (O(k_c|E|)), rather than (O(k_c|E_{red}|)). Its conclusion/abstract sometimes abbreviates away the (+|E_{tr}|) scan term; use the detailed Theorem 5/§4 bound.
- The paper’s linear-time filter finds only a large subset (E'_{tr}\subseteq E_{tr}). Its complement (E'_{red}=E-E'_{tr}) is a *superset* of the true non-transitive edges. This retains the same transitive closure but is not guaranteed to be the exact transitive reduction. Full transitive reduction, its edge-filter heuristic, the chain index, and explicit transitive closure are distinct objects.
- Experiments use generated ER, BA, WS and path-based DAG families (the 2023 published tables show ER/BA/WS/PB), 5,000/10,000 nodes, average degrees 5–160, edges oriented from lower to higher node IDs, and a laptop with Intel i5-6200U and 8 GB. Tables 3–4 measure transitive-edge fractions ranging from 0.11–0.99 across configurations. The authors’ qualified prose says “almost all” tested 5k/10k models at average degree *above* 20 have >85% transitive edges, and some denser cases exceed 95%. It is not a 85–95% universal interval: table entries reach .98/.99 and sparse examples are much lower.
- The paper says the heuristic decomposition is “very close” to optimum and plots/counts generated examples; I found no universal 5–10% optimality guarantee in the body. The claimed 5–10% in the skill is unsupported as a general claim. Keep exact table/algorithm/graph-family measurements if wanted, and state the denominator and scope.
- “10–20x compression” is not established as general storage compression. Even if fraction (p) of a raw edge list is removed, the conditional edge-list ratio is (1/(1-p)) (e.g. 85%→6.7x; 95%→20x), but this does not describe the paper’s partial filter in every case, index-array storage (O(k_c|V|)), or a guaranteed exact reduction. O(1) query is supported only after preprocessing and while the indexed static DAG/decomposition remains valid. The authors do not present dynamic chain/index maintenance; detecting a transitive edge does not imply O(1) maintenance after arbitrary graph change.

## Hand-checkable examples for a repaired entrypoint/reference

**Width is not workers or makespan.** Take (A\to C, B\to C, C\to D), with task durations (A=5,B=20,C=1,D=1). Under the DAG’s reachability order, ({A,C,D}) and ({B}) are two disjoint chains. ({A,B}) is an antichain of size two, so this cover and witness prove width (=2). They prove the minimum *reachability-chain partition* size, not the minimum workers or schedule length. With two identical workers, A and B start at time 0, then C runs 20–21, D 21–22: makespan 22. With one worker the same precedence graph takes 27 units; durations/capacity determine the schedule. A worker may also execute multiple ordered chains sequentially. This is a constructed illustration, not a paper measurement.

**Reachability chain cover is not direct-edge vertex-disjoint path cover.** Take (a\to x, x\to b, c\to x, x\to d). The reachability poset has width 2 (antichain ({a,c}); a matching two-chain partition is ({a,x,b},{c,d}), where c reaches d through x even though x is in the other chain). A vertex-disjoint direct-edge path cover needs 3 paths: a path can use x only once, and the bipartite graph on direct arcs has maximum matching 2, hence (5-2=3) path components. This example catches a common direct-edge-versus-closure error.

For an exact chain partition via Fulkerson, construct bipartite copies (L,R) of every vertex and include (u_Lv_R) iff (u\prec v) in the strict reachability order (a path exists from u to v). A maximum matching with (m) edges maps to a chain partition with (n-m) chains. Replacing that relation with original arcs computes the vertex-disjoint *path* cover objective instead. Neither objective minimizes weighted makespan.
