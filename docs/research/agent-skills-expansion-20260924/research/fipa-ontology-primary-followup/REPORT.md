# FIPA Ontology Service primary-source recovery and method map

Research date: 2026-09-24

## Recovery result and version boundary

The canonical FIPA catalog is reachable at <https://www.fipa.org/specs/fipa00086/index.html>. It identifies XC00086D as the 2001-08-15 Experimental revision (document version D). This corrects the task note's 2001-08-10 date. The catalog's XC00086D HTML link now redirects to a frozen-redirect page and fails (502); the D PDF endpoint fails parsing in the browser reader. The prior CiteSeerX PDF retrieval record at `../fipa-ontology-primary-retrieval/retrieval.json` reports 404. These attempts did not yield D body text.

The same FIPA catalog exposes XC00086C, the complete 2000-06-15 Experimental predecessor as direct FIPA HTML: <https://www.fipa.org/specs/fipa00086/XC00086C.html>. I opened the FIPA HTML body and read the relevant sections: scope, service/reference model, all six ontology relationship definitions and general properties, directory registration/query, ontology/meta-ontology actions, translation request/result, exception behavior, shared-ontology interaction protocol, and the informative annexes on conceptualization/integration. The browser extraction reports 2,620 total lines. This is primary FIPA text, but it is version C, not a byte-level recovery of D.

A primary implementer paper by Suguri et al., “Implementation of FIPA Ontology Service” (CEUR-WS Vol. 52, <https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-52/oas01-suguri.pdf>), states that FIPA 98 Spec 12 and the then-latest Experimental ontology-service specification had only cosmetic differences and “no functional change” (paper pp. 2–3; extracted lines 104–109 and 185–190). That provides contemporaneous author/implementer support to use XC00086C for method recovery of the later D body, while retaining an explicit version caveat. It does not prove byte identity or replace an exact D citation. The same paper describes a concrete implementation, but implementation behavior must not be mistaken for normative requirements.

The FIPA 98 Part 12 catalog page, <https://www.fipa.org/specs/fipa00006/>, lists its later A revision as obsolete; the linked body/PDF fetch failed. XC00086C is the better stable equivalent here because the FIPA host serves its full body and its contemporaneous implementers explicitly compare it to the later experimental ontology-service specification.

## What the primary text actually specifies

- The OA scope is an agent-communication interface for explicit, declaratively represented ontologies. Potential services include discovery, maintenance, expression translation, relationship queries, and shared-ontology selection. The specification explicitly says that not every OA must implement every task, but every OA must be able to participate in the conversation, including saying it cannot perform a task. It does not require every agent platform to contain an OA. (XC00086C §1; §§2–3.)
- The storage/representation language of the ontology is not prescribed. FIPA standardizes how agents communicate about ontologies, using the FIPA meta-ontology/OKBC knowledge model for that interface; backend server protocols and internals are outside scope and left to developers. (XC00086C §§1–3, 5.)
- Ontology identifiers are logical names maintained by the OA and used in ACL; they abstract the physical ontology/server name. OA services and ontology/translation capabilities can be advertised through the Directory Facilitator, but the DF does not validate the advertised service. Non-OA agents may also advertise translation capabilities. (XC00086C §§3.2, 3.4.)
- The relationship predicate is directional where stated. The six labels are Extension, Identical, Equivalent, Weakly-Translatable, Strongly-Translatable, and Approx-Translatable. Extension preserves symbols and properties from the base ontology; Identical means vocabulary, axiomatization, and representation language are physically identical; Equivalent means same vocabulary and logical axiomatization possibly in different representation languages; Strong translation is total vocabulary translation, source axiomatization preserved, no information loss, and no inconsistency; Weak translation may lose information but should not introduce inconsistency; Approx translation may lose information and introduce inconsistency. (XC00086C §3.3.1–3.3.6.) FIPA also cautions that even identical ontologies may commit to different conceptualizations (footnote 3 in §3.3.2), and equivalent ontologies served by different systems may yield different deductions (footnote 4 in §3.3.3). Thus the labels do not certify shared intent or identical server behavior.
- The relationships do not form the simple strict chain sometimes drawn in the skill: stated implications are Strongly-Translatable ⇒ Weakly-Translatable ⇒ Approx-Translatable; Equivalent implies strong translation both ways; Identical implies Equivalent. Extension is not positioned as another rung in that implication chain. Determining relationships can be computationally difficult or undecidable at sufficient expressive power and may require manual intervention (footnote in §3.3). FIPA also cautions that even identical ontologies may commit to different conceptualizations (footnote 3 in §3.3.2), and equivalent ontologies served by different systems may yield different deductions (footnote 4 in §3.3.3). Thus these labels do not certify shared intent or identical server behavior.
- Translation must be checked against the directed source/target relationship before requesting it. `translate` returns a result and is used with FIPA-Request. An OA unable to provide a translation can return `nil`; common `not-understood`, `failure`, and `refuse` exceptions are available, including `READ-ONLY` and `INCONSISTENT` for modification refusal. (XC00086C §§5.2.6–5.2.7.)
- `assert`, `retract`, query acts, and `atomic-sequence` provide the ontology operation vocabulary. The primary text specifies consistency-aware refusals for assert/retract. Atomic modify sequences are required to be atomic, consistent, isolated from intervening actions, durable, and rolled back to the pre-operation state if a component fails; lock mechanism is implementation-dependent. (XC00086C §§5.2.2–5.2.5.)
- The shared-ontology protocol presents choices: use a directly supported ontology; find a provider supporting a shared ontology via DF; query a relationship and use identical/equivalent or a sub-ontology relationship; or use a translation service as a proxy. The examples are options, not a mandatory global negotiation protocol. (XC00086C §5.3.)
- Annex A (informative) distinguishes conceptualization, ontology, and knowledge base; formalizes ontologies as approximations of intended models; warns that syntactic/model overlap may not imply compatible intended models; and discusses top-level ontologies versus integration by local intersections. Because Annex A is explicitly informative, recommendations there are design guidance, not interface conformance requirements. (XC00086C §7, especially §§7.1–7.4.)

## Recommended method restoration

For any translation-dependent operation, record (1) exact source and target ontology identifiers and versions, (2) direction, (3) the declared relationship/capability and its authority/provenance, (4) the expression/result and protocol response, (5) a task-specific validation criterion, and (6) the application policy for known loss or possible inconsistency. This is a conservative implementation method inferred from the specification’s directionality and limited guarantees; it is not itself a FIPA-required receipt format.

Treat the six relationship values as declared information with the exact semantics in §3.3. Do not infer a relation from lexical overlap or from a translation service listing alone. For high-consequence applications, put confirmation, action gating, and domain validation in an explicitly named local safety policy; do not claim that FIPA defines those action classes.
