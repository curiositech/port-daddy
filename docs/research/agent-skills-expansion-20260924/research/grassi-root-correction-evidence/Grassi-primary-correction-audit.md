## Read primary source and exact method

Read the complete 152-scan-page public-release PDF [Grassi, *A Task Analysis of Pier Side Ship-Handling for Virtual Environment Ship-Handling Simulator Scenario Development* (Naval Postgraduate School, September 2000)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/c/cc/A_task_analysis_of_pier_side_ship-handling_for_virtual_environment_ship-handling_simulator_scenario_development_%28IA_taskanalysisofpi00gras%29.pdf). The body was accessible through the PDF text representation, not just search snippets. Page references below give the thesis's printed page and the PDF scan page (Pxx).

**GOMS model construction.** In IV.A–B, printed pp. 43–46 (scans P58–61), Grassi scopes two evolutions, reviews ship-handling publications and actual Commanding Officer's Standing Orders to identify phases, develops a GOMS-like model, and uses one experienced SWO to add detailed methods and rules of thumb; he iterates and updates the model through repeated reviews. The task is deliberately generic because ship type, weather, pier, and other conditions make exhaustive coverage infeasible. The scenario uses a twin-screw ship, empty pier, minimal wind/current; for underway, the harbor-pilot tug is optional, and for mooring, a tug rendezvouses with the ship and is also optional (IV.B.1, printed pp. 44–45, scans P59–60). This exact condition matters: the initial model assumed no tug would be available even though the validation scenario supplied an optional tug.

**CDM as described in Chapter II.** II.F.1, printed pp. 21–22 (scans P36–37), says CDM elicits knowledge in “three distinct phases”: (1) uninterrupted event/procedure recall, followed by interviewer retelling to establish shared understanding; (2) a second pass to establish context and identify decision points; (3) a third pass with probes, informational-cue questions, and “what if” questions. The uninterrupted-account claim belongs to this generic three-phase CDM description and is accurate. Do not replace this with a claim that all four later study steps are the canonical CDM phases.

**Grassi's validation protocol.** IV.C.3, printed pp. 48–50 (scans P63–65), says “The process consisted of three phases” and immediately enumerates **four**: `1) scenario familiarization, 2) initial task recount by participant, 3) probe questioning, and 4) model review and validation.` Preserve this source's count/list inconsistency explicitly instead of normalizing it. Operationally the text is clear: (1) participants received scenario facts before interview; (2) during the first session, each participant gave an uninterrupted account of both evolutions with a foam-core ship/pier model; (3) the interviewer stepped through phases again and probed cues and unclear procedure; this initial session lasted about 45–60 minutes, after which the account was formatted to resemble GOMS; (4) participants returned later and compared their account with the initial model; discrepancies were examined to distinguish opinion from model error. The tug discrepancy was identified in this final comparison (IV.C.3, printed pp. 48–50; scans P63–65).

Five participants were selected by defined criteria: experience with twin/multiple-screw ships, at least ten underway/mooring evolutions, and a recent operational sea tour (IV.C.1, printed p. 47; scan P62). They filled background questionnaires; a foam-core visual model helped them walk through/reset the scenario (IV.C.1–2, printed pp. 47–48; scans P62–63). The thesis does not document their being from different ships/commands/years in the detail claimed by one derivative essay.

**Tug discrepancy, exactly.** Grassi reports that the initial GOMS assumed no tugs available and included no tug procedures; all participants opted to use tugs. They all found the model inaccurate without tug procedures; all agreed that, due to severe ship-damage consequences, conducting any type of pier-side ship handling without tug assistance was very rare. The model was changed to include tug assistance (IV.C.3, printed p. 50; scan P65; PDF text lines around 1221–1231). This is clear primary evidence, but its scope is still a small, selected sample and a simple scenario. Chapter VI says the generic model “in no way depicts every possible method” (printed pp. 113–115, scans P128–131).

**CCI method and sample.** V.C, printed pp. 102–112 (scans P117–P126), lists cues for the two evolutions, explaining what was seen/heard and why each cue matters at a particular phase/subgoal. Table 6 (printed p. 106; scan P121) lists stern-water churning, stack smoke, engine acceleration sound, and Engine Order Telegraph acknowledgement for determining whether an engine order was executed. It is an inventory of cues in this model, not an independence/fusion/reliability study. Grassi's conclusion states GOMS alone could not efficiently describe what the officer observed and why; codifying the CCI alongside GOMS addressed that representational shortfall for the bounded task (VI.B, printed p. 115; scan P130–131). The CCI does not show every signal is independent, always available, jointly monitored, or a universal minimum sensor set.

## Corrected hand-check and bounded implication

Use this source-faithful trace when restoring method substance:

1. Set scenario: twin-screw ship; low wind/no current; empty pier; tug/pilot launch is present and use is optional.
2. Build an initial procedure model from relevant standing orders/publications and one experienced practitioner's methods/rules.
3. Give each selected validator the same scenario before interview; record the uninterrupted account of both evolutions; probe phases for cues; format account as GOMS; later walk through and compare with the initial model.
4. At the final comparison, record the discrepancy: initial no-tug model vs each of five validators choosing the optional tug. Ask whether this is a method difference or invalid assumption under the scenario; document the five-person agreement and stated consequence; revise the model to represent tug procedures.
5. Add a CCI entry at the affected method/decision, with cue, what it indicates, and how it is observed. Do not infer statistical independence or universal tug policy from the entry or sample.

This preserves rather than dilutes the useful evidence: a deliberately simplified assumption was directly challenged, every selected reviewer chose the available tug, and the author changed the model. The warranted inference is that the model needed this branch for the scenario and this expert sample; broader transfer needs its own domain assumptions and validation.
