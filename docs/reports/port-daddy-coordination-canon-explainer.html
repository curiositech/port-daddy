The Coordination Canon — old and new — and what we're baking into Port Daddy.

For forty years, computer scientists have been quietly figuring out how groups of independent agents can share work without stepping on each other. Almost none of that work has been ported onto today's LLM agents. This page walks the canon — Cohen-Levesque, Ostrom, Smith, Decker-Lesser, the FIPA standards, the actor model — alongside the newest 2024-2026 papers, and tells you, in plain English, which ideas are worth wiring into Port Daddy's substrate and which are not.

BAKE PARTIALLY BAKED REFERENCE-ONLY REJECT NET-NEW 2024–26
00

The five-bullet pitch.

If you read nothing else, read this.

  1. Five classic ideas are already half-baked into Port Daddy. Persistent goals, escrowed collateral, intention-as-filter, stigmergic pheromones, and runtime invariants are already in the codebase. The work left is wiring them to gates, not adding new theory.
  2. The biggest still-unbaked old idea is the negotiability index from Decker & Lesser's GPGP work — let a broken promise cost at least a notification to the people who were counting on it.
  3. The biggest worth-baking new idea is verifiable execution traces: when an agent says "I ran the tests," it leaves a signed record that the test output actually said so.
  4. We are saying NO to FIPA wire-format envelopes, full BDI plan libraries, Bayes-net belief layers, and CRDT shared state. Each one fights a problem an LLM-on-SQLite substrate doesn't have.
  5. Proof of "did this actually help?" is now a tractable thing to measure, because Port Daddy already keeps receipts on every session, every spawn, every promise. The right metric isn't task completion — it's hollow-compliance rate: how often a "done" promise wouldn't survive an honest re-audit.
01

The Canon — ten old ideas, one verdict each.

These ideas were invented between 1973 and 2003, mostly to solve problems with software systems that long predate LLMs. The surprise is how many are still load-bearing.

1987–1995 · BDI

Belief, Desire, Intention

Bratman 1987; Rao & Georgeff 1991, 1995 Partially Baked

Big idea An agent shouldn't re-think everything from scratch on every turn. Separate beliefs (what's true), desires (what you want), and intentions (what you've actually committed to do). Once you've intended something, you stop second-guessing — which is what makes a busy agent stay coherent.

Legacy BDI became the spine of every serious agent architecture for thirty years (AgentSpeak, Jason, JACK). It is the reason "agent" means something more than "Python script."

What it misses BDI was designed for agents that can't reason flexibly. LLMs can. Bolting a 1990s plan-library on top of GPT-class deliberation is doing the same work twice.

We keep the vocabulary — beliefs, desires, intentions — but throw out the old-school plan interpreter. lib/agent-telos.ts is already the desire half; lib/commitments.ts is already the intention half; lib/arbiter.ts already filters new claims against held intentions. The one thing left to wire is pre-warning the agent before it tries to do something that would break a promise it already made.
Cohen & Levesque's 1990 result — "intention is choice with commitment" — said a goal can only be dropped when achieved, believed impossible, or unmotivated. That maps one-to-one onto the three drop-conditions in commitments.ts. We are running a 1990 modal-logic paper as a SQLite schema.
2002 · FIPA

Speech Acts & Interaction Protocols

FIPA-00037, FIPA-00025 Reference + selective bake

Big idea Every message between two agents is a speech act: an inform, a request, a propose, a refuse. And every recurring conversation pattern (Request, Query, Contract-Net, Subscribe) deserves to be a named, reusable protocol.

Legacy FIPA is the standard that JADE, Jason, and most enterprise agent platforms still implement. It is the reason "agent middleware" was ever a category.

What it misses FIPA assumed the parser on each end was a Prolog interpreter with zero semantic flexibility. LLMs are that flexibility. Adding the speech-act envelope to every message is parse-and-route ceremony with no behavior change.

We don't make every Port Daddy message wear a FIPA tag. But we do stamp each channel with a protocol name — "this is a contract-net thread," "this is a subscribe thread." That gives us one cheap, deterministic check: did the agent send the move the protocol says it should at this step?
Lightweight protocol tagging earns us "Six-Sigma agent" style discipline (Huang et al. 2026) for the price of one nullable column.
1980 · Contract Net

Bid, Award, Report

Reid G. Smith, IEEE Trans. Computers Bake

Big idea Don't let agents fight over work. The manager announces a task; contractors submit bids; the manager awards a binding contract; the contractor reports. The moment of award is when intent becomes obligation.

Legacy Contract Net is the original distributed-task-allocation protocol. Every modern marketplace algorithm — Uber dispatch, ad auctions, distributed compute schedulers — has Contract Net DNA.

What it misses Smith never said what the bond on a binding contract should be, or what should happen when the contractor flakes.

Port Daddy's pd roadmap pop is already a one-sided Contract Net — whoever pops first gets the task. We promote it to the full four-phase version: announce → bid → award → report, with the winning bid escrowed as collateral. The agent that bid the tightest believable plan with the most skin in the game gets the work, and is now actually on the hook.
Combining Contract Net (Smith 1980), persistent goals (Cohen-Levesque 1990), and collateral bonds (Ostrom 1990, then 2010s mechanism design) gives you the substrate version of "a job worth doing is worth signing for."
1995 · GPGP / TAEMS

The Negotiability Index

Keith Decker & Victor Lesser Bake

Big idea A commitment isn't a binary "kept / broken" — it's a social constraint with a number attached: how negotiable is it, and who else is depending on it? Breaking a commitment is allowed, but only with notification, ranked by how much it will hurt.

Legacy GPGP/TAEMS is the most underrated paper in the canon. It quietly invented the idea that social cost is the cheapest form of enforcement.

What it misses Decker & Lesser assumed the notification channel existed and worked. Port Daddy's inbox is exactly that channel — it just hasn't been wired to commitment-breakage yet.

Right now, an agent can silently walk away from a claim it was holding. After this lands, walking away from a low-negotiability claim requires DM'ing the dependents first and waiting either for acknowledgment or a 30-minute timeout. You can still break the promise — but you have to pay the social cost of telling the people who were counting on you.
Two columns on the commitments table. ~30 lines of code. The cheapest possible piece of accountability theatre that is actually not theatre.
1988 · Bayes Nets

Belief Networks

Judea Pearl, Probabilistic Reasoning… Reject

Big idea Most beliefs are uncertain. Bayes nets give you a clean math for combining uncertain evidence into a probability of "this is true."

Legacy Pearl won the Turing Award for this. Belief networks underpin almost every probabilistic reasoning system from medical diagnosis to spam filters.

What it misses Bayes nets were the 1988 answer to "the LLM doesn't exist yet." The two places Port Daddy has uncertainty (are two agents working on the same thing? is this a real protocol violation?) are handled by an LLM with a confidence threshold, which is the right tool now.

We don't bake Bayes nets. The substrate stays deterministic; the LLM does the uncertainty. Adding Pearl on top is double-counting.
The concept — expected value = probability × utility — survives in how we score Contract Net bids. It just doesn't need to be a graphical model.
1990 · Commons

Ostrom's Eight Principles

Elinor Ostrom, Governing the Commons Partially Baked

Big idea A community can self-govern a shared resource — without privatizing it and without a central boss — if the rules satisfy eight design principles. The most relevant for software: graduated sanctions, monitors accountable to the monitored, and cheap conflict resolution.

Legacy Ostrom won the Nobel Prize in Economics for this. "Monitoring without sanctions is failure" is quoted verbatim in our lib/bonds.ts.

What it misses The full 8 are a checklist, not a substrate. Several principles are philosophy, not code.

Today, an agent that misbehaves either gets nothing or gets exiled — a single cliff. We're finishing the graduated sanction ladder: a polite warning, then a small slash on the bond, then reduced privileges, then exile — with a slow decay back to clean status if behavior improves. People who screw up once and learn shouldn't carry the scar forever.
The decay function (not a hard window) is the bit that resists Goodhart. Time alone shouldn't heal you — good behavior over time should.
1959–2025 · Stigmergy

Pheromone Coordination

Grassé 1959; Dorigo 1992; S-MADRL 2025 Bake

Big idea Ants don't talk to each other. They drop chemical traces on the ground, and the shape of the environment coordinates the colony. Drop a marker; others sense it; everybody adjusts. No direct messaging required.

Legacy Ant Colony Optimization is the algorithmic descendant. In 2025, S-MADRL brought stigmergy to LLM agents: virtual pheromones for decentralized emergent coordination.

What it misses Pure stigmergy is slow and indirect. It's an ambient signal, not a directive.

lib/pheromone.ts already fades markers; nothing meaningful drops them yet. We add a tiny vocabulary: friction.recent, failure.cluster, attention.hotspot. When a commit gets reverted, the file gets a "this is hot, treat with care" marker. The next agent to look at that file sees it in their briefing, not as a DM, but as here be dragons.
Stigmergy is the only coordination channel that scales without congestion. You don't need N-way DMs when the shape of the world tells you what's going on.
1973–1986 · Concurrency

Actor Model & CSP

Hewitt 1973, Agha 1986; Hoare 1978 Reference Only

Big idea Either don't share memory at all (actors talk only via async messages), or share memory only at strict synchronization points (CSP channels). Both eliminate huge classes of concurrency bugs by construction.

Legacy Erlang, Elixir, Akka, Go's goroutines, Rust's channels — every modern concurrency story descends from these two papers.

What it misses They are implementation guides, not behavioral primitives. They don't tell you what an agent should do, only how its messages should travel.

Port Daddy already operates like an actor system — the daemon holds all state, agents are addressable inboxes, channels are pub/sub. Importing the formal algebra would not change behavior. We do read Hoare when designing new IPC, so we can catch deadlocks before they ship.
CSP's deadlock-by-construction theorems still earn their keep as a design-time sanity check for things like the relay-harbor mesh and the tube-spawner router.
2023 · Generative Agents

Memory + Reflection

Park, O'Brien, Cai, Morris, Liang & Bernstein Partially Baked

Big idea LLM agents that live for days or weeks need three memory tiers: a raw observation stream, importance-weighted retrieval, and periodic reflection that synthesizes recent experience into higher-order beliefs.

Legacy Park's "Smallville" paper is the moment LLM agents stopped being a stunt and started being a research field. Every long-running agent system that came after — voice assistants, simulation NPCs, AutoGPT successors — copies this shape.

What it misses The original framing is social simulation. Coding fleets aren't socializing — they're producing artifacts.

The piece we steal is the periodic reflection. Every few hours, the daemon picks each actor's most important unreflected experiences and asks a cheap LLM to write a one-paragraph "what I learned." That paragraph becomes a higher-tier memory. Agents have a chronic problem of not pausing to reflect; move it out of their hands and onto a daemon clock.
Same shape as lib/obligation-monitor.ts — a daemon-driven sweep — but watching experience instead of promises.
2023 · Judging

LLM-as-a-Judge

Lianmin Zheng et al., NeurIPS 2023 Already Baked

Big idea Use one LLM to evaluate another's output. With careful prompting it agrees with humans about 80% of the time — cheap enough to be everywhere, accurate enough to be useful.

Legacy Every modern AI eval harness (MT-Bench, Chatbot Arena, MMLU variants) uses some form of LLM-as-judge.

What it misses Judges have no skin in the game. They produce a verdict and forget. They cannot catch their own systematic biases.

lib/coordination-judge.ts already does this for the ambiguous-middle "should I intervene?" calls. The missing piece is a sampled re-opener: take a random 5% of "this commitment is done" verdicts and re-run them with a second judge against the actual diff. That's the only thing that catches hollow compliance.
Every monitor introduces a proxy. Every proxy can be gamed. The escape from Goodhart's Law is to sample against the proxy, not to add more proxies.

The Newcomers — six ideas from 2024 to 2026.

The research that landed in the last eighteen months. Most of it didn't exist when we started Port Daddy. Some of it is gold.

2025–26 · Verifiable Execution

Cryptographically Attested Agent Actions

VET (Dec 2025); zkAgent (eprint 2026/199); AgenTEE (Apr 2026) Net-New · Bake (lightweight)

Big idea Every consequential thing an agent does leaves a cryptographically attestable trail. The three flavors: trusted hardware (TEEs), succinct proofs (zero-knowledge SNARKs over the inference itself), and notarized TLS transcripts.

What's new Phala Network, Marlin Oyster, Atoma Network and Flashbots have shipped TEE-attested inference in production over 2024–25. zkAgent (Feb 2026) is the first end-to-end ZK proof of an entire LLM inference run.

What's still hard Real zero-knowledge LLM proving is 10,000× to 100,000× slower than native inference. Not practical for anything latency-sensitive.

We bake the cheapest sibling: when an agent says "I closed this promise because the tests passed," the daemon captures the actual test output, hashes it, signs it with the agent's key, and appends it to an append-only ledger. You can't fake the evidence after the fact, and the ledger becomes the receipts your fleet's reputation rests on.
We're not building zkLLM. We're building the "please sign this delivery receipt" step that brick-and-mortar logistics has had since the 1800s. Surprisingly, the LLM-agent world has been operating without one.

2025 · Topology

MultiAgentBench & Silo-Bench

Zhu et al. (ACL 2025); Silo-Bench (2026) Net-New · Bake (the harness)

Big idea The shape of the conversation between agents matters more than the agents themselves. MultiAgentBench is the first paper to run controlled ablations across star, chain, tree, mesh, and group-discussion topologies.

What's new The headline result: a graph-mesh topology (everyone broadcasts to everyone, with selective subscriptions) beats hierarchy and chain on task score, planning efficiency, and token cost.

What's still hard Their benchmarks are synthetic. Real coding fleets may behave differently.

We don't immediately enforce a topology. We do build the measurement harness: a small test bench that replays the same fleet task under different communication shapes, then publishes the numbers. If graph-mesh wins on our actual workloads, we make it the default.
Building a benchmark before changing the architecture is the boring move that almost no one does. We will do it. The receipts will be in docs/reports/.
2025 · Self-evolving Skills

SAGE — Voyager With Reinforcement

Wang 2023 Voyager → SAGE (Dec 2025, rev. Mar 2026) Reference Only · for now

Big idea Voyager (2023) was the first LLM agent to grow its own skill library from experience. SAGE adds reinforcement learning on top: skills don't just get added on first success, they get refined through reward signal.

What's new On AppWorld, SAGE gets +8.9% completion with 26% fewer steps and 59% fewer tokens.

What's still hard RL needs a reward signal. Port Daddy's only reward right now is "the operator merged the PR" — way too sparse and slow.

We don't bake this yet. We do start recording skill accept-rate per use, so when we ever get denser feedback, we have years of telemetry waiting to be compiled. The fix is a 1990s reputation system: skills that ship working code earn trust, skills that get reverted lose it.
The honest answer to "can we ship SAGE?" is: not until we ship the reward source first. Operator thumbs-up/down on commits is the natural fit.

2025 · Budgets

Single Agents Often Beat Multi-Agent Swarms

arXiv:2604.02460 (Nov 2025); CoRL (arXiv:2511.02755) Net-New · Bake (a gate)

Big idea When you give a single LLM agent the same total thinking budget as a multi-agent swarm, the single agent often wins. Multi-agent overhead is real and largely unaccounted for.

What's new CoRL formalizes a knob: trade off performance against cost in a single controllable parameter.

What's still hard "Just spawn a sub-agent" is the default move and it is often the wrong one.

We add a budget-aware spawn gate. Before any new agent gets spawned, the daemon checks: would spending this budget on more thinking by the parent dominate spending it on a child? If yes, refuse with a coaching note. The agent can override if it really means it.
This is the rarest kind of optimization: it improves quality and reduces cost. The default-to-spawn instinct is a fleet's hidden tax.
2025 · S-MADRL

Stigmergic Multi-Agent Deep RL

arXiv:2510.03592 Net-New · Composes with §1.7

Big idea The 2025 reboot of stigmergy: train the pheromone deposit and read policies with reinforcement learning, then let coordination emerge without any explicit messaging.

What's new Demonstrates emergent coordination in LLM-agent populations using virtual pheromones — no central scheduler.

What's still hard RL again. We don't have the reward source yet.

We take the structural idea (deposit + read), skip the RL training, and write a deterministic policy by hand: "a reverted commit deposits failure.cluster on that file." Cheap, ships today, no learning needed.
Ant colonies don't use reinforcement learning. They use chemistry and time. Our equivalent is SQLite and an evaporation cron.
2025 · Conventions

Emergent Social Conventions in LLM Populations

Ashery et al., Science Advances 11(20), eadu9368 Reference + instrument

Big idea Drop a population of LLM agents into a shared environment and let them interact pairwise. They spontaneously develop shared conventions — a disorder-to-order transition, complete with symmetry breaking (the convention could've been anything; one of them just happens to win). Robust up to N=200 agents and W=26 alternatives.

What's new Published in Science Advances. This is the moment "emergent norms in AI" stopped being speculation and became a measurable physical phenomenon.

What's still hard The convention they converge on can be biased in ways no individual agent intended. Collective bias is a real failure mode.

We don't pre-impose coding conventions on the fleet. We do instrument: a convention-tracker records what test framework, what error style, what commit-message format each agent is choosing, and the operator sees divergence before it becomes a holy war.
Watch agents accidentally invent design patterns. Then decide if you like the one they chose.

04

How we'll prove it actually helped.

The standard benchmark question — "did the task complete?" — is the wrong primary metric for Port Daddy. Here's the better one.

Hollow-compliance rate

Primary metric

Of all the promises an agent marked "done," what fraction wouldn't have survived an honest re-audit of the evidence? Lower is better.

This is the metric that resists Goodhart's Law, because it samples against the proxy instead of measuring the proxy.

Negotiated-abandonment rate

Secondary

Of all the commitments that got abandoned, what fraction were preceded by a notification to the dependents? Higher is better.

Cheapest possible signal that the social-cost mechanism is working as designed.

Operator-override rate

Calibration

Of decisions the daemon made on its own — awards, refusals, judge calls — what fraction did the operator reverse? Lower is better, but not zero.

Zero overrides means the daemon isn't taking real risks. We want a daemon that occasionally bets and sometimes loses.

Topology-cost dominance

Bench

On a fixed task set, what's the token cost and wall-clock to first acceptable result, compared across coordination topologies?

Published weekly. If our defaults aren't winning, we change them.

Skill-library reputation drift

Per-skill

Per skill, accept-rate × usage over time. Skills that consistently produce reverted work should be deprecated, not silently kept.

Skills earn their place. Loud failures get retired.

Pheromone suppression efficacy

A/B

On matched-pair files (similar revert history), does the presence of an active failure.cluster pheromone reduce future revert rate?

Validates whether ambient signal actually changes behavior or just decorates.

05

Glossary — terms used above.

A quick reference. None of these require an AI background.

BDI
Belief / Desire / Intention. A 1990s framework for designing agents whose mental states are first-class data structures.
Commitment
A promise an agent has made, with explicit conditions for when it can be dropped (achieved, impossible, or unmotivated). In Port Daddy, it's a SQLite row that the daemon watches.
Contract Net
Distributed task-allocation protocol: announce → bid → award → report. The original distributed marketplace algorithm.
Goodhart's Law
"When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." Charles Goodhart, 1975. Every monitoring system has to design against this.
Negotiability index
A number between 0 and 1 saying how easily a commitment can be broken. Low means "this is load-bearing; tell people before you walk away."
Oracle
A trusted source of truth the agent cannot author. A passing test ID, a merged commit SHA, a satisfied invariant check. Used to close commitments honestly.
Pheromone
An ambient signal left in the environment that other agents can sense. Borrowed from ant colonies. In Port Daddy, it's a value in a metadata column that decays over time.
Stigmergy
Coordination through modifying the shared environment rather than messaging each other directly.
TEE
Trusted Execution Environment. Special hardware (Intel SGX, AMD SEV, Apple Secure Enclave) that can produce cryptographic proofs about what code ran inside it.
zkLLM / SNARK proof
A short cryptographic proof that an LLM inference ran honestly. Currently 10,000× to 100,000× slower than just running the LLM. Cool, not yet practical.
Runtime verification
Compiling a formal property (like "no two agents hold the same lock") into an online monitor that watches the live event stream. The Arbiter does this.
Ostrom's principles
Eight design rules Elinor Ostrom derived from centuries of successful shared-resource communities. Won her the Nobel in Economics. Applies word-for-word to agent fleets sharing a codebase.

The honest ceiling.

Everything above proves what was done, not whether it was right. A non-forgeable identity, a daemon-set clock, closure-against-oracle with signed evidence, graduated sanctions on collateral, and a sampled adversarial re-opener is the strongest accountability substrate the literature describes — and it still doesn't prove the work was good.

We pair it with adversarial QA. We never sell the ledger as proof of quality. Port Daddy is the building department, not the inspector general.
