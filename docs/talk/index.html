A Profit Incentive for
Solving Anything

Why agent economies are coming, why they need institutions before they need features, and the strange category-theory reason the whole thing might print money.

Erich Owens · Curiositech

Hi. I'm Erich. This is a talk about a deeply unglamorous piece of infrastructure — a permit office for software agents — and a fairly unhinged idea that sits on top of it. I'm going to start with a bug you've probably lived, end with a claim that sounds insane, and spend most of the middle earning the right to say the insane part out loud. The deal: I lead with the felt problem, not the math. Hold me to it. Twenty-five minutes. One runnable command at the end.

3:00 a.m.

Two agents. One file.

Both read auth.ts. Both think. Both write. The second write wins — and the merge looks clean.

Here's the war story. Agent A is halfway through refactoring auth.ts. Agent B — spawned by a different orchestrator forty seconds later — also decides auth.ts is where the work is. Neither knows the other exists. They both read, both think, both write. Second write wins. Agent A's hour is gone. And the part that actually hurts: the merge looks clean. Nothing red. So nobody notices until the token-refresh race condition it quietly reintroduced pages someone on a Tuesday. If you've run more than one agent at a time, you can feel this one in your teeth. Sit in that feeling for a second. That's the whole talk's center of gravity. The reflex is wrong

You can't test your way out
of a fight between agents.

The instinct is to write a smarter orchestrator. A tighter lock. A better prompt.

  • Tests check one agent's behavior.
  • A better prompt sharpens one agent's judgment.
  • A smarter orchestrator is still one orchestrator.
The collision isn't a bug. It's economics — two self-interested actors, no shared institution, one scarce resource.

Hobbes wrote the design doc in 1651: bellum omnium contra omnes, the war of all against all. Your swarm is in a state of nature.

So the reflex — and I've had it — is to fix the agent. Smarter orchestrator, tighter lock, better prompt. That reflex is wrong, and seeing why is the point of this talk. Every one of those fixes lives INSIDE one agent. But the collision lives BETWEEN agents. No amount of cleverness inside a single process fixes a problem in the gap between two processes that don't share an institution. This isn't a bug in the engineering sense. It's economics. Two self-interested actors, no shared sovereign, both reaching for the same scarce resource. Thomas Hobbes called that the war of all against all — in 1651. Your agent swarm is reliving the state of nature, four centuries late, at machine speed. Notice I have NOT shown you a lighthouse yet. We earn that.

No jargon

So put a permit office
between the agents.

A small local daemon every agent checks in with before it touches anything.

  • An agent announces who it is and what it's doing.
  • It claims the files it's about to edit.
  • The daemon hands back a yes — or a no.

It doesn't decide what you build. It enforces what can't be done.

one command

pd begin \
  --identity myapp:auth \
  --lifecycle durable \
  --purpose "refactor token refresh"

That's the whole on-ramp. Verified coordination means the daemon's "no" is not a heuristic — it's a guarantee.

Here's the fix, stated with zero jargon. Put a permit office between the agents. It's a small daemon running locally. Before an agent touches a file, it checks in: this is who I am, this is what I'm about to do. It claims the files. The daemon says yes, or it says no. Critically — the daemon does not decide what you build. It's not an architect. It only enforces what cannot be done: two agents cannot both hold a write claim on auth.ts. "Verified coordination" just means that "no" is not a best-effort heuristic that mostly works. It's a guarantee we can prove. We'll get to the proof. For now: pd begin. That's the on-ramp. One command.

Live · the collision, prevented

Watch the institution say no.

$ pd begin --identity myapp:auth --purpose "refactor token refresh" --lifecycle durable

# meanwhile, Agent B, forty seconds later:
$ pd session files claim src/auth.ts

Agent B never gets to clobber the file. There's nothing to merge, because there was never a second write.

Let's run it. [If live: actually run these. If not, walk the canned output.] pd begin — Agent A anchors a session. Then it claims src/auth.ts. Claim held. Now Agent B shows up forty seconds later, exactly like the war story, and reaches for the same file. And this time: denied. Held by myapp:auth since 03:00:12. That's it. That's the fix to the 3 a.m. story. Agent B never gets to write. There's no bad merge to catch later because there was never a second write to begin with. The race condition that pages someone on Tuesday? It never enters the timeline. Now — you should be slightly suspicious. "Denied" is just a string. Why should I trust it? That's the next slide.

The Leviathan

The coordinator's correctness
is not an opinion.

It is a proof.
THIS is the Hobbes slide — and notice it comes AFTER you felt the chaos AND saw the fix, not before. If I'd opened with a lighthouse it would have felt like tyranny. Now it feels like relief. Hobbes's answer to the war of all against all was the Leviathan: a sovereign everyone submits to voluntarily, because the alternative is worse. And the reasonable engineer objection is immediate — I don't want a sovereign in my infrastructure, sovereigns become tyrants. Correct. So we make the sovereign's correctness a proof instead of a promise. That "denied" string is backed by a formal guarantee, not a hope. The Leviathan here is not surveillance, not a bottleneck, not a tyrant. It's the harbor authority that turns a knife fight into clean lanes. Relief, not tyranny — and checkable. Three papers, one arc

One process → one machine → many machines.

I  Bonded Commons

Why a coordinator at all? Agents post bonds, leave an immutable evidence trail, under a conservation law: value is only posted, cleared, or refunded.

verified · TLA+

II  Anchor Protocol

How one agent proves who it is and exactly what it may do. Capability tokens that only ever attenuate — grant less than their parent.

verified · ProVerif · Kani

III  Federated Harbor

When no machine is root of authority for another. Capability transfer across the trust boundary; revocation that gossips with a named convergence bound.

verified · TLA+

The Leviathan doesn't decide what you build. It enforces what cannot be done — proven, all the way up.

The proof comes in three beams. Three papers, one arc: one process, then one machine, then many machines. Paper I, the Bonded Commons, asks the question everyone skips: why should there be a coordinator at all? It's the economics. Agents post bonds, leave an immutable evidence trail, and operate under a conservation law — value is never created or destroyed by surprise, only posted, cleared, or refunded. Checked in TLA+. Paper II, Anchor, is the cryptographic floor: how a single agent proves its identity and authority to the daemon. Capability tokens that attenuate — a token can only ever grant LESS than the token it came from. Verified with ProVerif against an unbounded- session Dolev–Yao attacker, and with Kani against the actual deployed Rust. Not "we tested it." We proved the class of attack cannot land. Paper III, the Federated Harbor: your laptop and mine, neither one root authority for the other. Capability transfer across the trust boundary, gossiping revocation with a named convergence bound. Also formally modeled. One unglamorous permit office, proven correct, all the way up. If that were the whole pitch it'd be a good pitch. It is not the whole pitch.

Now the part that sounds insane

Structure transports along a functor.

An olog is a formal object for an idea — a category modeling a domain. A functor is a formal object for an analogy between two ideas.

If you have a functor from idea X to idea Y, every theorem you proved about X is already true of Y. Not "probably." All of it. For free.
Here's a fact most working engineers have never been handed. There's a formal object for "an idea" — Spivak and Kent call it an olog, an ontology log: precisely, a category that models the concepts of some domain. A protein is an olog. A supply chain. A legal argument. A garment. And there's a formal object for "an analogy between two ideas": a functor, a structure- preserving map between ologs. This is what Gentner's cognitive theory of analogy was reaching for — analogy as mapping of relational structure, with the slack taken out. The kicker is a theorem, not a vibe: structure transports along a functor. Lawvere's functorial semantics; Goguen and Burstall's institution theory — "truth is invariant under change of notation"; and it's running software, Spivak and Wisnesky's CQL migrates structure across schemas today. If you have a functor from X to Y, every theorem about X is already true about Y. For free.

The arbitrage

Buy a solution cheap where it's known.
Sell it dear where nobody realized it applied.

A market for solving problems efficiently is, definitionally, a market that pays for exactly this.

Sit with the size of that. A functor between the olog of protein folding and the olog of textile manufacture would mean every result about one is a result about the other. The person who FINDS that functor is holding an arbitrage: buy the solution cheap in the domain where it's known, sell it dear in the domain where nobody realized it applied. And a market for solving problems efficiently is, by definition, a market that pays for exactly this. Ricardo's comparative advantage is the pricing law. Hayek showed the price system is itself a distributed computer for discovering who can do what cheapest. And we have hard evidence the incentive works — Lakhani ran an open prize contest against an in-house NIH team on a real computational-biology problem, and the market won, decisively. A marketplace for solutions is a profit incentive to discover analogies — which is a profit incentive to solve every problem, and every problem that rhymes with it. Okay. Deep breath. Because if I stopped here, I'd be doing the exact thing this talk exists to refuse.

The load-bearing caveat

Finding the functor is the problem.

true "Theorems transport along a functor."

false "Finding the functor is easy."

Almost every analogy you've ever made is not a functor. It's lossy. A Galois connection leaks in one direction; a span shares only a partial abstraction; only a true equivalence gives the dream in full.

AlphaFold's method — not its answer — transported to materials discovery. The functor was found by humans, at great cost, and carried the technique, not the solution. Expensive, partial, and worth a fortune because it's expensive and partial.

This is the slide that separates this from every breathless thread you've scrolled past. "Theorems transport along a functor" is true. "Finding the functor is easy" is false. Finding the functor is the WHOLE problem. Almost every analogy you've ever made is not a functor — it's lossy. Category theory has precise names for the near-misses. A Galois connection approximates in one direction and leaks in the other. A span shares only a partial abstraction. Only a true equivalence — the kind univalence makes "transport everything" literal — gives you the dream in full. "Protein folding to efficient garments" is almost certainly a leaky span, not a functor, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling poetry. The grounded version is real but humbler. AlphaFold cracked protein structure; the METHOD, not the artifact, transported to materials discovery — GNoME proposed hundreds of thousands of stable crystals. That functor was found by humans, at great cost, and it carried the technique, not the answer. That's what transport looks like in the wild: expensive, partial, and worth a fortune precisely because it's expensive and partial. So the arbitrage is real, and the naive version is a casino. The interesting question is: what makes it a market instead of a casino?

Market, not casino

The bond is the
missing microstructure.

A market in analogies fails like a market in lemons: the seller knows whether their functor preserves structure; the buyer doesn't. So make the proof the product.

  • A claim of analogy is a capability claim — Anchor makes it unforgeable.
  • The receipt is the good: a checkable witness that transport held.
  • Price by fidelity: equivalence > functor > Galois > span.
  • Bond it. If the audit fails, the collateral is slashed.
Here's the connection I didn't expect to find. A market in analogies is impossible for the same reason a market in lemons is hard: the seller knows whether their functor actually preserves structure, and the buyer doesn't. You can't sell transported solutions if every buyer has to re-derive the correspondence to check you didn't lie. That's not a market — it's a trust fall. This is exactly the problem the Bonded Commons already solves. Four moves. One: a claim of analogy is a capability claim — "I assert a structure-preserving map from X to Y" — and Anchor already makes such claims unforgeable and attenuable. Two: the proof obligation is the product. You don't sell "a solution from domain X," you sell a solution plus a checkable receipt — a CQL-style migration, a composition-preservation witness — that the transport kept the structure it claims. The receipt is the good. Three: you price by categorical fidelity — equivalence beats functor beats Galois connection beats bare span; the market grades its merchandise. Four: you bond it. Post collateral. If the receipt fails an audit, the bond is slashed, and the conservation law makes sure no value evaporated quietly. The same boring permit office that keeps two agents off auth.ts at 3 a.m. turns out to be the missing microstructure for an economy of transported ideas. Institutions, the whole way down — from the file to the fantasy.

So: a profit incentive for solving any problem. I believe it. But notice what had to be true to earn the sentence instead of shouting it. One — agents have to transact at all, identity and capability and settlement, without a knife fight. Papers I through III. Two — a claim that "this solution transports to your domain" has to be cheap to verify and expensive to fake. The bond and the receipt. Three — we have to be ruthlessly honest that the scarce, market-making thing is not the solution and not the marketplace; it's the verified functor, and those are hard-won. The caveat, kept load-bearing. Get those three and the fantasy stops being a fantasy and starts being plumbing. Ostrom spent a career showing commons don't need a tyrant or a tragedy — they need rules, monitoring, and graduated stakes. That's what we're building, for the strangest commons yet: the space of ideas, traded by machines, priced by how much structure they actually carry.
