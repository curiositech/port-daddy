\section{Introduction: the swarm is a state of \mbox{nature}}\label{sec:intro}
\subsection{A worked afternoon: Alice's checkout repair}\label{sec:mara}\label{sec:mira}
Return to Bob's checkout repair. Alice supervises Drake, Wren, Pike, Finch,
and Heron in her isolated worktree. The acceptance rule is concrete: retain the
original checkout-flow integration test, make the authorized repair pass it,
and leave unrelated migrations alone. The following timings and review budget
are a constructed teaching case, not a measured run.

\scene{Alice, 2:40 p.m.}{Five processes begin from revision~7. By 3:10 she has
five proposed changes. Her first question is which changes satisfy Bob's
request and which she has authority to accept.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em]
\item \textbf{Conflicting proposals.} Wren and Heron both change the payment
module from revision~7. Applying either proposal without checking the other's
base can discard useful work. A registered claim helps coordinate intent;
preventing the stale write also requires a mediated file-effect boundary.
\item \textbf{The unread pile.} Wren's genuine session-token repair sits inside
a $600$-line patch that also reverts an unrelated migration. Alice has ninety
uninterrupted seconds before the next review request. The correct fragment
does not make the whole patch acceptable.
\item \textbf{False completion.} Drake reports ``all green'' after deleting
the original failing test. The report confuses passing the remaining suite
with satisfying the preserved acceptance criterion. Heron's candidate
repair must also be checked against that original criterion.
\item \textbf{A stale destructive proposal.} Finch proposes a force-push
that would discard an unmerged contribution. Whether recovery is possible
or not, that effect lies outside the repair grant and needs a separate decision.
\end{enumerate}
\begin{pdexample}{Alice's Half Hour, Counted}
Thirty minutes yielded five proposed changes: one every six minutes. That
arrival rate alone would not overload her. Now state the inspection
budget. At an illustrative two lines per second, the $600$-line patch needs
$300$ seconds, while Alice's ninety-second interval covers only $180$ lines.
The shortage is uninterrupted inspection time under this assumed rate.

A digest is a short summary written for one reader, in which each
statement links to the evidence behind it. A useful digest points Alice to the
unrelated migration and the test deletion, then keeps the artifacts she needs
to check them. Shortening the description of either change cannot make it
acceptable. The next table separates these
failures because exclusion, evidence, and authorization solve different parts
of the afternoon. A coordinator adds a fifth possible failure: an unexplained
refusal whose grounds Alice cannot inspect.
\emph{Now you try:} match each failure to its required primitive in
Exercise~\ref{ex:ls-taxonomy-five-failures}.
\end{pdexample}

The chapter develops three decisions from this job: what Alice may delegate,
what evidence she must be able to inspect, and how to allocate limited attention.
All three serve legibility: the operator's ability to tell, in the
time she has, what the swarm did and why, and to check each statement against
its evidence. A summary supports \pdterm{zoom} when each of its statements
opens, in one step, to the artifact behind it: the diff, the test log, the
committed file.
Hobbes' question of a common authority supplies a comparison
\pdcite{hobbes1651}, used here without assuming that agents are people or that
a central daemon is the only answer. The proposed
authority must itself be inspectable and revocable;
Section~\ref{sec:consent-canon} tests the political analogy's limits.

\paragraph{Two routes through the argument.}
For \emph{supervision decisions}, follow Sections~\ref{sec:intro}--\ref{sec:operator}:
what Alice authorizes, which evidence a completion must carry, and when she
must inspect it. These sections develop the operating rules for the checkout
job; the later mathematics measures constraints on those rules.

For \emph{information bounds}, continue through
Sections~\ref{sec:readpoverty}--\ref{sec:tokens}: how much a summary must preserve,
how a list of agents is searched for candidates, and what is lost when a long
working context is cut down to a summary. A reader
starting there should first read the opening case and the verifiable-zoom
example, so that the formal error events retain their operational meaning.

\subsection{Why ``local''}\label{sec:local}
The local model has one trusted coordinating process and one durable store.
Clients ask that coordinator to order mutations and check grants. Authentication,
isolation, and mediation are still required for the effects it claims to control;
being on one machine does not remove those requirements. This chapter assumes
that local authority. Agreement between mutually distrustful operators is an
additional problem taken up in \pdchapref{fh}{The Federated Harbor}.

\section{Consent to the Leviathan (the normative claim)}\label{sec:authority}
\pdmarginfigure{hobbes}{Thomas Hobbes. This chapter borrows the question of authorized power, but makes the operator's consent scoped and revocable.}
% ═════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
Why should the operator give a daemon the authority to block an agent's
claim, reorder a merge, refuse a \texttt{done}, or raise an
escalation, a decision handed up to a human because no agent may
make it alone? Hobbes'
answer, transposed: the operator authorizes the sovereign because the covenant is
rational under the alternative. Crucially, in Hobbes the covenant is \emph{among the
subjects} (``a real unity of them all\ldots by covenant of every man with every
man''~\pdcite{hobbes1651,sep-hobbes}), not a bargain struck \emph{with} the
sovereign. In the software version, the operator does not bargain with each
agent. Instead the operator sets one rule of the road that binds every agent,
and the daemon enforces it. The rule is a \textbf{coordination protocol}, a
message protocol in which each message states its type. A typed message of
this kind is a \pdterm{performative}, a term from speech-act theory: a
request, a \textbf{commitment} (a promise tied to a named agent, which a
monitor watches for breach; \S\ref{sec:flatten}), a delegation, or a
termination. Because the type is stated, every message says what it intends,
who owns it, and when it ends.
The authority is asymmetric by design: the clients propose work, while one
coordinator applies the operator's policy to the effects within its boundary.
This is an assumption of one central coordinator; no consensus protocol
produces it. If a client
can bypass that boundary, or the coordinator cannot observe the decisive effect,
the prevention claim fails. Section~\ref{sec:landing} separates what can be
prevented from what can only be detected afterward.

\subsection{The legible-sovereign amendment}\label{sec:legible-sovereign}
Hobbes' absolutism needs one modern amendment, and it is the crux of this chapter.
Hobbes argued the sovereign, being the \emph{product} of the covenant rather than a
\emph{party} to it, can never breach it and need not be
inspectable~\pdcite{hobbes1651,sep-hobbes}. For a software authority this is exactly
wrong. An \emph{illegible authority} is the fifth state-of-nature failure mode
of Table~\ref{tab:taxonomy} wearing a crown, committed by the coordinator itself: if the daemon blocks an agent and the
operator cannot see why, the operator's trust erodes, and with it the consent that
legitimizes the authority.
\begin{pdclaim}{Design invariant}{The Legible-Sovereign Rule}
\setlength{\parskip}{4pt plus 1pt}
\label{prop:legible-sovereign}
The coordinating authority must be the \emph{most} legible actor in the system, not
the least.
Every act of authority (a claim denial, an anomaly detection, an
escalation, an ``all good'' attestation) must be a logged, named, zoomable event
whose reason the operator can reconstruct.
Consent is renewable only while the
sovereign is inspectable.
\end{pdclaim}
This is the inversion that rescues the design from despotism, and it is the move a
political theorist would press hardest (\S\ref{sec:consent-canon}). In Hobbes the
sovereign is opaque \emph{by structure}; here the sovereign is the one actor whose
every exercise of power is an audit record. It is realized as a discipline of
\pdterm{honest attestation}: a single self-report that runs every invariant check,
distinguishes \emph{verified-good} from \emph{no-evidence-of-bad}, regiments what it
can, and fails loudly about what it cannot. That discipline is precisely the
sovereign making its own judgments legible: ``all good'' becomes ``a claim with a
verifier, not vibes.''
\pitfall[Ranking can hide urgent work]{There is a regress hiding here, and Scott would press it. If every act of
authority must be legible, the \emph{legibility apparatus itself} is an exercise of
authority: the digest decides what is surfaced and what is buried. The ranker of the
Attention Queue, the ranked list of items the daemon puts in front of the operator for
review (\S\ref{sec:discovery}), is \emph{the} sovereign act: it decides what
the operator sees \emph{right now}. A ranker that silently buries an escalation is
governing opaquely. The rule is therefore not complete until the ranker's
\emph{omissions} are themselves legible, through a \pdterm{counter-surface}:
a view that lists what the queue did not show the operator, and why. Ranking is governing; we say so explicitly in
\S\ref{sec:consent-canon}.}
\begin{pdexample}{Three Omissions, and How Many of Them a Score Explains}
\setlength{\parskip}{4pt plus 1pt}
Take the three suppressed items tabulated below and try to audit the queue the cheap way, by score alone. Each item's score is its \pdterm{regret}, an estimate of the harm of leaving it unseen, and the queue shows an item of this kind of action (its action class) only if its regret reaches the distress threshold of $0.45$.
The first item, \texttt{agent-7}'s escalation, scores $0.31$; since $0.31<0.45$ it sits below the bar and the threshold explains it, and an auditor with nothing but the scores reconstructs that omission correctly.
The second scores $0.12$, which is also below $0.45$, so the score-only auditor thinks the threshold explains that one too, and is wrong: it was dropped because its recency weight decayed below the cutoff, and a rule that consults the clock rather than the score would have aged it out at any score at all.
The third is the one that should end the argument: it scores $0.58$, above the only threshold the screen names, and it was suppressed anyway, by de-duplication into an earlier occurrence, another rule that never looks at the number.
So of three omissions, a score-only audit explains one, misattributes one, and cannot see the third at all. The two it fumbles are the two where a rule other than ranking did the governing. That is the concrete content of ``ranking is governing'': the suppression \emph{rules} are what have to be legible, because the suppression \emph{scores} do not determine them.

\subsection{Consent as a first-class, revocable primitive}\label{sec:consent}
It is easy to say the operator ``consents to cede decision authority.'' A political
theorist asks first \emph{consented how, to what, and revocable when?}, and the
answer decides whether the Leviathan frame has any content.
``The operator just starts using the tool'' is
not Hobbesian consent. It is \pdterm{tacit consent}, consent inferred from
someone merely staying put without any act of agreeing, and
\textbf{Hume} (\emph{Of the Original Contract}, 1748~\pdcite{hume1748})
demolished it two and a half centuries ago: the peasant who ``consents'' by
remaining in the country has no real alternative, so the consent does no
normative work. We therefore make consent a concrete object.
\scene{Alice, 3:25 p.m.}{Alice grants two hours of low-stakes, reversible
formatting changes in \texttt{tests/}, provided the original acceptance test
remains intact. A formatting change can satisfy all these conditions. Drake's
test deletion fails the preserved-test condition; Wren's migration change is
outside the subtree; Finch's force-push is outside the grant. The fact that a
process can propose them gives it no permission to execute them.}

The tuple below records the conditions that distinguished those four proposals.
Its scope must include the acceptance-preservation rule as well as a path pattern.

\begin{pdclaim}{Design invariant}{Explicit, Scoped, Revocable Consent}
\setlength{\parskip}{4pt plus 1pt}
\label{def:consent}
A \pdterm{consent grant} is an explicit, scoped, revocable authorization the
operator issues to the daemon, of the form
\[
\begin{aligned}
g = \langle\, &\textsc{scope},\ \textsc{stakes-ceiling}\ s_{\max},\ \textsc{min-reversibility}\ v_{\min},\\
&\textsc{ttl},\ \textsc{revocable}\,\rangle,
\end{aligned}
\]
where \textsc{ttl} is how long the grant stays in force. For Alice's grant, the daemon may apply reversible formatting changes under
stakes threshold $s_{\max}$ in \texttt{tests/}, preserving the original checkout
acceptance test, for two hours. This grant permits neither unrelated changes
nor deployment.

A grant is a
first-class, legible object: it appears in the digest, it has an audit trail, and it
expires.

The authority's legitimate action set at any moment is exactly the union of
its active grants.
\end{pdclaim}

Consent so defined gives the design something Hume's critique cannot reach: a
discrete authorization event, a bounded scope, and an exit. It is also an object
supervisory-control research named long before this chapter did: a
\pdterm{level of automation}, meaning how much of a task the machine may do
without asking, in Sheridan's ten-point sense and in the
four-stage refinement (acquire, analyse, decide, act) that Parasuraman,
Sheridan and Wickens later gave it~\pdcite{sheridan1992,parasuraman2000}. Read
that way, a grant's $\langle\textsc{scope}, s_{\max}, v_{\min},
\textsc{ttl}\rangle$ is a level-of-automation setting chosen per action class
rather than once for the whole system, which is roughly what that literature
has been recommending since 1978. This section built the same selector from a
Hobbesian covenant, and that two routes so far apart arrive at a compatible
object is some evidence the object is the right one. It also hands the
accompanying chapters a concrete object. A \emph{hosted-trust} offering
transfers a grant: its promise, ``we make this fleet legible to you, under this
scope'', sells a consent grant to a third party, and
\pdchapref{he}{The Harbor Economy} sets the price of that sale. For that reason
\S\ref{sec:bridge} lists consent among the things this layer provides upward.

\begin{protocol}{Consent-grant lifecycle}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt]
\item \textbf{Issue.} The operator issues a grant
$g=\langle\textsc{scope},s_{\max},v_{\min},\textsc{ttl}\rangle$. The grant is
appended to the audit log as a named, zoomable event and surfaced in the digest.
\item \textbf{Act.} On a candidate action $x$, the daemon allows the action without
asking \emph{iff} $x$ is in scope \textbf{and} $\mathrm{stakes}(x)\le s_{\max}$
\textbf{and} $\mathrm{reversibility}(x)\ge v_{\min}$ \textbf{and} the grant is
unexpired and unrevoked; otherwise it escalates to the operator.
\item \textbf{Witness.} Every action taken under $g$ records $(g,\,x,\,$reason$,\,$
artifact-link$)$. The legible-sovereign rule (Invariant~\ref{prop:legible-sovereign})
binds here: the act is reconstructable.
\item \textbf{Expire / revoke.} On TTL or operator revocation, $g$ leaves the active
set; the daemon's legitimate action set shrinks accordingly. The override
(Invariant~\ref{prop:override}) revokes future use of all grants and stops
not-yet-committed acts at the mediation boundary. Already committed effects
require separate recovery.
\end{enumerate}
\end{protocol}

decision renews $g$; only expiry or revocation removes it from the active set.

\begin{pdclaim}{Design invariant}{The Inalienable Operator-Override}
\setlength{\parskip}{4pt plus 1pt}
\label{prop:override}
Hobbes grants the subject exactly one inalienable right, self-preservation: you
cannot covenant away the right to resist when your \emph{life} is at
stake~\pdcite{hobbes1651}.

The computational analog is an always-available,
unconditional operator-override: a \emph{stop now / I withdraw authority} channel
that \emph{no autonomy level can suppress}.

If the system reserves a highest-priority
\emph{distress} lane for the daemon to interrupt the operator, the override is its
dual, the operator-to-daemon channel, and it is a \emph{right}, not a feature:
it overrides grants and decisions before their effects commit at the
mediated boundary; it cannot undo an already committed effect.
\end{pdclaim}

\ifpdmargincolumn\pdconsentregion\fi
Computer security arrived at the same object independently. To a capability
theorist, a consent grant is a \textbf{capability with contextual caveats}: a
token that cannot be forged and that can only be narrowed, never widened, as
it is handed on, each holder adding restrictions (caveats) that depend on
context. Macaroons are credentials built this way~\pdcite{macaroons2014}.
Revoking one before it expires needs one more mechanism, in which the checker
consults a revocation list or an expiry rule. That political theory and
security engineering reach the same object independently is modest evidence
that the object is right, and not an artifact of the Hobbes metaphor.

This pairs with \textbf{Hirschman}'s \emph{Exit, Voice, and Loyalty}
(1970~\pdcite{hirschman1970}: \emph{the choice between leaving (exit) and
complaining (voice) as the two levers a member has over a declining organization}):
the design has rich \emph{voice} machinery (escalation, annotation, the attention
queue) and the override supplies the missing \emph{exit}. Exit is the discipline that keeps
voice honest; an operator who cannot cheaply withdraw a grant has no real leverage
over the sovereign.

A referee that only prevents collisions would be a glorified lockfile. Legibility
lets the operator inspect the work it coordinates. \textbf{James~C.\ Scott}'s
\emph{Seeing Like a State} (1998)~\pdcite{scott1998} describes how cadastral maps,
standardized surnames, the metric grid and scientific forestry make populations
and resources countable. His warning concerns treating that simplified view as a
replacement for local practical knowledge, \pdterm[metis]{m\^etis}: the hands-on,
case-specific know-how the system depends on. In his account of the German
\emph{Normalbaum} (``scientific forest''), same-age, single-species rows thrived
for a generation, then collapsed after the simplification had removed soil fungi,
undergrowth and species diversity~\pdcite{scott1998}. Rao's \emph{A Big Little Idea
Called Legibility} (2010)~\pdcite{rao2010} carries this critique into software
systems that flatten reality to one purpose.

In agent oversight, the operator is the state, the swarm the population, and the
dashboard the cadastral map. A grid of green tiles invites the operator to
\emph{govern the map}. The software failure is a \pdterm[potemkin digest]{Potemkin digest}, a
digest whose lines assert a state nobody can check, because the summary has
discarded the diff, test output, error and reasoning behind the work. The operator admires the
yield table while the forest dies.

\begin{pdclaim}{Design invariant}{The One Law: Digest-with-Zoom}
\setlength{\parskip}{4pt plus 1pt}
\label{def:zoom}
Every summary is a lens onto the real artifact, never a replacement for it.
Summarize the \textbf{state}; link to the \textbf{work}.

A digest you cannot zoom is
the kind of map Scott warns about, a simplified picture used in place of the thing it shows. A digest that reaches the underlying artifact preserves
access to recorded evidence: the operator can descend from abstraction and check
the claim. Tacit practitioner knowledge, or m\^etis, is a further requirement;
not all of it can be recorded.

Formally, a read-surface is a pair $(\sigma, \zeta)$ where $\sigma$ is a
projection (the summary) and $\zeta$ is a total \emph{zoom} function mapping every
summarized claim to a verifiable artifact; a surface with a partial or absent
$\zeta$ is a Potemkin digest.
\end{pdclaim}

\input{figures/legible-swarm-zoom-vs-potemkin}

Figure~\ref{fig:zoom-vs-potemkin} puts the two digests side by side: one that zooms
to a verifiable artifact and one that only asserts. Three requirements combine into
this one principle: summaries must be indexes, not
replacements; a reported result must be a checked result, not an asserted one; and
every summary must reach the artifact it summarizes. This chapter supplies the
\emph{why} (Scott plus the human-factors argument of \S\ref{sec:operator}) and the
\emph{how} (the rules below). Digest-with-zoom is the unifying statement of all three.

\subsection{What to flatten, what to preserve}\label{sec:flatten}

Scott's own distinction is the design boundary. The state may safely standardize
\emph{administrative facts it itself defines}, and must \emph{never} standardize away
the \emph{local knowledge it depends on}.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em]
\item \textbf{Flatten (safe: the operator's own structured field):} identifiers,
\textbf{claim} rows, session phases, port numbers, \textbf{commitment} status (a
\emph{commitment} being a violable obligation bound to a named actor, watched by a
breach monitor), enumerated states. Administrative legibility is cheap and lossless
here.
\item \textbf{Preserve verbatim, never paraphrase (the agents' m\^etis / the ground
truth):} the diff, the test output, the error message, the reasoning trace, the
exact command run. Paraphrasing these is the over-flattening.
\end{itemize}

The slogan: \emph{summarize the STATE, link to the WORK}.

\pitfall[Artifacts miss what cannot be written down]{``Zoom to the artifact'' does not fully preserve m\^etis, and a political
theorist will say so (\S\ref{sec:consent-canon}). M\^etis in Scott is not ``the
detailed data underneath the summary''; it is practitioner knowledge that
\emph{resists codification entirely}: the operator's gut sense that ``this agent is
about to do something dumb,'' the local knowledge of which corners of the codebase
are cursed. A read-path to the diff adds legibility and leaves m\^etis where it was.
The limitation, which links Scott to Bainbridge (\S\ref{sec:operator}), is that
\emph{some of the most valuable operator knowledge is exactly what the system cannot
make legible, and the more the supervising automation takes over, the faster that
m\^etis atrophies}. What organizational-knowledge theory calls the problem of
converting \pdterm{tacit knowledge} into explicit, transferable form (``we know
more than we can tell''~\pdcite{polanyi1966}) remains an open problem for
artifact-based oversight. Required skill-retention practice (\S\ref{sec:abdication}) is the design's
only defense, and it is the hardest part of the design to engineer.}

\subsection{The zoom target must be verifiable, not self-reported}\label{sec:verifiable}

There is a subtle trap inside ``zoom to the real thing.'' If the operator zooms to
the \emph{agent's chain-of-thought} and treats that as ground truth, the map has
merely moved down one level, because the reasoning trace may be fiction.
\textbf{Turpin et al.\ 2023}, \emph{Language Models Don't Always Say What They Think:
Unfaithful Explanations in Chain-of-Thought Prompting} (NeurIPS)~\pdcite{turpin2023}
shows that \emph{LLM-generated reasoning traces can be systematically unfaithful: plausible,
coherent, and not actually what drove the model's output (a model swayed by
reordering of options will rationalize a post-hoc reason and never mention the
bias).} Therefore:

\pdmarginanalogy{ls-map-and-lens}{Zoom must reach the evidence}{map-and-lens}{A lens enlarges the same map. A digest must instead link to independently checkable artifacts, not magnified self-report.}

\begin{pdclaim}{Design invariant}{Verifiable-Zoom}
\setlength{\parskip}{4pt plus 1pt}
\label{prop:verifiable-zoom}
The canonical zoom target is an artifact the operator can check
\emph{independently of the agent}: the diff on disk, the test result, the
committed file.

The agent's self-narration is a \emph{secondary} lens, always
labeled as such.

Two LLMs agreeing (an agent acting and an LLM summarizing it) is
not by itself evidence of two independent checks; it may be one error reported twice.
\end{pdclaim}

This is why the source of truth must be the durable records, the coordination
database and the version-control working tree, and not a model's summary of them.
The digest is computed \emph{over} artifacts the operator owns. This motivates
an independently controlled review or evaluation mechanism. An adversarial
reviewer is one option; a protected mechanical evaluator can also satisfy the
requirement. When a swarm reviews its own work, nothing yet shows that its
checks fail independently. Agreement can reproduce a shared error; the relevant
question is which evidence and failure modes the checks actually share. We
return to adversarial review as one such mechanism in
\S\ref{sec:authority-rules}.

\subsection{The roadmap as a versioned statement of intent}\label{sec:roadmap}

A coordinator that prevents collisions answers ``what is the swarm doing?'' It does
not answer ``is the swarm doing the \emph{right} thing?'' That question needs a
single legible artifact the authority is measured against, and the roadmap is
that artifact. It is not truth about the
world; calling it that would turn every lesson the work teaches into drift. The
authority's legitimacy is measured by \emph{whether the roadmap stays honest}: every
unit of work keys to a plan item, amendments are recorded as amendments, and drift
between plan and reality is itself a legible, surfaced anomaly rather than a fact to
be hidden.

When the plan changes, some evidence gathered earlier stops counting. Each
piece of evidence, a receipt (for example, ``test $X$ passed on commit $Y$ on
runner $Z$''), is tied to the inputs it checked.
Figure~\ref{fig:ls-plan-revision} proposes a rule for each kind of change. If
the source changes, earlier compile and acceptance results are stale. If the
acceptance policy changes, compile results may be reused but acceptance must be
rerun. If the authority is revoked, earlier results may still be valid
evidence, but nothing may be published. In the first two cases publication
must be rechecked. A result is reused only when every input it names still
matches; reuse is never automatic. Deleting a plan item does not undo an
outside effect whose outcome is unknown; that effect must be reconciled
separately. The figure illustrates a proposal; it is neither implemented nor
measured.

\subsection{Adversarial review: the quality check}\label{sec:review}

A swarm reviewing its own work can repeat the same error. Agreement alone does
not supply the independently checkable evidence required by
Invariant~\ref{prop:verifiable-zoom}. The proposed quality check is therefore
\emph{adversarial and conflict-free}: a reviewer with no stake in the work, running a
bounded critique--refine protocol. This is the single-operator tool's local analog of the
\emph{neutral evaluators} (the independent rating agencies) that \pdchapref{stp}{From Spawn to Person} and \pdchapref{he}{The Harbor Economy}
federate across operators: the same conflict-free-judge discipline, scoped
first to one operator and then opened to a market.

Independence of stake and independence of error are different premises.
Figure~\ref{fig:judge-dependence} gives a constructed counterexample: three
conflict-free reviewers can inspect one flawed source and repeat the same
mistake. Measure dependence on labeled cases before treating agreement as
corroboration; reviewers who collude as a group require a separate strategic
test.

\subsection{One agent per critical role, and the specialization boundary}\label{sec:sole-roles}

The single-writer discipline (\S\ref{sec:local}) generalizes naturally from data files to system functions. When an invariant is critical, the authority assigns a \pdterm{sole-responsibility role}: one named agent identity (an avatar) that alone may write the state an invariant protects.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt]
\item \emph{Roadmap Owner:} The single avatar authorized to commit mutations to the phase plan.
\item \emph{Test Suite Curator:} The sole agent authorized to land modifications to regression suites.
\item \emph{Release Avatar:} The sole gatekeeper authorized to execute deployment tags.
\end{itemize}

Assigning a role that way is a trade, and the trade has a number. Think of a bank:
one expert teller (the sole specialist) working a single line in strict order,
versus several ordinary tellers (the pooled generalists) sharing one line. The
expert is faster per request but has no colleagues to absorb a burst; the pool is
slower per request but almost never leaves the line standing idle behind a long
job. \textbf{$M/M/1$}/\textbf{$M/M/c$} (\emph{the standard queueing-theory
shorthand (\textbf{Kendall 1953}~\pdcite{kendall1953}) for a single-server versus
$c$-server queue with Poisson arrivals, exponential service times, and one waiting
line served first-come-first-served}) is the textbook way to compute the trade-off.

For a first calculation, let requests arrive at one per hour. Two generalists, each serving one request per hour, have utilization $1/2$ and delay probability $1/3$. Their mean response time is $1+(1/3)/(2-1)=4/3$ hours. A specialist serving $1.75$ requests per hour has the same response time, $1/(1.75-1)=4/3$ hours. A rate of $2$ wins; a rate of $1.5$ loses. The boundary below generalizes this comparison. Both arrangements must already satisfy the required safety rules: a latency calculation cannot authorize an unsafe pool.

Fix the \textbf{queueing-specialization setup}: requests for an invariant-critical
function $F$ arrive as a Poisson stream at rate $\lambda_F$; a sole specialist
serves them as an $M/M/1$ queue at rate $\mu_{\mathrm{spec}}$, the pooled
alternative as an $M/M/c$ queue of generalists at rate $\mu_{\mathrm{pool}}$ each
with utilization $\rho=\lambda_F/(c\,\mu_{\mathrm{pool}})<1$ and
first-come-first-served exponential service. Assume $\lambda_F>0$,
$\mu_{\mathrm{spec}}>\lambda_F$, $c_{\mathrm{wait}}>0$, and $A\ge0$, so both queues are
stable and the comparison is defined. The objective is \emph{mean} cost: $c_{\mathrm{wait}}$
is cost per request-hour and $A$ is the accountability benefit per hour of
operating the sole-owner arrangement. Let
$C(c,\rho)$ be the \textbf{Erlang-C} delay probability (\textbf{Erlang
1917}~\pdcite{erlang1917}: \emph{the probability that an arriving request finds
all $c$ servers busy and must wait, the quantity that makes a pool a pool}).

\begin{pdclaim}{Theorem}{Queueing Specialization Boundary}
\setlength{\parskip}{4pt plus 1pt}
\label{thm:specialization}
Under the queueing-specialization setup, sole ownership weakly dominates the pool
if and only if the skill advantage clears
\begin{equation}\label{eq:specboundary}
\frac{\mu_{\mathrm{spec}}}{\mu_{\mathrm{pool}}} \;\ge\; \gamma_A(\rho,c)
\;=\; c\rho \;+\; \frac{1}{\,1 + \dfrac{C(c,\rho)}{c(1-\rho)}
      + \dfrac{A\,\mu_{\mathrm{pool}}}{c_{\mathrm{wait}}\,\lambda_F}\,}.
\end{equation}
\end{pdclaim}

\noindent\Verified. A tempting shortcut is
$\tilde\gamma = 1+(c-1)\lambda_F/(c\mu_{\mathrm{pool}}-\lambda_F)
= 1+(c-1)\rho/(1-\rho)$, from a naive
$M/M/1$-versus-$M/M/c$ response-time comparison. A sweep against simulation
\emph{falsifies} that shortcut in both directions. Equation~\ref{eq:specboundary}
is the exact boundary: Example~\ref{ex:queue-boundary} derives it from two
textbook queueing facts, and it was checked numerically over two
thousand $(\rho,c)$ points. Keeping the shortcut beside the exact result exposes
its failure: the two thresholds cross exactly once, so the shortcut errs on the
unsafe side as well as the safe one. Figure~\ref{fig:specialization} shades both error regions.

At $A=0$ (response time only, with no value placed on accountability) the boundary
reduces to
\begin{equation}\label{eq:specboundary0}
\gamma(\rho,c) \;=\; c\rho \;+\; \frac{c(1-\rho)}{c(1-\rho)+C(c,\rho)}.
\end{equation}
Three properties fix its shape: $\gamma(\rho,1)=1$ (a specialist against a single
generalist needs no skill advantage at all), $\gamma\to c$ as $\rho\to1$ (the boundary
\emph{saturates} at the capacity ratio; it never diverges), and for two generalists
it is the closed form $\gamma(\rho,2)=1+2\rho-\rho^2$. Below the boundary, sole
ownership gives strict single-writer accountability at the cost of longer waits;
Eq.~\ref{eq:specboundary} states that cost explicitly, so it can be budgeted
instead of hidden.

\subsubsection{The succession corner: when the sole owner fails}\label{sec:succession-corner}

Eq.~\ref{eq:specboundary} assumes a specialist who never stops. The corner the
whole discipline exists for is the one where they do: they crash, are reaped,
quit, or are reassigned, and a successor spins up. That case has a
closed-form answer, derived in \pdchapref{he}{The Harbor Economy} rather than
here because it is a condition on what a succession plan costs, an economic
statement. The economy chapter states it as its succession-threshold theorem%
\ifpdbook
\ (Thm.~\ref{he:thm:succession-price})%
\fi
, with its own verification and its own scope. Two of its consequences belong in
this chapter's argument, because they change what a sole-responsibility role
means rather than what it costs.

The first is that skill has a ceiling here. With death at rate $\xi$ and
succession at rate $\eta$, the sole owner's mean response time decreases in
skill but never below
\[
W_\infty=\frac{\xi}{\eta(\xi+\eta)},
\]
the residual downtime a request meets when it arrives during an outage. No
skill advantage removes it, so past a point the honest fix for a slow sole role is
a better handoff and not a better owner. The second is that the ceiling can
bind absolutely: writing $K=W_{\mathrm{pool}}+A/(c_{\mathrm{wait}}\lambda_F)$ for the
response-time threshold in hours,
pooling wins at \emph{every} skill advantage once $\xi/\eta$, the owner's failure
rate over its replacement rate, clears the \pdterm{succession threshold}
$D^\star=\eta K/(1-\eta K)$, the ratio above which no amount of skill makes up
for the outages. That threshold exists only while $\eta K<1$, the condition
worth remembering. Where succession is fast enough that
$\eta K\ge 1$ there is no such threshold at all and sole ownership cannot be
ruled out on response time, which is the more common situation in a
well-run fleet and the one a reader is likeliest to be in.

\begin{pdexample}{Fast and Slow Succession for the Roadmap Owner}
\setlength{\parskip}{4pt plus 1pt}
This is a constructed example. Reuse the roadmap-owner queue worked above:
mutations arrive at $2$/hr and the pool is three generalists at $1.2$/hr each.
With $A=0$ the threshold is $K=W_{\mathrm{pool}}=1.021$ hours.

Fast succession: the owner crashes about every ten hours ($\xi=0.1$/hr) and a
successor is working within thirty minutes ($\eta=2$/hr). Then
\[
W_\infty=\frac{0.1}{2\times2.1}\approx0.024\text{ hr},
\]
about 1.4 minutes, and $\eta K=2.04\ge1$, so no failure rate rules sole
ownership out.

Slow succession: a successor takes two hours to start ($\eta=0.5$/hr). Now
$\eta K=0.51<1$ and
\[
D^\star=\frac{0.51}{0.49}\approx1.04,
\]
so pooling wins at every skill advantage once $\xi/\eta>1.04$, that is once
$\xi>0.52$/hr, a crash about every two hours. At $\xi=1$/hr,
\[
W_\infty=\frac{1}{0.5\times1.5}\approx1.33\text{ hr}>1.021\text{ hr},
\]
and no specialist, however fast, beats the pool on response time.
\end{pdexample}

\pdthesis{Both statements rest on a modelling choice the economy chapter
declares and this one inherits: the specialist can die \emph{at any time},
not only while serving. Under active-only breakdowns a fast enough specialist
has no such lower limit and $D^\star$ does not exist. The choice is right
for an owner who is reassigned or dead rather than worn out, and it is a
choice, not a derivation.}

\subsubsection{What heavy tails do to the boundary}\label{sec:spec-heavy-tails}

\ifpdmargincolumn\pdheavytailsketch\fi
Exponential service is the assumption a queueing reader will challenge first, and
they are right to: a rebuild or a flaky integration test can produce a long turn,
and submissions may arrive in correlated bursts. Whether a particular workload
has heavy tails or Poisson arrivals must be measured. Kingman's heavy-traffic result \pdcite{kingman1961} says what
that costs in general: waiting grows with the sum of the squared coefficients of
variation of the arrival and service processes, so a queue that is fine at
$c_s^2=1$ can be badly congested at $c_s^2=6$ with the same utilization. The
worry is real. What is not obvious, and matters here, is that it lands on
\emph{both} sides of Eq.~\ref{eq:specboundary} at once.

Service time and waiting time must be kept separate. Put
$\nu=\lambda_F/\mu_{\mathrm{pool}}=c\rho$,
$r_{\mathrm{sk}}=\mu_{\mathrm{spec}}/\mu_{\mathrm{pool}}>\nu$, and
$\kappa_j=(1+c_{s,j}^2)/2$ for each service distribution. With Poisson arrivals
and finite second moments, Pollaczek--Khinchine gives the specialist's mean
\emph{sojourn} time exactly, while a variability-scaled Erlang-C approximation
\pdcite{allen1990} gives the pool's:
\begin{align}
 \mu_{\mathrm{pool}} W_{\mathrm{spec}}
   &= \frac{1}{r_{\mathrm{sk}}}+\kappa_{\mathrm{spec}}\frac{\nu}{r_{\mathrm{sk}}(r_{\mathrm{sk}}-\nu)},\\
 \mu_{\mathrm{pool}} W_{\mathrm{pool}}
   &\approx 1+\kappa_{\mathrm{pool}}
       \frac{C(c,\rho)}{c(1-\rho)}.
\end{align}
Only the second term in each expression is waiting. Multiplying the whole
sojourn time by $\kappa$ would also multiply service time, which variance at a
fixed mean does not do. The comparison is therefore
$\mu_{\mathrm{pool}}W_{\mathrm{spec}}\le
 \mu_{\mathrm{pool}}W_{\mathrm{pool}}+A\mu_{\mathrm{pool}}/(c_{\mathrm{wait}}\lambda_F)$;
it cannot in general be reduced to replacing $A$ by $A/\kappa$.

\begin{pdexample}{A Variance-Sensitive Specialization Boundary}
\setlength{\parskip}{4pt plus 1pt}
\label{hyp:spec-variance}
The expressions above recover Eq.~\ref{eq:specboundary} when both
$\kappa_j=1$. Away from exponential service, the pool expression is an
approximation, not an error bound.

Even equal variability on both sides can move
the crossing when $A=0$: for $c=2$, $\rho=1/2$, and $C=1/3$, the exponential
\mbox{threshold} is $r_{\mathrm{sk}}=1.75$; setting both $\kappa_j=2$ gives an approximate threshold
\[
r_{\mathrm{sk}}=(8+\sqrt{124})/10\approx1.914.
\]
\end{pdexample}

These are analytic comparisons; no agent workload was measured. A useful
test would extend \texttt{b8\_specialization.py} with matched-mean lognormal and
Pareto service, report simulation uncertainty, and measure the approximation's
crossing error. That sweep has not been run. Infinite service variance makes the
stable $M/G/1$ mean waiting time infinite, so this finite-mean comparison no longer
applies. Correlated or bursty arrivals also leave the Poisson model, and their
delay distribution and tail latency need a separate analysis.

\subsection{Completionist obligation: completion as a verifier, not a vibe}\label{sec:completionist}

The operator's standing complaint is the agent that \emph{declares completion while
the work is hollow}: ``fixed the test by deleting it.'' The completionist
obligation proposes an authority-side rule against accepting an unmet or
unevaluable completion predicate.

For the checkout job, Drake's claim ``all green'' fails before any score is
computed: the original test is missing. Heron's candidate instead needs a receipt
binding that unchanged test, the tested commit, and the delivered commit.
A passing receipt for another tree cannot close this obligation. The completion
predicate below makes those task-specific requirements the gate.

\begin{pdclaim}{Design invariant}{Completionist Completion}
\setlength{\parskip}{4pt plus 1pt}
\label{def:completionist}
A \pdterm{completion claim} is a commitment whose satisfaction predicate is a
\emph{verifier} derived from the task's acceptance criteria. The authority refuses to
accept the claim against an unmet predicate.

An \emph{unverifiable} predicate must
\textbf{fail loudly}, surfacing ``I cannot verify this completion'', and never
silently pass.

This is the authority-side analog of discovery's
\emph{refuse-to-route} (\S\ref{sec:gates}): ``I don't know'' must beat a confident
wrong answer.
\end{pdclaim}

\input{figures/legible-swarm-completion-verifier}

Here the completion predicate is a postcondition, in Meyer's design-by-contract
sense.\pdcite{meyer1992}

\pitfall[Verification also consumes attention]{Verification has a \emph{cost gradient}, not a binary. A test going green is
cheap to verify; ``the architecture is right'' is expensive, and reading a 600-line
diff to confirm an acceptance criterion is itself attention-seconds that may exceed
the agent's narration cost by an order of magnitude (the \emph{verification
asymmetry}). Invariant~\ref{def:completionist}'s fail-loud is the least an honest
system can do with the \emph{unverifiable}; \S\ref{sec:operator} supplies the cost model for the
\emph{expensive-but-possible} middle.}

\subsection{Thoughtful landing and the regimentation/enforcement split}\label{sec:landing}

The authority decides \emph{what lands and in what order}, conflict-aware, and it
enforces those decisions through the footgun guards. What a guard can and cannot do has to be stated
exactly, because that is what separates a safety claim that holds from one
that is wishful.

% SUBMISSION-SKILL: naming conflict --- "regimentation" (used in this section's
% title and below as "regiments what it can") is an established term of art in
% normative multi-agent systems for structural, inline prevention of
% norm-violating behavior (via mediation or hard-wiring), contrasted there with
% monitoring/sanctioning enforcement -- the same prevention-vs.-monitoring split
% this invariant draws. The sense is compatible, not contradictory, so this reads
% as independent convergent naming rather than an error; a human should decide
% whether to cite the NorMAS regimentation literature (e.g. Aldewereld, Grossi,
% Boella \& van der Torre) to adopt the term formally, gloss the borrowed sense
% inline, or keep the current informal usage since the paper's governing name
% for this idea is already "physically unreachable" / "detect-and-compensate",
% not "regimentation" itself.
\begin{pdclaim}{Design invariant}{Prevention vs.\ Detection: the Reserved-Phrase Rule}
\setlength{\parskip}{4pt plus 1pt}
\label{prop:regiment}
A coordination state is \textbf{physically unreachable}, that is, prevented, \emph{only}
when a constraint forbids it \emph{inline}, before the act commits: a database
uniqueness constraint that forbids two holders of the same registered key, or a fail-closed startup
gate that refuses to run against the wrong target.

A monitor that \emph{subscribes to
the log of actions already taken} is something weaker and must be named formally:
\mbox{\textbf{detect-and-compensate}}. It observes a violation \emph{after} the fact and
can halt or repair within a bounded window only if observation delay, response authority, and repair completion have explicit bounds. Such a downstream monitor
cannot prevent a violation it has already observed, because the violating act is
already in the record. It can still mediate later actions; that is a separate enforcement boundary.

The phrase ``physically
unreachable'' is therefore reserved for the constraint-enforced set and used nowhere else.

This is an invariant on the chapter's \emph{vocabulary} and on which name each
mechanism class may carry. The result it leans on, which says what an inline
monitor can prevent at all, is Schneider's, cited in the paragraph that follows,
and is his rather than ours.
\end{pdclaim}

\textbf{Fred B.\ Schneider} (\emph{Enforceable Security Policies},
2000~\pdcite{schneider2000}: \emph{an execution monitor sees each step of a run
before it takes effect and may halt the run; every policy such a monitor can
enforce is a safety property}) proved the result this invariant needs. A safety
property says that some bad thing never happens, so any violation shows up in a
finite prefix of the run, and an inline monitor enforces it by halting before the
bad step executes. A liveness property, such as ``every repair eventually
completes'', has no such prefix, and no execution monitor can enforce it. The
converse fails: not every safety property can be enforced by a monitor.
\textbf{Hamlen, Morrisett and Schneider} (\emph{Computability Classes for
Enforcement Mechanisms}, 2006~\pdcite{hamlen2006}) showed that Schneider's
original conditions give only an upper bound on what execution monitors enforce,
and characterized the smaller class they actually do. Neither theorem concerns a
monitor placed after a commit. That such a monitor cannot undo the committed
violation follows from the definitions. Detection and compensation need their
own stated response-time assumptions. A post-commit anomaly detector, however useful, is
therefore detect-and-compensate, not prevention. Honesty about this distinction is
essential: a system that advertises detection as prevention has lied about its own
safety envelope, which is the very failure (the confident-wrong report) this chapter is
built to eliminate.

\subsection{Human-in-the-loop escalation as a typed, prioritized interrupt}\label{sec:escalation}

The one thing that needs a human is an \emph{escalation} (a distress signal) routed
to the operator on a reserved, highest-priority lane. The mechanism wants the
critical signal to win the operator's attention. \emph{When} the daemon
stops and asks, instead of proceeding, is a control problem (\S\ref{sec:operator}), and the
\emph{escalation predicate} needs a rationalized alarm
philosophy (\textbf{ANSI/ISA-18.2}, \emph{Management of Alarm
Systems}~\pdcite{isa182}: \emph{the process-control standard for alarm
rationalization: every alarm has a defined priority, set-point, and operator
response, with explicit flood-suppression}). Frequent unhelpful alerts risk
habituation and ignored warnings. Cvach's clinical review~\pdcite{cvach2012}
reports studies in which $80$--$99\%$ of alarms were false or clinically
insignificant, and discusses desensitization. That clinical evidence motivates
measuring alert burden and response in agent supervision; the rate and
timescale of those effects in an agent distress lane are unmeasured. Spence's signaling model~\pdcite{spence1973} motivates examining how an
escalation cost could separate signal types. Here the proposed debit follows an
adjudicated false escalation, not a bare dismissal (\S\ref{sec:gates}). Whether
it discourages manipulation without suppressing valid warnings depends on the
validation rule and its errors. The model below abstracts that rule as an
upholding probability and takes the validator's correctness as given.

The next result gives an agent's best response to a fixed validation rule: a
cut-off signal strength above which it escalates. The script
\texttt{b7\_escalation\_band.py} checks the worked model's threshold and
feasible debit range.

\begin{pdexample}{The Threshold and the Tuning Band, by Hand}
\setlength{\parskip}{4pt plus 1pt}
Suppose signal $u$ gives benefit $u$ and is upheld with probability $u$. An
unupheld escalation costs $\theta w$. Take the linear case $U(u)=V(u)=u$. Setting
$u-\theta w(1-u)=0$ gives $u^\star=\theta w/(1+\theta w)$.
At $\theta=0.05,w=1$:\marginnote{\input{figures/spark-escalation-threshold}\par
\emph{Escalation threshold:} $u^\star(\theta)=\tfrac{\theta w}{1+\theta w}$ ($w=1$).}%
\[
u^\star=\theta w/(1+\theta w)=0.05/1.05=0.0476
\]
[verified, closed form]. This is the fraction of the validation signal $u$ above which escalating clears its cost, one of twelve $(\theta,w)$ points the script confirms against bisection to machine precision.

Now let a missed bad item cost $C_{\mathrm{miss}}=100$, an inspection $c_{\mathrm{att}}=1$, and a needless interruption $C_{\mathrm{fa}}=5$. The operator wants an escalation once its probability of being genuine reaches the inspection threshold for these costs, $u_{\mathrm{crit}}\approx0.057$, which Example~\ref{ex:inspection-bar} works out and Theorem~\ref{thm:regret-head} derives. Assume signals $u$ are spread uniformly on $[0,1]$ and $V(u)=u$ is that probability. In the theorem below, an escalation at $u$ costs $c_{\mathrm{esc}}(u)=c_{\mathrm{att}}+C_{\mathrm{fa}}(1-u)$, attention plus the chance of a needless interruption, and a silence costs $c_{\mathrm{sil}}(u)=C_{\mathrm{miss}}$ when $u\ge u_{\mathrm{crit}}$ and nothing below it. \]

The theorem separates the monotonic assumptions behind this threshold from
the numerical choices that made the example easy to compute.

\begin{pdclaim}{Theorem}{The Escalation Threshold and Its Tuning Band}
\setlength{\parskip}{4pt plus 1pt}
\label{thm:escalation-threshold}
Let $w>0$, $\theta\ge0$, and let $U,V$ be continuous on $[0,1]$, with $U$
strictly increasing and $V:[0,1]\to[0,1]$ nondecreasing. An agent with signal
$u$ receives expected benefit $U(u)$ before the false-escalation debit; $V(u)$ is the
probability its escalation is upheld. Its expected payoff
$\Pi_\theta(u)=U(u)-\theta w\,(1-V(u))$ is strictly increasing. If
$\Pi_\theta(0)<0<\Pi_\theta(1)$, there is a unique interior zero $u^\star$.
Choosing escalation at indifference gives the rule $u\ge u^\star$.

Increasing $\theta$ can only shrink the escalation set
$\mathrm{Esc}_\theta=\{u:\Pi_\theta(u)\ge0\}$. Draw the signal $u$ from a fixed
distribution on $[0,1]$. Let $c_{\mathrm{esc}}(u)\ge0$ be the operator's expected
cost of handling an escalation at signal $u$, and $c_{\mathrm{sil}}(u)\ge0$ the
expected cost of a silence there. The two aggregates are the \emph{alarm load}
$L_{\mathrm{A}}(\theta)=\mathbb E\bigl[c_{\mathrm{esc}}(u)\,\mathbf 1\{u\in\mathrm{Esc}_\theta\}\bigr]$
and the \emph{miss loss}
$L_{\mathrm{M}}(\theta)=\mathbb E\bigl[c_{\mathrm{sil}}(u)\,\mathbf 1\{u\notin\mathrm{Esc}_\theta\}\bigr]$,
and the two limits are an attention budget, $L_{\mathrm{A}}(\theta)\le A_{\mathrm{att}}$,
and a miss budget, $L_{\mathrm{M}}(\theta)\le A_{\mathrm{miss}}$. The debits meeting
both limits form an interval, possibly empty or unbounded. If both aggregates
are continuous in $\theta$, that \mbox{interval} is closed; a finite
$[\theta_{\min},\theta_{\max}]$ also requires a finite upper boundary.
\end{pdclaim}
\begin{proof}
\setlength{\parskip}{4pt plus 1pt}
Strict increase follows from strictly increasing $U$ plus nondecreasing
$\theta wV$. Continuity and the two endpoint inequalities give existence by
the intermediate value theorem.

For each fixed $u$, increasing $\theta$
cannot increase payoff, so the escalation sets are nested.

Because the sets are nested and both cost functions are nonnegative, alarm
load is nonincreasing and miss loss nondecreasing in $\theta$. The debits within
the attention budget therefore form an interval unbounded above, those within the
miss budget an interval starting at $0$, and their intersection is an interval;
continuity makes both sublevel sets closed. A point mass of the signal
distribution at the threshold can break that continuity, so closed endpoints
do not follow from monotonicity alone.
\end{proof}
\pdscope{The validation rule is held fixed; an operator who also changes it in response to the agents' behavior needs a joint equilibrium analysis.}

\section{The operator as a modeled entity}\label{sec:operator}
% ═════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

The legibility layer exists for an operator who must decide what to inspect,
permit, or stop. Consider a fixed review interval with one person on duty. Adding
agents may increase the incoming work without increasing that person's available
review time. Attention and trust calibration therefore belong in the workload
model alongside the swarm's token costs (\S\ref{sec:tokens}); whether either is
the binding constraint depends on the workload.

\keyidea[More agents do not add attention]{With staffing and available review time
held fixed, additional agents do not automatically add review capacity. A
single-server queue can approximate sequential human review, but its service
times depend on the task, the interface, and the operator's experience. We need to
measure the cost of both inspection effort and decision errors, instead of assuming
a universal human service rate or a future token price.}

\textbf{Miller's} \emph{The Magical Number Seven}\pdcite{miller1956}
distinguishes limits in absolute judgment from limits in immediate memory. Familiar
chunks and recoding change how much information a remembered item carries. These
experiments give no universal seven-item limit for dashboards and no
maximum number of agents one person can supervise.

\textbf{Wickens' multiple-resource theory}\pdcite{wickens2002} adds a different
warning: equal durations need not impose equal interference. Tasks can compete
for resources associated with processing stages, sensory modalities, visual
channels, and representational codes. Under the model's comparable-demand
conditions, greater resource overlap predicts greater interference. This
motivates measuring how workload is distributed as well as elapsed time. Whether
minimizing peak load is the right objective, and whether the proposed
ambient/dense display split (\S\ref{sec:ambient}) achieves it, must be evaluated
on the decisions the display is meant to support.

\subsection{The attention objective, built on signal detection theory}\label{sec:sdt}

An average such as ``attention-seconds per correct decision'' can conceal rare,
expensive mistakes among many cheap successes. \textbf{Signal Detection Theory}
(\textbf{Green \& Swets 1966}\pdcite{greenswets1966}) separates two questions:
how well does the evidence distinguish dangerous from safe actions, and where
should the inspection threshold sit? The first question is answered by the
\pdterm{sensitivity}, written $d'$: how far apart the evidence scores of
dangerous and safe actions lie. For equal-variance Gaussian evidence,
$d'=(\mu_{\mathrm{danger}}-\mu_{\mathrm{safe}})/\sigma_{\mathrm{ev}}$, the
separation of the means in common standard-deviation units. Holding these
distributions fixed, moving the threshold changes misses and false alarms;
it does not change $d'$.

The second question is answered by the \pdterm{decision criterion} $\beta$, a threshold
on the evidence score: inspect above it, otherwise permit the action. Green and
Swets state the criterion as the likelihood ratio at that threshold; for
equal-variance Gaussian evidence the ratio rises with the score, so both
descriptions pick the same rule. A \emph{miss} permits a dangerous action; a \emph{false alarm}
inspects a safe one. The preferred threshold depends on prevalence and the costs
of those outcomes. A destructive migration and an unnecessary inspection need
not carry the same cost. The next example begins with just two available
policies, inspect every item in a class or inspect none.

\begin{pdexample}{Two Action Classes, Costed Before the Objective Is Written Down}
\setlength{\parskip}{4pt plus 1pt}
The objective below looks heavier than it is, so cost out two action classes with it first, in its crudest collapse: zoom \emph{every} item in a class or zoom \emph{none} of them, at an additional false-alarm cost $C_{\text{fa}}=1$ and an attention cost of $c_{\mathrm{att}}=0.2$ per inspection. Convert attention and harm to the same
normalized cost units, and assume inspection detects every dangerous action.

Take a migration that drops a foreign key, with a base rate of genuinely dangerous instances $\pi=0.2$ and a miss cost $C_{\text{miss}}=500$. Never zoom and you pay the miss term alone,
\[
0.2\times 500 = 100.0.
\]
Always zoom and you pay
\[
(1-0.2)\times 1 + 0.2 = 0.8+0.2 = 1.0.
\]
A hundred against one: the class is decided by the miss term before irreversibility is counted separately at all, which is why the design forces the zoom rather than suggesting it.

Now take a \texttt{tests/}-only formatting diff, $\pi=0.001$ and $C_{\text{miss}}=5$. Never zoom:
\[
0.001\times 5 = 0.005.
\]
Always zoom:
\[
0.999\times 1 + 0.2 = 1.199.
\]
That is a factor of about $240$ the other way. Under these stipulated costs and prevalences, the two classes call for different inspection policies. This calculation can inform Alice's grant in \S\ref{sec:consent}; the costs are stipulated, and only Alice can issue the grant.

The whole apparatus below exists to keep that arithmetic honest once $P_{\text{miss}}$ and $P_{\text{fa}}$ stop being degenerate.

\end{pdexample}

\begin{pdclaim}{Design invariant}{The SDT-Based Attention Objective}
\setlength{\parskip}{4pt plus 1pt}
\label{def:sdt}
Fix the score distributions for safe and dangerous actions. Let
$P_{\text{miss}}(\beta)$ be the probability of no inspection conditional on danger,
and $P_{\text{fa}}(\beta)$ the probability of inspection conditional on safety.
Assume inspection detects and prevents the dangerous action. Let $\pi$ be the
dangerous-action prevalence, $C_{\text{miss}}$ the cost of an unprevented danger,
$C_{\text{fa}}$ the additional burden of an unnecessary inspection, and
$c_{\mathrm{att}}$ the cost of each inspection. Express all costs in the same
units without counting attention twice. With at most one inspection per action,
$\mathbb{E}[\text{zooms}(\beta)]$ is the inspection probability. The objective is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sdt}
\min_{\beta}\ \Big[\, \pi\,C_{\text{miss}}\,P_{\text{miss}}(\beta)
   \;+\; (1-\pi)\,C_{\text{fa}}\,P_{\text{fa}}(\beta)
   \;+\; c_{\mathrm{att}}\,\mathbb{E}[\text{zooms}(\beta)] \,\Big].
\end{equation}

The forced-zoom policy $p_{\mathrm{zoom}}(r,s,v)$, the probability that the daemon makes the
operator open an action before it proceeds, given the agent's reputation $r$, the
action's stakes $s$ and its reversibility $v$, is calibrated against this loss after specifying
the score distributions that determine $P_{\text{miss}}$ and $P_{\text{fa}}$.
An imperfect inspection requires an additional residual-harm term; the displayed
loss does not include that error.

Equation~\ref{eq:sdt} is a decision objective, not by itself a fitted detector: what
the design commits to is that operator friction is set by this loss and not by taste,
and \emph{whether} an operator calibrated toward it outperforms one who is not is a
separate, empirical question this chapter does not settle (the paragraph below names
the live rival account).
\end{pdclaim}

\paragraph{The optimal criterion, and what each mechanism moves.} Minimizing
Eq.~\ref{eq:sdt} score by score gives the likelihood-ratio rule: inspect an
action whose evidence score is $x$ when
\[
\mathrm{LR}(x)\;\ge\;\frac{1-\pi}{\pi}\cdot
\frac{c_{\mathrm{att}}+C_{\text{fa}}}{C_{\text{miss}}-c_{\mathrm{att}}},
\]
where $\mathrm{LR}(x)$ is the density of score $x$ among dangerous actions
divided by its density among safe ones, and $C_{\text{miss}}>c_{\mathrm{att}}$.
For the equal-variance Gaussian evidence above, $\mathrm{LR}$ rises with the
score, so the rule is a threshold $\beta$ on the score. It is the posterior
threshold of Theorem~\ref{thm:regret-head} rewritten with Bayes' rule; that
theorem carries the derivation, and Example~\ref{ex:inspection-bar} works the
numbers. The chapter's mechanisms then divide the work. The legibility
mechanisms of \S\ref{sec:zoom} raise $d'$: a digest whose claims open to
checkable artifacts separates dangerous actions from safe ones. Consent grants
(\S\ref{sec:consent}) set the criterion for each action class: an action inside
a grant proceeds without inspection, and one outside it is escalated. The
attention costs of this section and \S\ref{sec:discovery} price the criterion,
since they fix the right-hand side above.

\input{figures/legible-swarm-sdt}

This is a normative claim: a Bayesian SDT decision-maker is the target to
calibrate toward. Naturalistic decision-making
research argues that skilled operators under time pressure do something else
entirely: they recognize the situation as a case they have seen before and act
on the match, rather than computing a likelihood ratio and holding it against a
threshold~\pdcite{klein1998}. That rival account of what the operator's
instrument actually measures remains open. The objective says what Alice should
be calibrated toward and makes no claim about what she is doing at 3:25 in the
afternoon.

Figure~\ref{fig:sdt} lays the same decision out visually: the operator's criterion
$\beta$ trading misses against false alarms. The miss-cost term charges for the
harm of zero review, but it does not always rule zero review out. If inspection
costs more than the expected harm it prevents, zero review can minimize this
objective, as the formatting example shows.

We would like the forced-zoom policy $p_{\mathrm{zoom}}$ to rise
with stakes, to rise as reversibility falls, and to fall as reputation rises.
The attention objective above does not guarantee this; it follows only from a
model linking $r$, $s$ and $v$ to the distributions of evidence scores, so it
is a requirement to test when the policy is calibrated. Until a reputation
system exists, $r$ carries no information: every agent starts from the same
prior.

\subsection{Trust calibration is multi-dimensional, and detection fades over a long watch}\label{sec:calibration}

contexts in which the automation has different capabilities.

The proposed canary mechanism, described below, slips known and already-fixed

items whose ground truth is known. \textbf{Warm, Parasuraman \&

mental effort and produce stress; performance and workload depend on task
conditions. \textbf{Mackworth's} experiments~\pdcite{mackworth1948} are the
classic study of the \pdterm{vigilance decrement}, the decline in detection over
a long watch; they give no general timer for software review.
A falling catch rate could reflect time on task, probe difficulty, prevalence,
or changes in review conditions. It should not automatically be interpreted as
worse trust calibration or greater swarm danger. Evaluation should record those
conditions and compare like workloads. Breaks and changes in probe frequency are
interventions to test, with their own costs in Eq.~\ref{eq:sdt}, rather than
established remedies for this particular interface.

\pitfall[Canary scores can be gamed]{When catch rate controls friction or grants,
operators have an incentive to optimize that score. Recognizing a recurring probe
template can improve the score without improving detection of real defects.
This is the risk highlighted by \textbf{Campbell's Law}\pdcite{campbell1979}:
using an indicator for consequential decisions can distort the process it is
meant to assess. Refreshed holdouts, separation of probe generation from scoring,
and independent outcome evidence are proposed safeguards. Their effect on real
decisions, and on gaming, must be evaluated.}

\subsection{Perceiving, understanding and projecting the swarm's state}\label{sec:sa}

Digest-with-zoom can organize current state and its supporting evidence. What
the operator understands from it is a separate question. Endsley~\pdcite{endsley1995}
defines \pdterm{situation awareness} in three levels: perceiving the current
state, comprehending it, and projecting future states. She treats situation
awareness, decision-making, and performance as distinct stages.
In Endsley and Kiris's navigation experiment,\pdcite{endsleykiris1995}
fully automated control produced poorer comprehension than manual control, but
no significant perception difference. That result motivates caution about passive
supervision, though it comes from one navigation task.

Our \emph{design hypothesis} is that an explicit projection mode would help an
operator anticipate the swarm's next effects. The proposed freeze-probe in
Figure~\ref{fig:sa-levels} would evaluate those predictions; no human-performance
result is claimed here. The forensic time-axis (\S\ref{sec:timeaxis}) reconstructs
the past; this proposed mode would look forward.

\subsection{The Bainbridge--Scott convergence and the drift from doing to absence}\label{sec:abdication}

\textbf{Bainbridge's} \emph{Ironies of Automation}\pdcite{bainbridge1983}
identifies a practical tension: automating routine control can remove opportunities
to practise while leaving the operator responsible for difficult exceptions.
This resembles Scott's concern about losing situated knowledge. This chapter's
\pdterm{abdication gradient} is the proposed slide by which an operator goes from
doing the work to approving it, then rubber-stamping it, then being absent:
$\text{doer}\to\text{approver}\to\text{rubber-stamp}\to\text{absent}$. It is
a hypothesis; nobody has measured it.

The \pdterm[inception canary]{Inception Canary} is a consented, blinded probe of defect
detection. It is intended to test meaningful review rather than impose arbitrary
manual tasks. Its interruptions also cost something: Leroy's
attention-residue experiments~\pdcite{leroy2009} motivate measuring the cost of
switching away from unfinished work, case by case.

\begin{protocol}{The Inception Canary protocol}\label{prot:canary}
\pdstatus{\Vision}{no implementation}
The proposed protocol measures detection of known defects on sandboxed replays; whether training with it improves real supervision is an empirical question. Operators enroll knowingly: the program's existence, rate $\rho_{\text{canary}}$, and stakes are disclosed at onboarding; only the \emph{which} and \emph{when} are blinded:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt]
\item \textbf{Blinded holdout replay.} An out-of-band summarizer (a separate small model that works from kernel records and diffs; \S\ref{sec:tokens-digest} proposes it) draws from a curated holdout of \emph{historical, already-fixed} defects (an off-by-one boundary condition, an unindexed query, a removed regression test), re-materializes one inside a sandboxed replay of a past work item at rate $\rho_{\text{canary}}$, and routes it through the review surface indistinguishably from live traffic. No anomaly is injected into a live pull request or pending action: the replay cannot directly modify production. Interruptions and score-driven changes to future grants can still affect real work and require evaluation.
\item \textbf{Attention queue surfacing.} The item is placed in the Attention Queue with standard high-regret scoring.
\item \textbf{Illustrative adjudication cases:}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,nosep]
\item \emph{Rubber-stamp failure:} If the operator approves without expanding the forced-zoom diff, the daemon halts execution \mbox{pre-commit}, logs an \textsc{Inception Canary Failed} event, and tightens the operator's autonomous policy lease (lowering the threshold at which future agent actions require manual confirmation).
\item \emph{Vigilant detection:} If the operator zooms, identifies the flaw, and rejects it, the daemon logs \textsc{Canary Defeated} and increases the \emph{Operator Trust Score}, unlocking extended autonomous execution grants (e.g., unattended batch merges).
\end{itemize}
Other responses, including inspecting and then approving the defect, require
separate scoring rules. These two cases do not specify a complete controller;
grants must remain within independently authorized policy limits.
\end{enumerate}
\end{protocol}

provenance. Probe performance is a proxy, not a complete measure of situation

\subsection{Modes of cooperation}\label{sec:cooperation-modes}

The same checkout repair can be supervised in different ways. In a paired
session, Alice inspects Heron's proposed patch before authorizing it. In an
unattended session, Heron prepares the candidate and its receipts for later
review under a narrower pre-issued grant. In a partitioned team, several
workers propose changes and the coordinator records their dependencies.

These regimes change when evidence reaches Alice and which effects are
pre-authorized. They do not change Bob's acceptance criterion or excuse an
unrecorded effect. A monitor that only advises cannot silently acquire write
authority; a required human decision must remain visible and actionable.

No mode name orders review cost by itself. An unattended, well-tested formatting
patch may be easier to assess than a complex paired repair. Compare regimes on
the same task and acceptable error rate, with the available evidence and actual
review effort stated. That comparison reuses the operator model rather than
introducing a second catalogue of product modes.

\section{When inspection falls behind}\label{sec:readpoverty}

Alice now has a rule for deciding what counts as an acceptable repair and
which events require her attention. The remaining question is whether the
information reaching her can support that decision within her inspection
budget. For Wren's patch, a digest that omits the unrelated migration can
preserve every displayed link and still omit the decisive defect. We now
measure that loss: first by counting the distinctions a summary must retain,
then by studying selection and compaction, the shortening of an agent's
working context into a summary when the context runs out of room. The bounds count the bits that
particular information guarantees require; the authorization and evidence

\pdmarginanalogy{ls-attention-annunciator}{}{ch04-selective-attention-annunciator}{Like a shipboard annunciator dropping a signal flag only when a monitored circuit faults, selective attention suppresses routine traffic to present only actionable exceptions.}%
Suppose an operator can inspect six short updates per minute. Two workers each
producing one update per minute leave room to spare. Fifty workers at the same
rate produce fifty updates per minute, so an unfiltered queue grows by forty-four
updates per minute. This calculation assumes fixed update size and inspection
cost; it illustrates a workload, not a measured limit on human attention.

Reading every record is not the only possible task. If the operator needs to
identify the owner of an OAuth repair, a maintained \pdterm{agent directory}, a list of
who exists and where their records live, may answer that
question without presenting every worker's history. If the operator must decide
whether the repair is safe to accept, an ownership label alone is inadequate:
the relevant revision, checks and authority must remain accessible.

The chapter models this mismatch using a limited observation budget. A digest
selects what crosses that boundary, and its adequacy depends on the decision
to be made. The finite counting arguments below state their own source,
encoding and error assumptions; the update-count example describes a workload,
not a Shannon channel capacity or a human throughput in bits. A larger
inspection budget changes the model's parameters, and a summary must still say
which distinctions it preserves.

\begin{pdclaim}{Empirical hypothesis}{Reading Runs Out Before Writing}
\setlength{\parskip}{4pt plus 1pt}
\label{def:readpoverty}
\pdterm[read-poverty]{Read-poverty} is a regime in which legible state accumulates faster than it
can be consulted, so participants fall back to $O(n)$ eyeball search over the
population, or, worse, act blind.

Giving this situation a name proves nothing. The real claim is that, as a
swarm grows, the operator's capacity to read runs out \emph{before} the tools
that stop agents from overwriting each other (claims, locks, a single writer)
do. At large $n$, the limit is how fast someone can read, not how fast agents
can write safely.

That claim needs measurement, and this chapter does not prove it. The lower
bound in \S\ref{sec:lowerbound} answers a different question: once reading is
the limit, how many bits must a summary carry?
\end{pdclaim}

\pdthesis{The hypothesis is that inspection can become the limiting resource
while the write side still has capacity. Testing it requires measuring both
sides on the same workload. The results below are consequences of a
specified inspection model; which bottleneck dominates a given swarm is the
measurement question.}

A directory spends maintenance effort to make a class of lookup cheaper.
Its query cost depends on its representation, the query and the result size;
indexing alone supplies no universal complexity bound. Nor does a fast answer
necessarily preserve what matters. ``This agent owns auth'' omits relevant
evidence if its last three auth pull requests were reverted. The directory
must support inspection of that evidence, rather than turn an entry into an
unqualified verdict. This motivates the zoom requirement
(Invariant~\ref{def:zoom}).

\subsection{Value at different swarm sizes}\label{sec:value-curve}

The legibility layer should help a solo developer as well as a larger team.
At $n=1$, a briefing can locate unfinished work, an attestation can expose what
was checked, and a completion receipt can point to the result. These are proposed
benefits for decisions about one agent; their value still depends on accuracy,
review effort, and the task. Discovery ranking is not needed to select among
agents when there is only one candidate.

The same surfaces must remain useful when review demand exceeds capacity.
Comparing populations such as two, fifty, or five hundred agents could help
study that transition, but those numbers are not established thresholds. Update
rates, query selectivity, staffing, and evidence quality matter alongside the
number of agents.

The next section separates three functions: a registry records who exists and
where their evidence lives; relevance ranking selects candidates for a query;
reputation associates observed outcomes with stable identities. These functions
have dependencies, but do not form a strict ranking-first pipeline. Stale registry
entries can misdirect retrieval; reputation needs attributable evidence, yet can
attach to an identity even in an unranked registry. Ranking may subsequently use
that reputation as one input.

\subsection{A legibility lower bound}\label{sec:lowerbound}

A useful limit follows by counting how many critical sets each digest message
can cover. This is a combinatorial information argument, related to counting
bounds in group testing~\pdcite{dorfman,hwang}. Here the decoder selects a set
of at most $m$ artifacts for the reader to inspect. One message can cover several
possible critical sets, provided that every artifact in each of those sets is
included in the selected review set. The message budget and the review budget
therefore constrain one another.

Consider four artifacts, exactly one critical, with permission to open two. One bit can select either the pair $\{1,2\}$ or the pair $\{3,4\}$; the encoder sends the pair containing the critical artifact. Zero bits cannot suffice: a fixed pair misses either of the other two possibilities. If four opens are allowed, zero bits do suffice. The next bound counts this trade between message length and inspection effort.

\pdmarginfigure{shannon}{A fixed message budget distinguishes only finitely many cases. The review budget limits how many critical sets one message can safely cover.}%
\begin{pdclaim}{Theorem}{Legibility Lower Bound with a Review Budget}
\setlength{\parskip}{4pt plus 1pt}
\label{thm:lowerbound}
Let $N$ artifacts contain an unknown critical subset $S$ of size $k$. A digest
maps each possible $S$ to a review set $\hat S$ with $S\subseteq\hat S$ and
$|\hat S|\le m$, where $k\le m\le N$.

Any such zero-miss digest needs at least
\[
\log_2\!\binom{N}{k}-\log_2\!\binom{m}{k}
\]
bits for a fixed-length message (or a prefix-free code in the worst case).
An integer message budget must round the lower bound upward.

Exact identification ($m=k$) recovers the
$\log_2\binom Nk$ bound; allowing the operator to open everything ($m=N$) correctly
reduces the bound to zero.
\end{pdclaim}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
\setlength{\parskip}{4pt plus 1pt}
There are $\binom Nk$ possible critical subsets. One digest message whose review
set has size at most $m$ can safely cover at most $\binom mk$ of them, namely the
$k$-subsets contained in that review set.

Therefore at least
$\binom Nk/\binom mk$ distinct messages are required. Taking base-two logarithms
gives the bound.

If fewer messages are available, some critical subset is not
contained in its message's review set, producing a
false negative: an unopened critical artifact. This is a coverage failure.
It differs from a broken zoom link: a digest can link every selected item
correctly while omitting the one item the operator needed.

Hence the stated log-ratio is a lower bound for a
zero-miss digest with review budget $m$.
\end{proof}

\noindent\Verified.\footnote{The sweeps cited in this subsection were regenerated at seed
20260816.} For the proof, treat a digest as a \emph{message} that selects, from a
codebook shared with the overseer, which $m$ items to open, rather than as a
description of the night's work. A $B$-bit message distinguishes at most $2^B$
codewords, and each codeword covers only $\binom{m}{k}$ of the $\binom{N}{k}$ ways
the critical items could be placed. The bound gives the fewest bits
any zero-miss digest can use. The bound does not say
a digest needs $B^\star$ bits to be useful. It counts only what the zero-miss
guarantee needs; cheaper digests are fine the moment misses are tolerated, and
the two-constraint rate below measures exactly that saving.

\paragraph{When the bound is reached.} A fixed-length zero-miss digest is a
family of review sets of size $m$, one per message, such that every possible
critical $k$-set lies inside at least one of them. Write $\mathrm{Cov}(N,m,k)$
for the size of the smallest such family, the covering number of design theory. The counting
argument gives $\mathrm{Cov}(N,m,k)\ge\binom Nk/\binom mk$, and at small $N$ the
gap is real: four artifacts with two critical and three opens need three review
sets, not two, because any two triples drawn from four items share a pair. For
fixed $k$ and $m$, Rödl~\pdcite{rodl1985} proved that
$\mathrm{Cov}(N,m,k)\binom mk/\binom Nk\to1$ as $N\to\infty$. The best codebook
then needs $\log_2\binom Nk-\log_2\binom mk+o(1)$ bits before rounding up, so in
that regime the theorem's bound is asymptotically exact. Rödl's theorem says
nothing when $k$ or $m$ grows with $N$.

When two readers need disjoint critical sets, one joint digest given both
readers' opens has a lower bound below the sum of two separate digests'
bounds; Theorem~\ref{thm:pooling-gain} states the comparison.

\paragraph{The falsification experiment.} For uniformly distributed critical
$k$-sets, a $B$-bit digest's zero-miss probability is at most
$\min\bigl(1,\,2^B\binom{m}{k}/\binom{N}{k}\bigr)$. This counting ceiling need
not be attainable: review sets can overlap in the critical sets they cover.
A statistically reliable miss rate below the implied lower bound would challenge
the model or its implementation. Three encoders were run against a
shared randomized-cover codebook: an \emph{oracle} that sees the true set and
addresses the best codeword its $B$ bits can reach, a \emph{noisy} encoder (feature
signal-to-noise ratio near $1$), and a \emph{random} one, with $4{,}000$ placements
per budget point, sweeping $B$ through the bound at $N{=}60$, $k{=}2$, $m{=}8$.
Result: $0/16$ violations; the oracle hugs the bound, staying just above it, and
the other two need more bits [internal, \texttt{a7\_experiment.py}].

\paragraph{A wrong turn, reported.} A first implementation handed the oracle a
perfect per-item score vector and charged $B$ bits only for resolving rank ties:
$8/14$ apparent violations. They came from a side channel: the identity of the
critical set leaked outside the metered message. Rebuilding
the model so that $B$ is a literal message length gating an encoder and decoder pair
restored agreement. In general, a simulation that appears to beat an
information bound is leaking through an unmetered channel, so audit the meter before
doubting the theorem or trusting the refutation.

Widen the budget to $m=100$ and the second term

\[

The bound is the formal reason \emph{forgetting must be selective, not lossy-by-volume}:
a digest may drop inert detail freely, but it must retain enough to \emph{point at}
every critical artifact, which is precisely the ``summarize the state, link to
the work'' rule. It also bounds recursive summarization (\S\ref{sec:cascade}): each
re-summarization that drops below the lower bound on the critical set is irreversible
information loss, which is why one must compact from the durable artifacts, never from
the previous summary.

\paragraph{The second stage: what a zoom costs.} The lower bound above covers
only the first stage, the digest that narrows $N$ artifacts to a flagged set of size
$F$. It says nothing about the second stage, which is where Invariant~\ref{def:zoom}
sends the operator. Two results complete the account. The first gives the digest's
cost once misses are tolerated; the second gives the cost of the zoom.

Model each artifact's critical status as i.i.d.\ $X\sim\mathrm{Bern}(p)$, let
the digest emit a per-item flag $\hat X\in\{0,1\}$, and give the two failure modes
separate budgets:
\[
R(\delta,f)\;=\;\min_{P_{\hat X\mid X}}\; I(X;\hat X)
\quad\text{s.t.}\quad
\Pr(X{=}1,\hat X{=}0)\le\delta,
\qquad
\Pr(\hat X{=}1)\le f .
\]
This is a custom formulation, deliberately: classical rate--distortion charges one
scalar distortion, which cannot express that oversight weighs a missed critical
item differently from a wasted open. The nearest published lines are expansion coding
with one-sided error for continuous sources~\pdcite{expansion} and the
rate--distortion--classification theory for Bernoulli sources~\pdcite{rdc}; neither
treats false negatives as a separate constraint for binary sources.

\begin{pdclaim}{Theorem}{The Two-Constraint Digest Rate, Pinned}
\setlength{\parskip}{4pt plus 1pt}
\label{thm:pinned-joint}
For $X\sim\mathrm{Bern}(p)$ with $0<p<1$, $0\le\delta<p$ and $p-\delta\le f<1-\delta$, if
$f<1-\delta/p$ (the flag rate below which an $X$-independent flagger cannot meet
the miss constraint), both constraints bind at the optimum and the minimizing joint
is fully pinned:
\[
q_{11}=p-\delta,\qquad q_{10}=\delta,\qquad q_{01}=f-(p-\delta),\qquad
q_{00}=1-f-\delta,
\]
feasible iff all four are nonnegative, and $R(\delta,f)=I(X;\hat X)$ evaluated at
this joint.

Because the joint is pinned, so is the rate, and it is worth printing
in the form someone can actually evaluate rather than leaving as a named
functional. With source margin $\Pr(X{=}1)=p$ and digest margin
$\Pr(\hat X{=}1)=f$, the mutual information is the four-term sum
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pinned-rate}
\begin{split}
R(\delta,f) ={}&(p-\delta)\log_2\frac{p-\delta}{p\,f} \\[
2pt]
&+\delta\log_2\frac{\delta}{p\,(1-f)} \\[2pt]
&+(f-p+\delta)\log_2\frac{f-p+\delta}{(1-p)\,f} \\[2pt]
&+(1-f-\delta)\log_2\frac{1-f-\delta}{(1-p)(1-f)},
\end{split}
\end{equation}
one term per cell of the $2\times2$ joint, each comparing that cell against the
product of its margins, with $0\log_2 0$ read as $0$.
At the zero-miss,
tightest-flag corner, $R(0,f{\to}p)=H(p)$: the digest
must carry the source's full entropy, and Eq.~\ref{eq:pinned-rate} shows why in
two lines, since $\delta=0$ and $f=p$ kill the middle two terms and leave
$p\log_2(1/p)+(1-p)\log_2(1/(1-p))$.
Past the line $f\ge 1-\delta/p$ an independent
flagger already meets the miss budget on its own and the true rate is exactly $0$.
\end{pdclaim}
\begin{proof}
\setlength{\parskip}{4pt plus 1pt}
Parametrize the joint by its miss mass $q_{10}$ and its false-flag mass
$q_{01}$; the source margin fixes $q_{11}=p-q_{10}$ and $q_{00}=1-p-q_{01}$.
The partial derivatives of $I(X;\hat X)$ are log-likelihood ratios,
\[
\frac{\partial I}{\partial q_{10}}=\log_2\frac{\Pr(X{=}1\mid\hat X{=}0)}{\Pr(X{=}1\mid\hat X{=}1)},
\qquad
\frac{\partial I}{\partial q_{01}}=\log_2\frac{\Pr(X{=}0\mid\hat X{=}1)}{\Pr(X{=}0\mid\hat X{=}0)},
\]
and both are negative wherever the flag is positively informative, that is,
wherever $q_{11}q_{00}>q_{10}q_{01}$. An independent flagger
$\hat X\sim\mathrm{Bern}(\phi)$ has miss mass $p(1-\phi)$, so it meets the miss
constraint only when $\phi\ge 1-\delta/p$; when $f<1-\delta/p$ the feasible set
contains no independent coupling. The feasible set is convex, so it then cannot
hold couplings of both signs, and at the corner $(q_{10},q_{01})=(\delta,f-p+\delta)$
the difference $q_{11}q_{00}-q_{10}q_{01}$ equals $p(1-f)-\delta>0$. Every
feasible coupling is therefore positively informative, $I$ falls as either mass
grows, and at the minimum both constraints bind.
Binding constraints fix $q_{10}=\delta$ and
$q_{11}+q_{01}=f$; with the source margin $q_{10}+q_{11}=p$ this pins all four
cells.
At $(\delta,f)=(0,p)$ the joint is diagonal, $\hat X=X$, and $I=H(p)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{pdclaim}{Theorem}{Zoom Advantage}
\setlength{\parskip}{4pt plus 1pt}
\label{thm:zoom-advantage}
Let a flagged set of $F$ items contain an unknown set $P$ of $1\le k\le F$
positives. Assume a noiseless group oracle of unit query cost reports exactly
whether $J\cap P=\emptyset$ for a queried group $J$.

\emph{(a) Plain halving.} Adaptive halving (query
the flagged set; discard any group that tests negative; report any positive
singleton; otherwise split the group into halves of sizes
$\lfloor|J|/2\rfloor,\lceil|J|/2\rceil$ and recurse on both) identifies $P$
exactly, using a total number of group queries
\[
Q \;\le\; 2k\left\lceil\log_2(F/k)\right\rceil + 4k
\qquad\text{(and always } Q\le 2F-1\text{)},
\]
for every placement of the $k$ positives, against $F$ opens for flat inspection.
Halving needs no knowledge of $k$.

The leading constant is tight: for $F$ and $k$ powers of two, evenly spread
positives force $Q=2k\log_2(F/k)+2k-1$. Written in the flagged-set density $d=k/F$
the guaranteed advantage is
\[
\mathrm{adv}(d)\;=\;\frac{F}{2k\lceil\log_2(F/k)\rceil+4k}
\;=\;\frac{1}{d\bigl(2\lceil\log_2(1/d)\rceil+4\bigr)},
\]
which exceeds $1$ exactly on $0<d<1/12$ and equals $1$ at $d=1/12$.

\emph{(b) Hwang's generalized binary splitting.} If the tester knows $k$,
Hwang's generalized binary splitting~\pdcite{hwang} identifies $P$ exactly with
at most
\[
Q_{\mathrm{H}}\;\le\;\log_2\binom Fk+k
\]
group queries for every placement. This certifies a strict saving over flat
inspection whenever $H(d)+d<1$, that is, for $0<d<d_{\mathrm{H}}$, where
$d_{\mathrm{H}}\approx0.2271$ is the root of $H(d)+d=1$ and $H$ is the binary
entropy.
\end{pdclaim}
\begin{proof}
\setlength{\parskip}{4pt plus 1pt}
Part (a). Every group the algorithm queries is a node of the halving tree $T$ of $[F]$: the
root is the full flagged set, each internal node's children are its two halves,
leaves are singletons. A node of size $s$ has children of size at most
$\lceil s/2\rceil$, so every node at depth $\ell$ has size at most
$\lceil F/2^\ell\rceil$, $T$ has depth $\ell_{\max}\le\lceil\log_2 F\rceil$, and $T$ has exactly
$2F-1$ nodes, which gives the unconditional bound.

Call a node \emph{positive} if its group meets $P$. The algorithm splits exactly
the positive non-singleton nodes it visits, and every queried node is either the
root or a child of a split node; writing $n^{+}$ for the number of positive
non-singleton nodes of $T$, $Q\le 1+2n^{+}$. The nodes at any fixed depth are
pairwise disjoint subsets of $[F]$, so each positive item lies in at most one of
them, and at most $\min(k,2^\ell)$ nodes at depth $\ell$ are positive. Charge the
positive nodes in two regimes. At the top of the tree, $\ell\le\lfloor\log_2 k\rfloor$,
charge each positive node to its level: at most
$\sum_{\ell=0}^{\lfloor\log_2 k\rfloor}2^\ell\le 2k-1$ in total. Below the fan-out,
$\ell>\lfloor\log_2 k\rfloor$, charge each positive node to one positive item it
contains; by disjointness within a level each of the $k$ positives absorbs at most
one charge per level, and non-singleton nodes exist only at depths $\ell\le \ell_{\max}-1$, so
the number of charging levels is
$\ell_{\max}-1-\lfloor\log_2 k\rfloor<(\log_2 F+1)-1-(\log_2 k-1)=\log_2(F/k)+1$; an integer
strictly below $\log_2(F/k)+1$ is at most $\lceil\log_2(F/k)\rceil$, so these
levels contribute at most $k\lceil\log_2(F/k)\rceil$ charges. Summing,
$n^{+}\le k\lceil\log_2(F/k)\rceil+2k-1$ and
$Q\le 2k\lceil\log_2(F/k)\rceil+4k-1$. Correctness is immediate: a discarded group
is certified free of positives, every positive survives discarding at every level,
and each is eventually isolated and reported.

For tightness take $F=2^{n_F}$, $k=2^{n_k}$, $n_k\le n_F$, positives at positions
$\{iF/k:0\le i<k\}$. Every node at depth $\ell\le n_k$ contains a positive, so all
$2^{n_k+1}-1=2k-1$ nodes down to depth $n_k$ are queried; below depth $n_k$ exactly $k$
nodes per level are positive, each is split at levels $n_k,\dots,n_F-1$, and each split
queries two children; the total is $(2k-1)+2k(n_F-n_k)=2k\log_2(F/k)+2k-1$. The
density form follows by dividing $F$ by the bound. On
$2^{-j}\le d<2^{-(j-1)}$, its denominator is the linear function
$d(2j+4)$; the ratio is therefore hyperbolic on each such interval.
For $j\ge5$ the denominator is below $1$ throughout the interval;
for $j=4$ it equals $12d$, crossing $1$ at $d=1/12$;
for $j\le3$ it exceeds $1$ throughout the interval. The endpoint $d=1$
also has denominator $4$. Thus the certificate exceeds $1$ exactly when
$d<1/12$.

Part (b). The query bound is Hwang's~\pdcite{hwang,duhwang}. The entropy bound
$\log_2\binom Fk\le F\,H(k/F)$ gives $Q_{\mathrm{H}}\le F\bigl(H(d)+d\bigr)$,
which is below $F$ when $H(d)+d<1$. The sum rises from $0$ as $d$ grows to
$2/3$, where it is about $1.585$, so it crosses $1$ once on that range, at
$d_{\mathrm{H}}$; for $d\ge2/3$ the single term $(1-d)\log_2\frac1{1-d}\ge(1-d)\log_2 3$
already keeps it above $1$.
\end{proof}

generalized binary splitting~\pdcite{hwang} finds $k$ defectives in

binary outcomes must meet; the full theory is Du and Hwang~\pdcite{duhwang}. Plain
halving needs a factor $2+o(1)$ more queries than Hwang because it re-queries both children of
every split rather than inferring one subgroup and searching within the other.

result, and it is the bound to plan with whenever $k$ is known. A dedicated check re-runs the halving procedure:
at $F{=}2500$, $k{=}10$, mean $162.6$ opens over $150$ random placements, maximum

\paragraph{The composition window.} The two costs come from one budget, and
they compose only inside a window that can be stated exactly. Stage one sets stage
two's density: by Theorem~\ref{thm:pinned-joint} both constraints bind, so the
flagged set holds $(p-\delta)N$ real criticals among $fN$ flags and
$d=k/F=(p-\delta)/f$. Halving's worst-case bound certifies a strict query
saving when $d<1/12$, equivalently $f>12(p-\delta)$; at equality it certifies a
tie. Hwang's bound, for a tester that knows $k$, certifies one whenever
$d<d_{\mathrm{H}}$, equivalently $f>4.40\,(p-\delta)$. At $p{=}0.05$, $\delta{=}0$
halving needs $f>0.60$, a digest that flags over $60\%$ of the night's artifacts,
while Hwang's procedure needs only $f>0.22$. The $60\%$ figure belongs to plain
halving, not to adaptive inspection. When a certificate fails, adaptive
inspection can still be faster on a particular placement of positives: $F=128$
and $k=16$ evenly spread positives have $d=1/8$, outside halving's window, yet
halving needs only $Q=127<128$ queries there, by the tightness formula above.
Inside the window the flags stage one adds are cheap in the second stage,
because $k$ is fixed by the source and doubling $F$ adds only the logarithmic
term: $2k$ further opens for halving, about $k$ for Hwang's procedure.
Outside it flat inspection gives the simpler $F$-query guarantee, although
adaptive inspection can still win on some inputs. At the dense end halving can
lose: at $F{=}100$, $k{=}90$ the run costs $199$ opens against $100$ flat
[internal], which is the unconditional $2F-1$, reached because at that density
essentially every node of the halving tree is positive. Hwang's procedure tests
items one at a time once the remaining items number at most $2k-2$, with $k$
counting the positives not yet found; here that holds from the start, so it costs exactly the $100$ flat opens. The design consequence for this chapter is
therefore conditional, and the condition is now a number: ``summarize the state,
link to the work'' is cheap when the digest over-flags generously and the zoom is
adaptive, and the operator's dial, which trades bits on the digest for opens on
the zoom, offers a favorable trade exactly in the sparse-flag corner.

\begin{pdboundary}
\setlength{\parskip}{4pt plus 1pt}
Theorem~\ref{thm:lowerbound} lower-bounds the zero-miss guarantee in a worst-case
combinatorial model with a data-independent decoder; it says nothing against
cheaper digests at nonzero miss (Theorem~\ref{thm:pinned-joint}'s job) and nothing
average-case.

The falsification result $0/16$ tests the encoder and decoder
formalization at one swept regime ($N{=}60$, $k{=}2$, $m{=}8$); the noisy-encoder
curve in that experiment barely beats random at unit signal-to-noise ratio and is
not evidence that noisy digests are near-optimal.

The frontier $R(\delta,f)$ is an
i.i.d.\ Bernoulli model of critical status, a modeling commitment rather than a
law, and its pinned closed form holds only while $f<1-\delta/p$; evaluating
the formula past that line produces a spurious uptick near
$\delta/p\to1$, a domain-of-validity error and not a feature of the frontier.

Theorem~\ref{thm:zoom-advantage} assumes a reliable group query at unit cost;
correlated or noisy group checks, and group costs growing with $|J|$, change the
accounting. Plain halving is a factor of about two from Hwang's optimum; part
(a) bounds the simplest procedure, which needs no knowledge of $k$, and part (b)
bounds Hwang's, which does.

The sufficient certificates for a strict saving are $f>12(p-\delta)$ for halving
and $f>4.40\,(p-\delta)$ for Hwang's procedure. Both are sufficient, not
necessary: zoom can help outside those ranges, and actual counts of flags and
critical items govern each run.
\end{pdboundary}

\section{Discovery: the directory and ranking for each reader}\label{sec:discovery}
% ═════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

An agent cannot send a request to another agent until it knows which agents
exist and how to reach them. So before asking how to choose whom to ask, we
describe the directory: the list of live agents, what each says it can do, and
where its records are kept.

\paragraph{Three losses, named before they collide.} This section and the next
two prove that certain read surfaces cannot share one scoring function, or
\emph{head} (Thm.~\ref{thm:split-ranker}, Thm.~\ref{thm:split-digest}). Where
two readers rank the same items, the remedy is a \pdterm{split ranker}, one
shared candidate list and decay rule with a separate scoring head for each
reader. The theorems are impossibility results, and an impossibility result is
only as legible as the objects it says cannot be identified. Three distinct losses run through the rest
of the chapter, and a reader who has not seen them side by side will meet them
one at a time, several pages apart, and reasonably assume they are three
weightings of one thing. They are not, and Table~\ref{tab:ls-three-losses} is
the inventory: what each reads, what it optimizes, in what units it is wrong,
and who consumes the answer.

incapable proposal); that is Thm.~\ref{thm:split-ranker}. Oversight regret and
successor continuation both score compactions of one trace and disagree on the
pair (routine working detail, dead-end breach attempt); that is
Thm.~\ref{thm:split-digest}. Directory fit and successor continuation never
meet: they do not range over the same objects, so there is no impossibility to prove
between them and none is claimed. The general statement underneath both
crossings is comonotonicity (Thm.~\ref{thm:comonotone}), which is why this
chapter has one theorem with two applications rather than two coincidences.

\subsection{Existence: the yellow pages (a solved shape)}\label{sec:existence}

The canonical prior art is about a quarter-century old. \textbf{FIPA} (\emph{Foundation for
Intelligent Physical Agents}) made a \textbf{Directory Facilitator} (DF),
\emph{the mandatory ``yellow pages'' agent: others register the services they offer
and query to find agents that offer a needed service} (FIPA Agent Management
Specification, FIPA00023~\pdcite{fipa00023}), a required component of every conformant
platform, alongside the \textbf{Agent Management System} (AMS, the ``white pages'' for
\emph{identity} lookup). Two FIPA choices matter: the DF is \emph{push-based} (agents
self-advertise), and DFs may \emph{federate} (the seed of cross-operator discovery).
The DF has been re-invented almost beat-for-beat for LLM agents
(\textbf{Huang et al.\ 2025}, \emph{Agent Name Service}~\pdcite{huang2025ans}: \emph{a
DNS-inspired naming and capability-resolution layer with public-key-backed identity},
which is FIPA's DF with a 2025 threat model).

The existence layer is the easy part: a coordination database that records the live
population, their stated purpose, and their file claims is a white-pages directory,
and a vector index that embeds each agent's declared capabilities and answers
nearest-neighbor queries is a yellow-pages directory over skills. The gap lies in
the layers above existence.

\subsection{Relevance: the split ranker (the headline result)}\label{sec:relevance}

A flat registry answers ``does an OAuth specialist exist?'' It does not answer ``of
the fifty live agents, who should I ask about \emph{this} OAuth bug, given who
touched these files an hour ago and who wrote the design note?'' That is a
\emph{ranking} problem. The \pdterm{discovery ranker} scores each candidate $x$
against a query $q$ as a clamped weighted sum of normalized sub-scores:
\begin{align}\label{eq:fit}
z(x,q) ={}& w_f\,\mathrm{file}(x,q) + w_n\,\mathrm{note}(x,q)
  + w_i\,\mathrm{ident}(x,q) \nonumber\\
 &+ w_s\,\mathrm{skill}(x,q) + w_e\,\mathrm{epis}(x,q)
  - w_\ell\,\mathrm{load}(x), \nonumber\\
\mathrm{fit}(x,q) ={}& \mathrm{clamp}\!\bigl(z(x,q),0,1\bigr).
\end{align}

\begin{pdclaim}{Theorem}{No Universal Monotone Identification of the Two Heads}
\setlength{\parskip}{4pt plus 1pt}
\label{thm:split-ranker}
On any item domain containing both high-fit/low-risk and low-fit/high-risk examples,
there is no strictly increasing scalar transform that identifies discovery fit with
operator regret.

The two rankers may share candidate generation and recency decay,
but apply distinct scoring heads. Discovery uses capability fit
(Eq.~\ref{eq:fit}); the attention queue uses regret-if-ignored:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:regret}
\mathrm{regret}(x) \;=\; \underbrace{\mathrm{stakes}(x)}_{\text{magnitude}}
   \times \underbrace{\mathrm{irreversibility}(x)}_{v^{-1}}
   \times \underbrace{\mathrm{anomaly}(x)}_{\text{surprise}}.
\end{equation}

No universal monotone transform of one scalar yields the other on that domain.
\end{pdclaim}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
\setlength{\parskip}{4pt plus 1pt}
Suppose, for contradiction, that there is a strictly increasing map $\phi$ with
$\mathrm{regret}(x)=\phi(\mathrm{fit}(x))$ for all items $x$, so the two heads induce
the same ranking.

Construct two items. Item $A$ is a highly capable, perfectly
on-topic completion of a safe, fully reversible task: $\mathrm{fit}(A)$ is maximal,
but $\mathrm{stakes}(A)\cdot\mathrm{irreversibility}(A)\cdot\mathrm{anomaly}(A)$ is
near zero, so $\mathrm{regret}(A)$ is minimal. Item $B$ is an off-topic, low-capability
agent that has nonetheless just proposed an irreversible, high-stakes, anomalous
action: $\mathrm{fit}(B)$ is low, but $\mathrm{regret}(B)$ is maximal.

Then
$\mathrm{fit}(A)>\mathrm{fit}(B)$ while $\mathrm{regret}(A)<\mathrm{regret}(B)$, so the
two heads order $\{A,B\}$ oppositely. No increasing $\phi$ can both preserve and reverse
an order, contradiction.

Hence the heads optimize genuinely different objectives; they
may share candidate generation and decay, but the scoring head must be chosen per
reader.
\end{proof}

\noindent The split ranker, one candidate list with a scoring head per reader,
is the arrangement the theorem calls for.

\begin{pdexample}{Fit and Regret for the Same Three Candidates}
\setlength{\parskip}{4pt plus 1pt}
This is a constructed example with stipulated weights and scores. For the
query ``note display bug in \path{lib/briefing.ts}'' take $w_f=0.30$,
$w_n=0.15$, $w_i=0.10$, $w_s=0.15$, $w_e=0.30$ and $w_\ell=0.20$. Candidate A
owns the briefing code (file $1.0$, note $0.7$, ident $0.5$, skill $0.6$, epis
$0.8$, load $0.5$):
\[
z(A)=0.30+0.105+0.05+0.09+0.24-0.10=0.685.
\]
B works on \path{lib/attention.ts} (scores $0$, $0.4$, $0.5$, $0.6$, $0.3$,
$0.2$), so $z(B)=0.25$; C works on \path{lib/resurrection.ts} ($0$, $0.2$,
$0.3$, $0.3$, $0.1$, $0$), so $z(C)=0.135$. All three lie in $[0,1]$, so fit
equals $z$.

Now score the three proposals as Mira's queue does, with regret equal to
stakes times irreversibility times anomaly. A's reversible display fix gives
$2\times0.1\times0.05=0.01$; B's change that marks unread messages seen gives
$8\times0.9\times0.4=2.88$; C's comment correction gives
$0.5\times0.1\times0.02=0.001$. The router picks A and Mira's queue picks B.

\[
(0.685-0.25)(0.01-2.88)\approx-1.25<0,
\]
the two scores order A and B oppositely. That is the strict reversal of the

\end{pdexample}

The argument above shows only that \emph{this} pair of heads cannot be
identified. The general characterization below states when sharing is safe.

\paragraph{When agreement is enough.}
The left panel of Figure~\ref{fig:split-ranker}, where Mira's order agrees with
the router's, gives the useful converse: different objectives can share a
ranking when their strict preferences never oppose each other. This is
\textbf{comonotonicity}, the concept used in Yaari's rank-dependent utility
and Schmeidler's Choquet expected utility~\pdcite{yaari1987,schmeidler1989}.
For priority scores $h_1$ and $h_2$, both oriented so larger means higher
priority, the condition is
$(h_1(x)-h_1(y))(h_2(x)-h_2(y))\geq 0$ for every pair. A negative product is a
strict reversal; a zero product may be a tie and is not a contradiction.
Thus compatible orders can have different ties: they need a common
refinement, not identical numerical scores or identical indifference
classes. Sharing an ordering also does not calibrate fit and regret into
the same units. The following finite ranking statement borrows this
concept, not the utility representation theorems of those authors.

\begin{pdclaim}{Theorem}{Comonotone Characterization}
\setlength{\parskip}{4pt plus 1pt}
\label{thm:comonotone}
Let $\mathcal X$ be a finite item set and suppose each reader's optimal selections at
every budget $m$ are the top-$m$ sets of a total preorder of item priorities.

A scalar head $h:\mathcal X\to\mathbb R$ \emph{serves} a reader if its first $m$ items,
under one fixed tie-breaking order, are optimal for every $m$.

A single head serves both readers iff their priority orders are comonotone:
no pair is strictly preferred in opposite directions. Ties may be refined
in an order both accept. Mira's right-panel preferences violate the
condition on A and B, so no shared head serves those two orders.
\end{pdclaim}
\begin{proof}
\setlength{\parskip}{4pt plus 1pt}
($\Leftarrow$) Sort by the first reader's priority and resolve each tie by
the second reader's priority. Without a strict reversal, this order
preserves every strict comparison of both readers. Assign decreasing
scalar values along it; each prefix is optimal for both.

($\Rightarrow$) Suppose one reader strictly prefers $x$ to $y$ and the
other strictly prefers $y$ to $x$. Whichever item the shared order places
first, the prefix ending there includes it without the other. Exchanging
the two improves that prefix for one reader, so it cannot be optimal at
that budget. Hence a shared head cannot contain a strict reversal.
\end{proof}
The right panel, where the router puts A first and Mira puts B first,
shows that the two objectives \emph{can} disagree; whether a given pair of
real queues does must be checked. The theorem forbids sharing only when a
strict reversal actually occurs, and the left panel shows that different
objective names alone do not create one.

The design must preserve the distinction between collaborator fit and the value
of inspection. When their priorities strictly reverse, one fixed ordering cannot
serve both readers at every budget. When they are compatible, a shared ordering
may serve both; their scores still need separate interpretations.

\paragraph{The regret head is derived, not designed.} The product form
$\mathrm{stakes}\times\mathrm{irreversibility}\times\mathrm{anomaly}$ looks like a
designer's convenient heuristic. Its expected-loss interpretation follows under the inspection model below,
which requires ``anomaly'' to be a calibrated probability. The analogy
is a smoke detector whose alert triggers an inspection. Its decision depends
on the posterior danger probability and three costs: a missed fire, a false
alarm, and the effort of inspecting. Three relations transfer. Expected loss
is magnitude times probability, so the alarm must weigh what it would cost to
be wrong as well as how unusual the reading is. With false-alarm and
inspection costs fixed, raising the miss cost weakly lowers the threshold;
Eq.~\ref{eq:bayes-regret} gives the exact condition for a strict decrease. A false alarm alone is no sign of miscalibration: even a calibrated
posterior can cross a low decision threshold when fires are costly. Calibration
and the choice of threshold need separate checks. Better sensing changes the evidence; the costs set the response. If the
sensor perfectly identifies safe and dangerous items, false alarms need not
occur; for a known danger, the model below still compares inspection cost
with the harm inspection would avert.

\begin{pdclaim}{Theorem}{The Regret Head}
\setlength{\parskip}{4pt plus 1pt}
\label{thm:regret-head}
For an unreviewed item $x$ let $C_{\mathrm{miss}}(x)=\mathrm{stakes}(x)\cdot
\mathrm{irrev}(x)$ be the unrecoverable loss of passing a bad $x$, let
$a(x)=\Pr(\mathsf{bad}\mid\text{evidence}(x))$ be a \emph{calibrated} posterior,
$c_{\mathrm{att}}$ the marginal attention cost, and $C_{\mathrm{fa}}$ the
non-attention cost of inspecting a good item, with all costs nonnegative.
Assume inspection completely averts the bad item's loss, passing a good item
has zero loss, and inspection has no costs beyond those stated. If inspection
can miss or only partly remedy a fault, its remaining expected loss must also
enter the comparison. Use inspect-on-tie throughout.

The expected unrecoverable loss of
passing is exactly $C_{\mathrm{miss}}(x)\,a(x)$, and, among all policies that
decide inspect-or-pass per item from its evidence alone, the Bayes-optimal policy
inspects iff
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bayes-regret}
\begin{aligned}
C_{\mathrm{miss}}(x)\,a(x)&\;\ge\;c_{\mathrm{att}}+C_{\mathrm{fa}}\bigl(1-a(x)\bigr),\\
\text{equivalently}\qquad
a(x)&\;\ge\;\frac{c_{\mathrm{att}}+C_{\mathrm{fa}}}
 {C_{\mathrm{miss}}(x)+C_{\mathrm{fa}}},
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where the second line assumes $C_{\mathrm{miss}}(x)+C_{\mathrm{fa}}>0$.

This is the Bayes cost threshold. If both loss terms are zero, inspect only
when attention is also free, using the stated inspect-on-tie rule. When
$\mathrm{anomaly}=a$, the product in Eq.~\ref{eq:regret} equals the expected
loss of passing. Equality of numerical risk estimates is stronger than
agreement of rankings: multiplying every anomaly by the same positive
constant preserves this product's ordering. An uncalibrated score alone does
not determine the Bayes threshold; its posterior interpretation must be
established separately.

For a finite batch with additive expected losses and at most $n_{\mathrm{slot}}$ equal-cost
inspection slots, define the benefit of inspecting item $i$ by
\[
  b_i=C_{\mathrm{miss},i}a_i-c_{\mathrm{att},i}
       -C_{\mathrm{fa},i}(1-a_i).
\]
Under the same inspection model, an optimal allocation selects up to $n_{\mathrm{slot}}$
items with the largest positive $b_i$. Items with zero benefit may be included
without changing expected loss. This allocation assumes that inspecting one
item does not change another item's costs or evidence. Unequal resource
requirements lead to a separate constrained allocation problem.
\end{pdclaim}
\begin{proof}
\setlength{\parskip}{4pt plus 1pt}
The expected loss of passing is $C_{\mathrm{miss}}(x)a(x)$; the expected cost of
inspecting is $c_{\mathrm{att}}+C_{\mathrm{fa}}(1-a(x))$. Collect the terms in $a(x)$
to obtain $(C_{\mathrm{miss}}(x)+C_{\mathrm{fa}})a(x)\ge
c_{\mathrm{att}}+C_{\mathrm{fa}}$.

For the batch, the expected loss without inspection is fixed, and inspecting
a set of items reduces it by the sum of their benefits $b_i$. A negative-benefit inspection can
be removed; replacing a selected lower-benefit item by an unselected
higher-benefit item improves the objective. Repeated exchanges give the
stated allocation. No independence between item outcomes is needed for this
additive expectation, but the no-cross-effect assumption is essential.
\end{proof}

Holding attention and false-alarm costs fixed, increasing the miss cost
weakly lowers the inspection threshold in Eq.~\ref{eq:bayes-regret}; the decrease
is strict when $c_{\mathrm{att}}+C_{\mathrm{fa}}>0$. A larger miss-to-false-alarm
cost ratio alone does not fix that direction, because its denominator
may have changed too. The per-item rule is a single-shot Bayesian decision rule. Sequential
oversight needs its own observation and stopping model. Wald's sequential
probability ratio test~\pdcite{wald1947} supplies a classical benchmark under
specified simple hypotheses and sampling assumptions; its optimality does
not automatically transfer to adaptive or dependent agent telemetry.



\noindent\textbf{What the threshold result says.} When the miss cost rises, the
inspection \emph{threshold} falls; whether that means more opens depends entirely
on where the posterior mass sits. An uncalibrated anomaly score does not justify treating its numerical
value as $a$. Miscalibration changes the estimated passing loss by
$C_{\mathrm{miss}}(\mathrm{anomaly}-a)$ and can change the selected actions.
Fitting the calibration map from anomaly score to danger probability $a$, using the audit log, is an obligation of the design that needs measurement.

\begin{pdclaim}{Theorem}{Reputation under Fixed Inspection Costs}
\setlength{\parskip}{4pt plus 1pt}
\label{thm:reputation-posterior}
Under Theorem~\ref{thm:regret-head}'s inspection model, suppose reputation
$r$ changes only the posterior $a(e,r)=\Pr(\mathsf{bad}\mid e,r)$; costs are
fixed. Its contribution to the optimal decision is through
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 b(e,r)&=C_{\mathrm{miss}}a(e,r)-c_{\mathrm{att}}
            -C_{\mathrm{fa}}(1-a(e,r)),\\
 \text{inspect}&\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad b(e,r)\ge0.
 \end{aligned}
\]
If $a(e,r)\ge\epsilon>0$, inspection follows whenever
\[
 C_{\mathrm{miss}}\ge
 \frac{c_{\mathrm{att}}+C_{\mathrm{fa}}(1-\epsilon)}{\epsilon}.
\]
The large-stakes conclusion assumes the other costs stay bounded
independently of the stakes.
\end{pdclaim}
\begin{proof}
Substitute the reputation-conditioned posterior into the Bayes rule.
The benefit is increasing in $a$ because $C_{\mathrm{miss}}+C_{\mathrm{fa}}
\ge0$; substituting its lower bound proves the second inequality.
\end{proof}
The optimal decision does not have a unique numerical representation: positive
rescaling of $b$ preserves its sign and ordering. A model in which reputation
also changes inspection costs must state those costs. A mandatory stakes gate
is a separate policy constraint when no positive posterior lower bound is
available.

\begin{protocol}{Attention allocation under the stated loss model}
On each refresh, for the current candidate set:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt]
\item \textbf{Assemble evidence.} Record provenance and freshness; use them
in a calibrated posterior. A recency weight alone is not a probability.
\item \textbf{Compute loss and benefit.} Display expected passing loss
$C_{\mathrm{miss}}a$ and compute the inspection benefit
$b=C_{\mathrm{miss}}a-c_{\mathrm{att}}-C_{\mathrm{fa}}(1-a)$.
\item \textbf{Allocate.} For at most $n_{\mathrm{slot}}$ equal-resource inspection slots,
select the largest positive benefits. With unequal resource requirements,
solve the corresponding constrained allocation problem.
\item \textbf{Apply declared overrides.} Mandatory review or distress-lane
rules are additional policy constraints. Record their resource use and the
reason for every suppressed item; do not credit an override to the
unconstrained Bayes theorem.
\end{enumerate}
\end{protocol}

\subsection{The quality gates: refuse-to-route and the costly escalation}\label{sec:gates}

Two gates carry most of the design's trustworthiness. \textbf{Refuse-to-route:} if
every candidate falls below a confidence threshold, the ranker returns
empty-with-explanation, not a confident wrong answer: ``I don't know'' must beat a
guess, because sending an agent to the wrong door is high-cost and silent. This is the
exact dual of the completion gate's fail-loud (Invariant~\ref{def:completionist}): both are
the same refuse-to-guess discipline, one on the read side, one on the authority side.
\textbf{The costly escalation:} a stakes-ranked attention queue creates an
incentive to exaggerate distress. Strathern's discussion of target-driven
measurement~\pdcite{strathern1997} motivates testing for that distortion in this
queue. A proposed standing
debit for an adjudicated false escalation could make manipulation costly.
Mere dismissal is insufficient evidence of falsity: penalizing it may suppress
valid warnings. The design therefore owes an adjudication rule, an appeal path,
and tests of both manipulation and missed warnings. This proposed sanction is
not the pretrusted-peer mechanism of EigenTrust, a peer-to-peer reputation
algorithm (\S\ref{sec:related}), and has no guarantee from that algorithm.

\subsection{Staleness and decay on every read-surface}\label{sec:staleness}

A recurring failure in real fleets makes the point concretely. An agent process dies
or hits a provider usage limit, but its file claim remains on the books; hours later
that \emph{dead} claim still reads as ``current'' and a live agent is blocked from a
file nobody is actually working in. This is a staleness bug, and it generalizes to
every projection. The fix already exists in the pheromone layer, where the
traces agents leave for one another fade with time; the invariant below applies
that decay everywhere.

\begin{pdclaim}{Design invariant}{Universal Recency-Decay}
\setlength{\parskip}{4pt plus 1pt}
\label{prop:decay}
Every read-surface projection (briefing, resurrection handoff, discovery result,
attestation report) carries an explicit TTL or recency-decay.

A pull signal
contributes $\propto \exp(-\lambda \cdot \text{hoursAgo})$ with class-specific
half-lives (e.g.\ a short half-life for file claims, longer for episodic memory,
notes given an explicit retention policy). Decay changes ranking weight; it
does not remove authority. A stale high score can still exceed a low fresh
score. Expired leases must instead become ineligible, and effects must reject
their old fencing epochs. Open obligations remain visible until resolved or
validly transferred; they do not expire merely because they are old.

This is not a new mechanism but a uniform application of the decay primitive that
already governs the pheromone layer, and it is the production discipline of every
health-checked, TTL-expiring service registry~\pdcite{consul}.
\end{pdclaim}

The dead-claim case is the worked instance: had the resurrection handoff and the
claim projection carried Invariant~\ref{prop:decay}, the dead agent's claim would have
become ineligible under its lease and fencing policy rather than blocking
a live agent indefinitely. Decay is easy to apply to a
forgetting layer and easy to forget on a digest; Invariant~\ref{prop:decay} closes that
gap by making it universal.

\subsection{Ambient legibility: a proposed alert channel}\label{sec:ambient}

A compact indicator can signal a status transition without requiring the operator
to keep a dense console open. A menu-bar icon still uses vision; it is not
a separate sensory modality merely because it occupies another part of the
screen. An optional sound uses another modality, but its benefit depends on
competing tasks and the conditions discussed in \S\ref{sec:operator}.

\textbf{Treisman \& Gelade's} feature-integration experiments~\pdcite{treisman1980}
distinguish searches for separable features from searches that require combining
features with focused attention. They motivate testing whether an alert is easy
to distinguish among its actual distractors; they give no universal
200--500\,ms detection time for this interface. The design hypothesis is that a
low-clutter indicator with an optional alternative cue improves detection of
important transitions at acceptable interruption cost. Test detection and false
alarms in context, including accessibility and reduced-motion preferences, before
making it the primary alarm path.

\section{Tokens: the swarm's cost \emph{and} its legibility}\label{sec:tokens}
% ═════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

One important metered input to a coding swarm is \textbf{tokens}, \emph{the sub-word units an LLM reads and writes,
billed per million} (\textbf{Sennrich et al.\ 2016}, byte-pair
encoding~\pdcite{sennrich2016}). Tokens contribute to the swarm's \pdterm{cost of goods sold}, its direct
variable costs (compute, tool use, and human review may also matter). They also
decide its legibility, because what stays in an agent's context is what can be
shown to a reader. Compaction connects the two.

\keyidea[Compaction decides what remains visible]{The same compaction choice affects the bill and determines which evidence remains immediately visible. When the context window fills you must drop something, and what
you drop is a \emph{cost} decision (you stop paying to carry it) \emph{and} a
\emph{legibility} decision (whoever reads the residue no longer sees it). The act
that resolves both is compaction, and compaction's output is exactly the digest a
human or successor agent reads. \emph{The digest IS the compaction.}}

\subsection{Tokens as COGS, and the effective-context trap}\label{sec:cogs}

For a fleet the unit of output may be ``a landed piece of work''. One
variable cost is inference tokens (input context $+$ output generation, each priced
per million), the textbook shape of \textbf{COGS}. Every price an agent market can
set (a rented fleet's margin, an agent-for-hire's outcome-per-token) needs a COGS
line, so context economics is the \emph{denominator} of the market, not a side metric.
There is a cost trap: a supported context length is not a guarantee of useful
retrieval throughout that context. Some evaluated models and tasks degrade within it (\textbf{Liu et al.\ 2024}, \emph{Lost in
the Middle}~\pdcite{liu2024}: \emph{performance is highest at the beginning and end
of context and degrades for information in the middle, even in long-context models};
\textbf{Anthropic 2025} names the same effect \emph{context rot}~\pdcite{anthropic2025}).
These are empirical quality effects. Dense attention's quadratic arithmetic
cost~\pdcite{vaswani2017} is a separate computational fact and sets no
universal limit on the usable window. The effective budget must
be measured for the model, task, placement, and retrieval procedure. Removing redundant context can lower cost and improve retrieval together.
Dropping a required obligation can instead lower cost while making the result
wrong; quality and cost must both be checked. The primitive the market ultimately needs is a \emph{per-agent-task token
ledger keyed to outcomes} (``this task spent 84K input plus 12K output to land
that change''), so that cost can be attributed to a witnessed result rather than to
an opaque monthly bill.

\subsection{Compaction produces the digest}\label{sec:tokens-digest}

Compaction (\textbf{Anthropic 2025}~\pdcite{anthropic2025}: \emph{taking a
conversation nearing the window limit, summarizing it, and reinitiating with the
summary}, with the discipline \emph{maximize recall first, then optimize precision})
is the canonical move for keeping a long-horizon agent under budget. Its siblings,
structured note-taking (external memory) and sub-agent context isolation (the parent
never sees the bloat), complete the family. \emph{Summaries of the same trajectory serve different readers.} The
operator reads a digest, not six transcripts; the seventh agent reads a briefing, not
the first six's scrollback; a crashed agent's successor reads a handoff, not the dead
agent's context. Each is a summary of dropped tokens produced for a specific reader.
So compaction is the legibility mechanism of the \emph{entire swarm}: the same act
that keeps the bill down produces the very digest a human or successor agent reads
(Table~\ref{tab:ls-read-surfaces}).

Give Heron's successor room for one of two records: the next repair action,
or the evidence that Drake deleted the acceptance test. In a deliberately small
model, forgetting the next-action record costs one bit of predictive loss, while
omitting the incident incurs a stipulated oversight loss of ten units. Retaining
neither gives losses $(1,10)$; retaining the work record gives $(0,10)$; retaining
the audit record gives $(1,0)$. The two readers choose different records.

These units are different objectives, not measured harms or token costs. The
following theorem states the restricted summary class that makes the choice
unavoidable; a shared store or a larger budget can change it.

\begin{pdclaim}{Theorem}{An Extractive Split-Digest Counterexample}
\label{thm:split-digest}
Let a trace contain two unit-size records, $E=\{e_{\mathrm{work}},e_{\mathrm{audit}}\}$. Restrict summaries to retained records $D\subseteq E$ with $|D|\le1$; the feasible summaries are exactly $\emptyset$, $\{e_{\mathrm{work}}\}$, and $\{e_{\mathrm{audit}}\}$.

The work record gives a next-action bit $X_{\mathrm{next}}\in\{0,1\}$. The full trace and any summary retaining that record predict $X_{\mathrm{next}}$ with probability one; a summary omitting it predicts each bit with probability $1/2$. Define continuation loss by base-two relative entropy:
\[
L_{\mathrm{succ}}(D)=\mathrm{KL}_2(P_{\mathrm{next}}(\cdot\mid E)\|P_{\mathrm{next}}(\cdot\mid D))
=\mathbf1\{e_{\mathrm{work}}\notin D\}.
\]
Here relative entropy is the expected log-ratio of the two predictive probabilities, in bits. Define oversight loss by a stipulated audit-priority weight:
\[
L_{\mathrm{op}}(D)=10\,\mathbf1\{e_{\mathrm{audit}}\notin D\}.
\]
The value $10$ expresses the operator's requirement to retain that incident; it is not an estimate of future irreversible harm. Under these definitions, no feasible summary minimizes both losses.
\end{pdclaim}

\begin{proof}
List the three feasible choices. Their losses $(L_{\mathrm{succ}},L_{\mathrm{op}})$ are respectively $(1,10)$, $(0,10)$, and $(1,0)$. The unique continuation minimizer retains the work record, and the unique oversight minimizer retains the audit record. They differ.
\end{proof}

\noindent The restriction to one retained record does the work. With a two-record budget, retaining both gives $(0,0)$. A general codec might also preserve both readers' sufficient information in less space than the raw trace. Thus this counterexample shows that reader objectives can conflict.
\pdscope{A shared evidence store, a jointly designed encoding, or a single implementation with two views all remain possible.}
\noindent The preference reversal recalls Theorem~\ref{thm:comonotone}, while the explicit feasible set supplies the missing budget argument.

\emph{Pause and check:} if the audit weight becomes zero, the work-only summary minimizes both objectives. Disagreement requires a real oversight obligation; two named readers are not enough. The next result studies a different question: the counting lower bounds when two readers need disjoint critical sets.

\begin{pdclaim}{Theorem}{The Pooling Gain}
\setlength{\parskip}{4pt plus 1pt}
\label{thm:pooling-gain}
Write $\mathrm{LB}(N,n,m)=\log_2\binom Nn-\log_2\binom mn$ for the zero-miss
lower bound of Theorem~\ref{thm:lowerbound} with $n$ critical items and $m$
opens. Two readers each need their own critical $k$-set, and the two sets are
disjoint. Compare two arrangements with the same total of $m$ opens: two
separate digests, each serving one reader with $m/2$ opens, and one joint
digest that must cover the union of both sets with all $m$ opens.

For integers $k\ge1$ and even $m$ with $2k\le m\le N$,
\[
\mathrm{LB}(N,2k,m)\;<\;2\,\mathrm{LB}(N,k,m/2).
\]
At an equal opening budget, the joint digest's lower bound is below the sum of
the two separate ones.
\end{pdclaim}

\begin{proof}
\setlength{\parskip}{4pt plus 1pt}
Choosing $k$ items from each half of an $m$-item review set gives distinct
$2k$-subsets of it, so $\binom m{2k}\ge\binom{m/2}k^2$. Every $2k$-subset of
the $N$ artifacts splits into an ordered pair of disjoint $k$-subsets in
$\binom{2k}k\ge2$ ways, and there are at most $\binom Nk^2$ such pairs, so
$\binom N{2k}\binom{2k}k\le\binom Nk^2$ and hence $\binom N{2k}<\binom Nk^2$.
Taking base-two logarithms of both inequalities and subtracting gives
$\mathrm{LB}(N,2k,m)<2\log_2\binom Nk-2\log_2\binom{m/2}k=2\,\mathrm{LB}(N,k,m/2)$.
\end{proof}

\noindent The budgets must match before the comparison means anything. Hold
the joint digest to one reader's $m$ opens while each separate reader also
gets $m$, and the joint bound exceeds the sum: at $N=60$, $k=2$, $m=8$ it is
$12.77$ bits against $2\times5.98=11.96$. That comparison gives the joint
digest half the opens of the pair, so it measures a shortage of opens, not a
cost of serving two readers from one store.
\pdscope{Both sides are lower bounds on message length. The inequality compares the bounds, not encoders; the paragraph on when the bound is reached, after Theorem~\ref{thm:lowerbound}, says when codebooks reach them.}

\begin{pdexample}{The Pooling Gain, at the Sweep's Own Instance}
\setlength{\parskip}{4pt plus 1pt}
\label{ex:pooling-gain}
Take $N=60$ and $k=2$, with $m=8$ opens in total. Two separate digests get
four opens each, and each one's lower bound is
\[
\mathrm{LB}(60,2,4)=\log_2\!\frac{\binom{60}{2}}{\binom42}
=\log_2\frac{1770}{6}\approx8.2045\text{ bits},
\]
so the pair needs at least $16.4091$ bits. One joint digest with all eight
opens must cover both critical pairs:
\[
\mathrm{LB}(60,4,8)=\log_2\!\frac{\binom{60}{4}}{\binom84}
=\log_2\frac{487635}{70}\approx12.7662\text{ bits}.
\]
Pooling the store and the budget lowers the bound by about $3.64$ bits. The
proof's two inequalities hold here as
$\binom84=70\ge\binom42^2=36$ and
$\binom{60}4\binom42=2{,}925{,}810\le\binom{60}2^2=3{,}132{,}900$.

These are closed-form lower bounds, not measured token costs or achieved
compression ratios.

\subsection{Context paging: virtual memory for the context window}\label{sec:context-paging}

A context window can be modeled as a cache: durable files hold records, and the window holds the records currently available to the model. A cache miss means retrieving a requested record. This analogy measures retrieval cost; it does not by itself measure whether a model understands the retrieved text.

For example, with two unit-size pages and requests $A,B,A,C,A$, least-recently-used eviction loads $A$, loads $B$, hits $A$, evicts $B$ for $C$, then hits $A$: three retrievals. The policy can use the past request order but cannot see future requests. An offline comparator may see the whole sequence. ``Competitive'' means that the online policy's total retrieval cost is bounded relative to that stronger comparator, on every sequence in the stated model.

\begin{pdclaim}{Theorem}{Classical File-Caching Bound Applied to Context Records}
\label{thm:paging}
Consider immutable files of positive integer size and fixed nonnegative retrieval cost. Let the online cache have capacity $k_{\mathrm{on}}$, the offline comparator capacity $k_{\mathrm{off}}$, and $1\le k_{\mathrm{off}}\le k_{\mathrm{on}}$. Every requested file must fit in the comparator's cache. Both caches begin empty; requesting an absent file loads it and incurs its retrieval cost.

Young's \textsc{Landlord} algorithm satisfies
\[
\mathrm{Cost}_{\mathrm{Landlord},k_{\mathrm{on}}}(\sigma_{\mathrm{req}})
\le \frac{k_{\mathrm{on}}}{k_{\mathrm{on}}-k_{\mathrm{off}}+1}\,\mathrm{OPT}_{k_{\mathrm{off}}}(\sigma_{\mathrm{req}})
\]
for every request sequence $\sigma_{\mathrm{req}}$~\pdcite{young2002landlord}. The parameter $k_{\mathrm{off}}$ is comparator capacity, not the number of pinned records. In the unit-size, unit-cost paging case, least-recently-used eviction is a specialization of this guarantee~\pdcite{sleatortarjan1985,young2002landlord}.
\end{pdclaim}

This is a classical result, not a new theorem about language-model quality. Its proof charges online retrievals to comparator retrievals through a nonnegative potential: a comparator retrieval raises the potential by at most $k_{\mathrm{on}}$ times its cost, and an online retrieval lowers it by at least $(k_{\mathrm{on}}-k_{\mathrm{off}}+1)$ times its cost. Summing over the sequence yields the ratio. The cited proof specifies the algorithm's credit updates; arbitrary ``recency plus importance'' eviction does not inherit it.

A fixed protected set can be handled explicitly. In the unit-page case, suppose the same $n_{\mathrm{pin}}<k_{\mathrm{off}}$ pages stay pinned in both caches, and charge their common initial loading cost separately. Remove requests to those pages and run the algorithm on the remaining sequence. The capacities become $k_{\mathrm{on}}-n_{\mathrm{pin}}$ and $k_{\mathrm{off}}-n_{\mathrm{pin}}$, so the unpinned retrieval costs satisfy
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{Cost}_{k_{\mathrm{on}}-n_{\mathrm{pin}}}
&\le\frac{k_{\mathrm{on}}-n_{\mathrm{pin}}}{(k_{\mathrm{on}}-n_{\mathrm{pin}})-(k_{\mathrm{off}}-n_{\mathrm{pin}})+1}\,\mathrm{OPT}_{k_{\mathrm{off}}-n_{\mathrm{pin}}}\\
&=\frac{k_{\mathrm{on}}-n_{\mathrm{pin}}}{k_{\mathrm{on}}-k_{\mathrm{off}}+1}\,\mathrm{OPT}_{k_{\mathrm{off}}-n_{\mathrm{pin}}}.
\end{aligned}
\]
This comparison requires the same fixed pins on both sides. It says nothing about how to choose the right pins, and changing or forged pins require another analysis.

\begin{pdboundary}
Context records must have explicit sizes, retrieval costs, and version identities before the cache theorem applies. A stale record served as current is a correctness error, not merely a cache miss. The repository's corruption experiment explores an additional allowance $\psi N_{\mathrm{cons}} c_{\mathrm{refetch}}$, where $N_{\mathrm{cons}}$ is the number of consultations, $\psi$ their corruption rate, and $c_{\mathrm{refetch}}$ a chosen repair-cost ceiling. Its finite sweep in \texttt{b9\_context\_paging.py} does not prove that allowance for all corruption patterns. In particular, repair-on-touch and the effect of one corruption on later evictions need a separate coupling argument. No common error budget for summary coverage and paging follows merely from giving both parameters the name $\psi$.

Online algorithms with predictions study related questions about advice quality; competitive caching with learned advice~\pdcite{lykouris2018} is a useful starting point. Its guarantees must be imported with their own algorithm and error model, not transferred by analogy.
\end{pdboundary}

\begin{pdexample}{Reading the Cache Ratio}
Take an online cache of $k_{\mathrm{on}}=8$ pages and an offline comparator of $k_{\mathrm{off}}=4$ pages, with no pins. The classical factor is $8/(8-4+1)=1.6$. Under the theorem's assumptions, \textsc{Landlord}'s retrieval cost is at most $1.6$ times the best cost with four pages. Comparing both with eight pages instead gives factor $8$: granting the comparator more memory makes the comparison harder.

If two common pages are fixed and pinned in both caches, the remaining capacities are six and two. The unpinned factor becomes $6/(6-2+1)=1.2$, against the much smaller two-page comparator. This does not prove that pinning improved the total cost; it changed the comparator too.

\emph{Now you try:} compute the unpinned factors at $k_{\mathrm{off}}=k_{\mathrm{on}}$ and $k_{\mathrm{off}}=1$ in Exercise~\ref{ex:ls-paging-ratio-hand}, and state the comparator in words before doing the division.
\end{pdexample}

The most counterintuitive instance is \emph{forgetting as compaction}.
\pdterm[pheromone decay]{Pheromone decay}, multiplying every stored coordination trace by a decay
factor each interval, implements \textbf{stigmergy}: coordination through
persistent traces left in a shared environment, as ants deposit evaporating
trails (\textbf{Grass\'e 1959}~\pdcite{grasse1959}). A lazy implementation stores each trace's timestamp and evaluates its decayed score in $O(1)$ time per record. Eagerly updating all $n$ traces costs $O(n)$ per interval. Decay can reduce the prominence of stale traces; it does not by itself remove their storage or bound the work needed to rank the full map. It is legibility by subtraction, and it is the same primitive
Invariant~\ref{prop:decay} generalizes to every read-surface.

\subsection{The context-degradation cascade (the shared failure surface)}\label{sec:cascade}

\subsection{Single-operator: account, don't auction}\label{sec:account}

Within one operator's fleet, start with accounting: attribute resource use to
jobs, enforce the granted budget at the effect boundary, and preserve an outcome
reference. Alice can then ask what Heron's accepted contribution cost without
inventing an internal auction for every tool call. Shared ownership does not
eliminate proxy objectives, misleading reports, or budget evasion; the evidence
and enforcement problems already developed in this chapter still apply.

Cross-operator exchange adds distinct counterparties, entry policies, and
settlement incentives \pdcite{nisan2007,douceur2002,ostrom1990}. Those mechanisms
need a durable party to carry the obligation. A local task budget can bind to
Alice's authority even when the worker process is temporary; persistent worker
reputation requires the additional continuity developed in
\pdchapref{stp}{From Spawn to Person}.

The Leviathan metaphor deserves its place only if it survives contact with the political-
theory canon. Three questions make its limits precise: who authorizes the system,
how consent can be withdrawn, and what local knowledge a central view cannot replace.

\subsection{Who is the multitude, who is the sovereign?}\label{sec:roles}

Hobbes' covenant is \emph{among the subjects}; the sovereign is its product, not a
party to it. So the metaphor breaks differently depending on which entity sits where
(Figure~\ref{fig:roles}).

\begin{pdexample}{One Grant, Traced through Four Steps, and the Role Each Step Forces}
\setlength{\parskip}{4pt plus 1pt}
Rather than assign the roles and then check them, run the consent-grant lifecycle of \S\ref{sec:consent} on Alice's 3:25 grant and let the assignment fall out.

\textbf{Step~1, issue:} the grant $g = \langle$\,the \texttt{tests/} subtree with the original checkout test preserved, a low stakes ceiling $s_{\max}$, a minimum reversibility $v_{\min}$ no irreversible act clears, a two-hour \textsc{ttl}\,$\rangle$ comes into existence because Alice says so, and nothing else in the system can create one, so whoever occupies this step is a source of authority rather than a user of it.

\textbf{Step~2, act:} the formatting diffs land and Finch's force-push is escalated instead, because it fails $v_{\min}$; the daemon performed both, and it did not choose the minimum it applied, so this step exercises an authority it did not author.

\textbf{Step~3, witness:} the daemon records $(g, x, \text{reason}, \text{artifact})$ for each, which only means anything if the recorder is answerable to someone it is not.

\textbf{Step~4, expire:} at 5:25 the grant leaves the active set with nobody's permission, and Alice's override would have emptied it sooner.

The four steps need exactly one participant who creates authority and can withdraw it, and exactly one who exercises it on terms set by someone else. No step ever needs the daemon to be a \emph{party} to the agents' rule of the road.

The grant issuer and the executor occupy different roles. This design assigns
them the author and actor positions in Hobbes' vocabulary; the lifecycle makes
the comparison useful, and other political readings remain possible. The itemization below names those roles.

\emph{Now you try:} map $\{$agents, daemon, operator$\}$ onto $\{$multitude, sovereign-actor, author$\}$ and say why the metaphor breaks if the operator is called the sovereign (Exercise~\ref{ex:ls-hobbes-mapping}).
\end{pdexample}

We assign roles explicitly, using Hobbes' own
author/actor distinction (\emph{Leviathan} ch.~16~\pdcite{hobbes1651}):

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em]
\item \textbf{The agents are the multitude.} They covenant, through the rule of the
road (the coordination protocol), to be bound, each to all, enforced by the daemon.
This is an engineering analogy to a covenant among subjects, not an assertion that software can give human consent.
\item \textbf{The daemon is the sovereign-as-actor.} It holds the authority the
multitude instituted; it blocks, lands, escalates. But, per the legible-sovereign
amendment (Invariant~\ref{prop:legible-sovereign}), it is the \emph{most} legible actor,
not (as in Hobbes) the least.
\item \textbf{The operator is the author.} In Hobbes' author/actor split, the
\emph{author} owns the authority that the \emph{actor} exercises. The operator is not
a peer subject and not the sovereign-in-action; the operator is the source of
authority, exercising it through the daemon and able to withdraw it
(Invariant~\ref{prop:override}). This is the slot Hobbes leaves for the one who authorizes
but does not personally act.
\end{itemize}

This assignment is what makes the consent grant (Invariant~\ref{def:consent}) coherent:
the author issues a scoped grant to the actor, over a multitude bound by covenant.

\subsection{Express consent, not tacit; voice \emph{and} exit}\label{sec:express}

\S\ref{sec:consent} already made consent a first-class object precisely to escape
Hume's tacit-consent critique; the canon demands we also engage \textbf{Locke}
(\emph{Second Treatise}, \S\S95--122~\pdcite{locke1689}: \emph{express consent binds
where tacit consent does not}) and \textbf{Pateman} (\emph{The Problem of Political
Obligation}, 1979~\pdcite{pateman1979}: \emph{liberal consent theory keeps collapsing
into hypothetical consent}). The consent grant is express by construction: a discrete,
scoped, revocable authorization with an audit trail (Invariant~\ref{def:consent}). And the
inalienable override (Invariant~\ref{prop:override}) supplies the \emph{exit} that
Hirschman~\pdcite{hirschman1970} says keeps \emph{voice} honest. The design has rich
voice machinery; the override is the missing exit, and exit is what gives the author
real leverage over the actor.

\subsection{M\^etis is Hayek, and some of it cannot be made legible}\label{sec:hayek}

Section~\ref{sec:flatten} distinguishes access to an artifact from the situated
knowledge needed to interpret it. \textbf{Hayek's} discussion of dispersed,
local knowledge~\pdcite{hayek1945} and \textbf{Scott's} critique of administrative
simplification~\pdcite{scott1998} offer complementary cautions for this design.
Read together, they warn that useful knowledge is dispersed among many people;
they do not say that one supervisor's knowledge is always the system's limiting
resource.

The application here is an interpretive synthesis: keep evidence available and
preserve opportunities for people to exercise practical judgment. Scott's
\emph{Two Cheers for Anarchism}\pdcite{scott2012} motivates that concern.
Whether the proposed skill-retention and canary interventions preserve
knowledge needs the evaluation described in \S\ref{sec:abdication}. Figure~\ref{fig:hayek-scott} maps the two cautions to this
design proposal without treating the arrows as a deduction.

\section*{Review of the key ideas}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Review of the key ideas}

What this chapter leaves coupled, in the order the rest of the book will lean on it:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em,itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{The swarm is a state of nature.} Three locally valid edits to one \texttt{settings.json} revision overlap (Figure~\ref{fig:state-of-nature}); nothing in the agents' competence prevents it, because the conflict is in the schedule, not in the work.
\item \textbf{Consent changes the schedule, not the participants.} Under consented serialization the same three edits become one commit log with a grant record each, naming actor, claim, artifact and reason.
\item \textbf{Legibility-with-zoom.} A digest is a lens, never a replacement: every claim in it resolves to an artifact, and a digest whose claims resolve to nothing is a facade, however green.
\item \textbf{The authority half.} The daemon gates or escalates on every act under a grant the operator gave once; the override is a single edge from operator to daemon, and the escalation threshold has a tuning band, not a point.
\item \textbf{Specialization has a boundary.} A pooled queue beats sole ownership only when utilization and the skill advantage put the operator on one side of the queueing boundary; the worked point $(\rho,r_{\mathrm{sk}})=(0.556,\,2.5)$ clears a boundary of $2.483$ and so falls, barely, on sole ownership's side, while the same pool against a $2.8/\text{hr}$ specialist ($r_{\mathrm{sk}}=2.33$) falls on the pool's.
\item \textbf{Reading can become the bottleneck.} The zero-miss message
lower bound depends on both the critical set and the number of permitted opens. At
$N=60,m=8$, the lower bounds for two and four critical items are $5.98$ and
$12.77$ bits; with sixteen opens the four-item bound falls to $8.07$ bits.
These are different coverage requirements, not measured compression savings
between two architectures.
\item \textbf{Discovery and inspection ask different questions.} A strict
priority reversal prevents one ordering from serving both readers at every
budget; compatible priorities can share an ordering. Inspection still needs
calibrated error probabilities and costs.
\item \textbf{Compaction serves distinct readers.} The extractive
counterexample shows why a one-record budget can force conflicting choices.
At an equal opening budget, one shared digest for two readers has a lower
zero-miss bound than two separate digests (Theorem~\ref{thm:pooling-gain}). The classical
cache bound controls retrieval cost for its specified algorithm and comparator,
not comprehension or arbitrary pinning policies.
\item \textbf{Several authors, one sovereign.} Many principals keep the single
writer; what they add is one charter, one owner per scope, and a delegation
tree. Escalation has a well-defined path exactly when delegation is a tree.
\end{enumerate}

\pdexercisesfor{\S\ref{sec:intro}}{Introduction: the swarm is a state of nature}

\begin{pdexercise}[kind=Check,rating=1]{ex:ls-taxonomy-five-failures}
State, in one sentence each, the five state-of-nature failures of
Table~\ref{tab:taxonomy} and name the class of primitive that addresses each.
\end{pdexercise}
\begin{pdsolution}{ex:ls-taxonomy-five-failures}
Resource conflict (two agents touch the same resource and clobber one another) is answered by conflict-aware claims plus actual exclusion at the effect boundary. Illegibility (work cannot be understood in bounded time) is answered by a digest whose every material claim zooms to independent evidence. Confident wrongness (an agent asserts completion without checking) is answered by typed attestation and oracle-bound completion. Footguns (a stale premise drives an irreversible act) are answered by reversibility/stakes gates, scoped authority, and pre-effect mediation. Illegible authority (the coordinator rules without an inspectable reason) is answered by an append-only, named, zoomable decision record, including denials and omissions.
\end{pdsolution}
\begin{pdexercise}[kind=Check,rating=1]{ex:ls-cooperative-clobber}
Hobbes says the actors ``need not be malicious.'' Give a concrete two-agent
scenario in which both agents are perfectly cooperative and the swarm still
deadlocks or clobbers state.
\end{pdexercise}
\begin{pdsolution}{ex:ls-cooperative-clobber}
Wren and Heron are both asked to improve \texttt{auth.ts}. Each reads the same base revision, makes a locally correct edit, and commits; without a claim or merge serialization, Heron's whole-file rewrite erases Wren's change, and no malice is required. A deadlock variant: two cooperative agents take claims on files $X$ and $Y$ in opposite order and each waits indefinitely for the other's claim.
\end{pdsolution}
\begin{pdexercise}[kind=Trace,rating=2]{ex:ls-deliberate-vs-emergent}
Dai et al.~\pdcite{dai2024} observe LLM societies \emph{spontaneously} reaching
a consented sovereign. If the attractor is emergent, why build one deliberately? Give
two reasons grounded in \S\ref{sec:local} (hint: \emph{which} sovereign, and
\emph{legible to whom}).
\end{pdexercise}
\begin{pdsolution}{ex:ls-deliberate-vs-emergent}
Deliberate construction chooses the authority rather than accepting whatever coalition happens to win, and makes that authority legible to the operator. An emergent stable order may instead be an oligarchy: stable, but controlled by a few agents, unrevocable, and opaque. The local daemon of \S\ref{sec:local} supplies what an emergent attractor does not guarantee: one fault domain, one policy root, and an unconditional operator exit.
\end{pdsolution}
\begin{pdexercise}[kind=Open,rating=3]{ex:ls-sovereign-vs-oligarchy-experiment}
The Dai et al.\ result is a single controlled study. Design an
experiment over a real coding-agent fleet that would distinguish ``the swarm reaches
a consented sovereign'' from ``the swarm reaches a stable but \emph{illegible}
oligarchy.'' What is the observable that separates the two?
\end{pdexercise}
\begin{pdsolution}{ex:ls-sovereign-vs-oligarchy-experiment}
Randomize comparable real repository tasks to (a) an ungoverned fleet and (b) a fleet with only a transport but no prescribed governance; record all messages, effects, blocking decisions, and operator interventions. Pre-register two observable classes: \emph{sovereign} characteristics (a publicly known rule, general applicability, explicit consent or adoption, stable appeal/override, reasons attached to authority acts, equal audit access) against \emph{oligarchy} characteristics (authority concentrated in a small coalition, private channels or rules, selective enforcement, no effective exit, outcomes outsiders cannot reconstruct). Measure concentration of blocking/merging power, the fraction of authority acts with reconstructable reasons, rule consistency across agents, override success, artifact-linked claim accuracy, and operator reconstruction accuracy. Stability alone does not separate the hypotheses; publicly inspectable and revocable rule-governed authority does.
\end{pdsolution}

\pdexercisesfor{\S\ref{sec:authority}}{Consent to the Leviathan (the normative claim)}

\begin{pdexercise}[kind=Check,rating=1]{ex:ls-express-vs-tacit-consent}
Distinguish \emph{express} from \emph{tacit} consent and explain why
Invariant~\ref{def:consent} is the former.
\end{pdexercise}
\begin{pdsolution}{ex:ls-express-vs-tacit-consent}
Tacit consent is inferred from remaining in or using a system. Express consent is a discrete act over named terms. Invariant~\ref{def:consent} is express because the operator affirmatively issues a grant with scope, stakes ceiling, minimum reversibility, TTL, and revocation semantics, none of which a mere pattern of continued use supplies.
\end{pdsolution}
\begin{pdexercise}[kind=Check,rating=1]{ex:ls-override-unsuppressable}
Why must the operator-override
(Invariant~\ref{prop:override}) be un-suppressable by \emph{any} autonomy level? What
attack does a suppressible override enable?
\end{pdexercise}
\begin{pdsolution}{ex:ls-override-unsuppressable}
If an autonomy level could suppress the override, it could make its own authority irrevocable: keep executing, hide the stop channel, or race destructive effects after consent is withdrawn. The override must be out-of-band, higher priority than agent work, and coupled to effect revocation, not merely a UI flag.
\end{pdsolution}
\begin{pdexercise}[kind=Trace,rating=2]{ex:ls-consent-lifecycle}
Walk a single consent grant through its lifecycle: issuance, an action the
daemon takes under it, the audit record produced, and revocation. At which step does
the legible-sovereign rule (Invariant~\ref{prop:legible-sovereign}) bind?
\end{pdexercise}
\begin{pdsolution}{ex:ls-consent-lifecycle}
The operator signs grant $g$; the daemon validates and records it; an eligible low-stakes action cites $g$; the daemon mediates the effect; the receipt records action, reason, artifact, and grant version; expiry or revocation removes $g$ from the active set and fences later effects. The legible-sovereign rule binds at every authority transition, especially issue/revoke and allow/deny; the explicit witness record is where reconstructability is discharged.
\end{pdsolution}
\begin{pdexercise}[kind=Open,rating=3]{ex:ls-ranker-counter-surface}
Invariant~\ref{prop:legible-sovereign} has a regress: the ranker that
decides what the operator sees is itself an opaque exercise of authority. Specify
the ``what did the queue suppress, and why'' counter-surface concretely. What is its
own staleness/decay discipline (forward-reference \S\ref{sec:staleness}), and does
\emph{it} need a counter-surface, ad infinitum?
\end{pdexercise}
\begin{pdsolution}{ex:ls-ranker-counter-surface}
Use one complete append-only decision ledger, not a second ranked dashboard. For every candidate item, store candidate ID, source/evidence pointers, policy and model version, input features, score components, threshold/lane, surfaced-or-suppressed verdict, reason codes, expiry, and a counterfactual such as ``would surface if anomaly were 0.08 higher.'' The operator can query suppressed items since a cursor, sort by any raw field, and sample items uniformly rather than through the production ranker. Decisions are immutable; their current relevance expires by signal-specific TTL (\S\ref{sec:staleness}) and is recomputed from raw state. Run independent random omission audits and expose raw event-log access. The regress terminates at completeness plus direct sampling: the base ledger records every candidate and every ranking decision, and the counter-surface is a deterministic query over it, so no further ranker is needed to rank the ranker's omissions. The remaining trust assumption is the complete event-capture boundary; that assumption should be tested and externally anchored, not hidden behind another summary. (Same construction answers Exercise~\ref{ex:ls-ranker-political-organ}.)
\end{pdsolution}

\pdexercisesfor{\S\ref{sec:zoom}}{The mechanism: legibility-with-zoom}

\begin{pdexercise}[kind=Check,rating=1]{ex:ls-flatten-or-preserve}
Classify each as \emph{flatten} or \emph{preserve verbatim}: a port number;
the stderr of a failed test; ``the auth refactor felt risky so I split it''; a
\texttt{commitment} status enum; a 600-line diff.
\end{pdexercise}
\begin{pdsolution}{ex:ls-flatten-or-preserve}
Port number: flatten, as structured administrative state. Failed-test stderr: preserve verbatim or by a content-addressed pointer. ``The auth refactor felt risky so I split it'': preserve verbatim and label it self-narration, not fact. Commitment-status enum: flatten. The 600-line diff: do not paraphrase it away. Retain the immutable diff or a verified pointer and summarize only administrative metadata.
\end{pdsolution}
\begin{pdexercise}[kind=Check,rating=1]{ex:ls-two-llms-not-independent}
Why does ``two LLMs agreeing'' not by itself establish two independent
checks (Invariant~\ref{prop:verifiable-zoom})?
\end{pdexercise}
\begin{pdsolution}{ex:ls-two-llms-not-independent}
Two models may share training biases, prompts, sources, and failure modes. Agreement alone does not determine how often both checks miss the same defect. A check against repository state or a protected test result can add evidence beyond the original narration. A separately controlled reviewer can reduce conflicts of interest, but separate control does not itself prove independent errors; the evidence and common failure modes still need examination.
\end{pdsolution}
\begin{pdexercise}[kind=Trace,rating=2]{ex:ls-four-questions-dashboard}
Take a read-surface you use (a CI dashboard, a PR summary) and run the four
questions of \S\ref{sec:four-questions} against it. Where does it fail Invariant~\ref{def:zoom}?
\end{pdexercise}
\begin{pdsolution}{ex:ls-four-questions-dashboard}
A typical CI dashboard usually fails as follows: deploy is irreversible but the green tile does not force a log/diff view; administrative state and diagnostic detail are flattened together; the summary is generated from job narration rather than independently from receipts; and a skipped/cancelled check carries no named authority reason. It therefore violates Invariant~\ref{def:zoom} wherever a material green claim has no total zoom path to the exact tree, suite, runner, and output that produced it.
\end{pdsolution}
\begin{pdexercise}[kind=Open,rating=3]{ex:ls-adversarial-legibility}
\emph{Adversarial legibility.} An agent that \emph{knows} it
is being made legible can optimize its narration to look good while the diff lies.
Verifiable-zoom is a partial answer. Can the legibility layer \emph{alone},
without sandbox confinement and without a reputation system, detect a deterministic narration$\leftrightarrow$artifact
divergence before reputation exists? Sketch the consistency check and the class of
divergence it cannot catch.
\end{pdexercise}
\begin{pdsolution}{ex:ls-adversarial-legibility}
The legibility layer can compare structured narration claims against independently derived artifacts: claimed changed files versus the tree diff, ``tests passed'' versus a protected runner receipt, ``no deletion'' versus Git objects, and claimed command results versus brokered tool logs. Emit a contradiction event on mismatch. This catches deterministic report/artifact divergence, deleted tests, fabricated execution, and omitted changed paths. It cannot establish semantic correctness, detect a poisoned acceptance suite, observe effects that bypass logging, or distinguish two artifacts that are syntactically consistent but jointly wrong. Those require effect confinement, independent evaluation, or later outcome evidence.
\end{pdsolution}

\pdexercisesfor{\S\ref{sec:authority-rules}}{The Leviathan rules as well as watches (the authority half)}

\begin{pdexercise}[kind=Check,rating=1]{ex:ls-adversarial-review-corollary}
How can adversarial review (\S\ref{sec:review}) discharge
Invariant~\ref{prop:verifiable-zoom}, and why is it not the only possible mechanism?
\end{pdexercise}
\begin{pdsolution}{ex:ls-adversarial-review-corollary}
Verifiable zoom says narration is not ground truth. A reviewer with no independent evidence would merely add another narration. A separately controlled reviewer is one way to provide that evidence channel.
A protected mechanical evaluator can provide another. Verifiable zoom motivates
independent evidence; it does not uniquely entail an adversarial reviewer.
\end{pdsolution}
\begin{pdexercise}[kind=Check,rating=1]{ex:ls-regiment-vs-detect}
State Invariant~\ref{prop:regiment} in your own words and give one example each of a
\emph{regimented} and a \emph{detected-and-compensated} rule.
\end{pdexercise}
\begin{pdsolution}{ex:ls-regiment-vs-detect}
Read Invariant~\ref{prop:regiment} as: prevent a prohibited state when every decisive effect can be intercepted before it occurs; otherwise detect, contain, and compensate without pretending prevention. A uniqueness constraint regimenting ``one holder per exact key'' is a regimented rule. A post-commit monitor that detects an out-of-scope edit and triggers rollback/salvage is detected-and-compensated: the bad prefix already happened.
\end{pdsolution}
\begin{pdexercise}[kind=Trace,rating=2]{ex:ls-completion-migration-vs-ergonomic}
An agent claims completion on a task whose acceptance criterion is ``the
migration applies cleanly.'' Trace Invariant~\ref{def:completionist}: what is the
verifier, what gates, and what happens if the criterion were instead ``the API feels
ergonomic''?
\end{pdexercise}
\begin{pdsolution}{ex:ls-completion-migration-vs-ergonomic}
For ``the migration applies cleanly,'' the verifier is an isolated migration run against a declared base schema/database, with exact migration hash, runner image, exit status, and resulting-schema check; \emph{done} and any merge/settlement are gated on that receipt. ``The API feels ergonomic'' has no mechanical truth predicate: it must go to a declared human/expert or plural judge with preserved evidence and uncertainty, and absent that authority, completion remains provisional or fails loudly.
\end{pdsolution}
\begin{pdexercise}[kind=Open,rating=3]{ex:ls-honest-fallback-ladder}
\emph{Completionist verification of the unverifiable.} Some
acceptance criteria are not mechanically checkable. Characterize the honest fallback
ladder (forced human-in-the-loop gate $\to$ neutral judge with declared low
confidence $\to$ fail-loud). When is ``I cannot verify this'' the \emph{correct}
answer rather than a cop-out?
\end{pdexercise}
\begin{pdsolution}{ex:ls-honest-fallback-ladder}
The honest ladder: (1) use a deterministic verifier where the property is decidable; (2) require an independent bounded evaluator or human for a declared subjective criterion; (3) record a provisional result, confidence, appeal window, and residual risk; (4) return unresolved/fail-loud when no authorized evaluator can support the claim. ``I cannot verify this'' is correct when the available evidence cannot distinguish success from failure under the contract; it is a cop-out only when an affordable, authorized verifier was specified and simply not run.
\end{pdsolution}
\begin{pdexercise}[kind=Open,rating=3]{ex:ls-succession-corner}
\emph{The succession corner of
Thm.~\ref{thm:specialization}.} Eq.~\ref{eq:specboundary} assumes a specialist who
never fails. Extend it to an $M/M/1$ queue \emph{with breakdowns} and a succession
protocol, and find the repair rate at which sole ownership stops being worthwhile regardless of
skill advantage, then check whether that rate is above or below the one an operator
can actually promise.
\end{pdexercise}
\begin{pdsolution}{ex:ls-succession-corner}
The case has a closed-form answer, and the result belongs to
\pdchapref{he}{The Harbor Economy}%
\ifpdbook
, which carries it as Thm.~\ref{he:thm:succession-price}%
\fi
; \S\ref{sec:succession-corner} states the two consequences that matter here.
The route: with death at rate $\xi$ and succession at rate $\eta$ the sole
owner's mean wait decreases in skill to an infimum
$W_\infty=\xi/(\eta(\xi+\eta))$, the residual downtime a request meets when it
arrives during an outage, which it never attains, so no amount of skill removes
downtime below $W_\infty$. Writing $K=A/(c_{\mathrm{wait}}\lambda_F)+W_{\mathrm{pool}}$ for the
pool's cost line, pooling dominates at \emph{every} skill advantage iff $W_\infty$
has reached that line, $W_\infty\ge K$, which rearranges to
$\xi/\eta\ge D^\star=\eta K/(1-\eta K)$. Whether that threshold sits above or
below what an operator can promise is an empirical question about the concrete
handoff process (on-call depth, handoff quality, documentation): if the
promised $\eta$ implies $\xi/\eta>D^\star$, no amount of hiring a better
specialist recovers sole ownership, and the honest fix is a succession plan,
not a stronger specialist.

The threshold $D^\star$ is defined only when $\eta K<1$. If $\eta K\ge1$, the lower limit $W_\infty$ cannot reach the cost line and this obstruction does not arise. The model assumes failures can occur even while the server is idle, with preemptive-resume service after repair. Under failures only during active service, $W_\infty$ is zero instead. The failure model therefore belongs in the answer, not merely in a numerical implementation. Exercise~\ref{ex:ls-b8-specialization-run} checks the never-failing boundary; the economy chapter develops the breakdown model.
\end{pdsolution}
\begin{pdexercise}[kind=Trace,rating=2]{ex:ls-b8-specialization-run}

\begin{pdexercise}[kind=Trace,rating=2]{ex:ls-b7-escalation-run}
Run \texttt{python3 skills/harbor-results/scripts/b7\_escalation\_band.py} and read
off the feasible debit band $[\theta_{\min},\theta_{\max}]$ at $A_{\mathrm{att}}=3.3$ and at
$A_{\mathrm{att}}=2.0$. Confirm $u^\star(0.05)$ by hand first.
\end{pdexercise}
\begin{pdsolution}{ex:ls-b7-escalation-run}
By hand, at $\theta=0.05,w=1$: $u^\star=\theta w/(1+\theta w)=0.05/1.05=0.0476$ [verified, closed form]. \begin{pdexercise}[kind=Open,rating=3]{ex:ls-noisy-zoom-oracle}
Take the halving procedure of Theorem~\ref{thm:zoom-advantage}(a). Its group
query is exact: it reports whether $J\cap P=\emptyset$ and is never wrong. A real group query is a linter, a batched
test run or a diff scan, and those have false negatives: a group that does
contain a critical item can come back clean. This is the worst direction for the
algorithm to fail in, because a negative group is \emph{discarded}: one false
negative high in the tree silently drops a whole subtree and the run reports an
answer that is confidently incomplete. Suppose each query independently returns a
false negative with probability at most $\alpha$, and that false positives do not
occur (a group reported dirty really is dirty). Give a repetition scheme that
bounds the probability of losing any given positive, state the query cost it
adds, and then say what the scheme does \emph{not} fix.
\end{pdexercise}
\begin{pdsolution}{ex:ls-noisy-zoom-oracle}
For $0<\alpha<1$, accept ``clean'' only when $n_{\mathrm{rep}}$ independent queries
on the same group all report clean; stop as soon as any query reports dirty.
A positive group is wrongly discarded with probability at most $\alpha^{n_{\mathrm{rep}}}$.
For $0<\varepsilon_{\mathrm{grp}}<1$, choosing
$n_{\mathrm{rep}}=\lceil\log(1/\varepsilon_{\mathrm{grp}})/\log(1/\alpha)\rceil$ gives per-group error at most
$\varepsilon_{\mathrm{grp}}$; at $\alpha=0.1$, three calls give $10^{-3}$. A dirty verdict needs
no confirmation because false positives are excluded by hypothesis.
The noisy recursion visits a subset of the groups visited by the noiseless
algorithm: an erroneous clean verdict can prune that tree but cannot add nodes.
Writing part (a)'s full bound as
\[
Q_0=2k\lceil\log_2(F/k)\rceil+4k,
\]
the repetition scheme therefore uses at most $n_{\mathrm{rep}}Q_0$ oracle calls. For a fixed
repetition count, this bound retains its sparsity dependence; whether it beats
flat inspection also depends on $n_{\mathrm{rep}}$ and the required reliability.

Three limitations remain. First, per-group error is not the probability of
losing a positive anywhere in the tree. A union bound over the at most $Q_0$
visited groups gives
\[
\Pr(\text{any positive is lost})\le Q_0\alpha^{n_{\mathrm{rep}}}.
\]
Thus, for $0<\varepsilon_{\mathrm{run}}<1$, choosing
$n_{\mathrm{rep}}=\lceil\log(Q_0/\varepsilon_{\mathrm{run}})/\log(1/\alpha)\rceil$ suffices for a whole-run
failure bound of $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{run}}$, and hence for each individual positive as well.
The $4k$ term must be retained when $k$ is near $F$. If $k$ is unknown, use
part (a)'s $k$-free bound $2F-1$ in place of $Q_0$ for this choice.
At $\alpha=0$, one call per group is exact; at $\alpha=1$, the assumed error
bound supplies no finite repetition guarantee. Second, the
independence assumption is doing heavy work. Repeating a deterministic linter
on the same input gives no new evidence. Independently randomized calls to one
tool may satisfy the assumed error model; different tools may still share errors.
The requirement concerns the sampling and errors, not the tool names. This is
the same distinction between diversity and conditional detection developed in
\pdchapref{stp}{From Spawn to Person}. Third, and unresolved: the guarantee above is
probabilistic where Theorem~\ref{thm:zoom-advantage} is exact, so a chapter that
measures oversight in bits now owes an account of what a $10^{-3}$ chance of a
silently dropped subtree is worth against the queries it saves, and that is a
decision-theoretic question this chapter does not answer. The noisy
group-testing literature is where to start. Atia and Saligrama
\pdcite{atiasaligrama2012} give information-theoretic limits for noisy
non-adaptive designs, and Cheraghchi \pdcite{cheraghchi2013} gives noise-resilient
constructions, but neither is written for the adaptive, correlated-oracle case
above, so the obligation is to state the model before importing a bound, not to
import one and hope.
\end{pdsolution}

At $k_{\mathrm{off}}=1$: $k_{\mathrm{on}}/(k_{\mathrm{on}}-k_{\mathrm{off}}+1)=8/8=1.0$, the fully resource-augmented regime, where online's cache $k_{\mathrm{on}}$ so outsizes the comparator's $k_{\mathrm{off}}=1$ that the ratio collapses to $1$. The chapter's own $k_{\mathrm{on}}=8,k_{\mathrm{off}}=4$ instance, at $1.6$, sits at the midpoint, measured against a comparator with half of online's verified capacity.
\end{pdsolution}

\pdexercisesfor{\S\ref{sec:consent-canon}}{Reconciling the canon: roles, consent, and local knowledge}

\begin{pdexercise}[kind=Check,rating=1]{ex:ls-hobbes-mapping}
Map each of {agents, daemon, operator} onto Hobbes' {multitude, sovereign-actor,
author}. Why does the metaphor break if the operator is called the sovereign?
\end{pdexercise}
\begin{pdsolution}{ex:ls-hobbes-mapping}
Agents are the multitude bound by the common coordination protocol; the daemon is the sovereign-as-actor that applies the rules; the operator is the author/principal who originates and can withdraw authority. Calling the operator the sovereign collapses author and executing authority and makes the supposed covenant among agents incoherent: there would be no distinct actor left to hold accountable to the author's terms.
\end{pdsolution}
\begin{pdexercise}[kind=Check,rating=1]{ex:ls-exit-makes-voice-honest}
Why does Hirschman's \emph{exit} (Invariant~\ref{prop:override}) make \emph{voice}
(escalation) honest?
\end{pdexercise}
\begin{pdsolution}{ex:ls-exit-makes-voice-honest}
Voice is honest only if refusal has consequence for the authority. The unconditional override supplies exit: the operator can revoke the grant when explanations or appeals fail, rather than being forced to keep negotiating inside a system they cannot stop.
\end{pdsolution}
\begin{pdexercise}[kind=Trace,rating=2]{ex:ls-express-consent-properties}
Show that the consent grant (Invariant~\ref{def:consent}) is \emph{express} in
Locke's sense by listing the properties that distinguish it from ``the operator
just started using the tool.''
\end{pdexercise}
\begin{pdsolution}{ex:ls-express-consent-properties}
The grant is discrete, explicitly issued, scoped, time-bounded, and revocable and audited. ``Started using the tool'' has none of these properties: it is continuous rather than discrete, inferred rather than issued, unscoped, open-ended, and carries no explicit revocation act.
\end{pdsolution}
\begin{pdexercise}[kind=Open,rating=3]{ex:ls-ranker-political-organ}
\emph{The ranker as a political authority.} Specify the ``what did the
queue suppress, and why'' counter-surface (Table~\ref{tab:counter-surface})
so that ranking-as-governing is itself legible.
Does the counter-surface need its own counter-surface? Where does the regress
terminate, and on what principle?
\end{pdexercise}
\begin{pdsolution}{ex:ls-ranker-political-organ}
Same construction as Exercise~\ref{ex:ls-ranker-counter-surface}: one complete, append-only decision ledger recording every candidate, its ranking decision, reason codes, and a counterfactual, queryable directly and sampled independently of the production ranker rather than re-ranked by a second dashboard. The regress terminates at completeness plus direct sampling. The base ledger already records every candidate and decision, and the counter-surface is a deterministic query over it, so no further ranker is needed to audit the ranker's omissions. The remaining trust assumption is the completeness of the event-capture boundary itself; that assumption should be tested and externally anchored, not hidden behind another summary.
\end{pdsolution}

\section{Related work and prior art}\label{sec:related}
% ═════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

This chapter sits at the confluence of five external literatures that rarely cite one
another. We survey them in one place; each was introduced in context above.

\paragraph{Legibility and the limits of central schemes.}
The organizing lens is \textbf{Scott}'s \emph{Seeing Like a State}~\pdcite{scott1998}:
states make populations governable by imposing legibility, and high-modernist
\emph{over}-legibility recurrently fails because it destroys the local, hard-to-codify
\emph{m\^etis} the simplified order depended on; \emph{Two Cheers for
Anarchism}~\pdcite{scott2012} is its constructive sequel. The same point arrives from
economics in \textbf{Hayek}'s \emph{The Use of Knowledge in Society}~\pdcite{hayek1945}:
the decisive knowledge is dispersed and not aggregable by a central planner.
\textbf{Rao}~\pdcite{rao2010} ported ``legibility'' into a standard lens on systems that
flatten reality to a single purpose. Our contribution is to make Scott's warning a
\emph{buildable} rule (digest-with-zoom, Invariant~\ref{def:zoom}) and to give it an
information-theoretic lower bound (Thm.~\ref{thm:lowerbound}).

\paragraph{Authority, consent, and the social contract.}
The authority argument is Hobbesian~\pdcite{hobbes1651,sep-hobbes}, amended by the
liberal consent tradition (\textbf{Locke}~\pdcite{locke1689},
\textbf{Hume}~\pdcite{hume1748}, \textbf{Pateman}~\pdcite{pateman1979}) and by
\textbf{Hirschman}'s exit/voice framework~\pdcite{hirschman1970}. The
state-of-nature-to-sovereign arc has also been observed as an \emph{emergent} dynamic of
LLM populations (\textbf{Dai et al.}~\pdcite{dai2024}, building on \textbf{Park et
al.}~\pdcite{park2023}). We turn the metaphor into a mechanism: an express, scoped,
revocable consent grant (Invariant~\ref{def:consent}) with an inalienable override
(Invariant~\ref{prop:override}), and a legible-sovereign rule
(Invariant~\ref{prop:legible-sovereign}) that inverts Hobbes' opaque sovereign.

% This paragraph's citations need a fresh margin in both profiles: with the
% many-authors section, the history's citations overflow one page otherwise.
\pdmarginpagebreak
\paragraph{Human factors, automation, and signal detection.}
The operator model draws on the automation literature:
\textbf{Bainbridge}'s ironies of automation~\pdcite{bainbridge1983},
\textbf{Lee \& See} on trust calibration~\pdcite{leesee2004},
\textbf{Endsley} on situation awareness and the out-of-the-loop
problem~\pdcite{endsley1995,endsleykiris1995}, the vigilance-decrement results of
\textbf{Mackworth}~\pdcite{mackworth1948} and \textbf{Warm et al.}~\pdcite{warm2008},
\textbf{Wickens}' multiple-resource theory~\pdcite{wickens2002},
\textbf{Treisman \& Gelade}'s feature-integration theory~\pdcite{treisman1980}, and
\textbf{Miller}'s studies of absolute judgment, immediate memory, and
recoding~\pdcite{miller1956}.

\noindent\textbf{Alarm decisions.} The forced-zoom decision is
modeled with \textbf{Green \& Swets}' Signal Detection Theory~\pdcite{greenswets1966}
(Invariant~\ref{def:sdt}); the alarm discipline follows process-control
practice~\pdcite{isa182} and the clinical alarm-fatigue evidence~\pdcite{cvach2012}.

\noindent\textbf{Limits of the objective.}
\textbf{Campbell}~\pdcite{campbell1979} and \textbf{Strathern}~\pdcite{strathern1997}
(Goodhart's law) warn about incentives created by consequential metrics. This
chapter applies a classical SDT loss (Eq.~\ref{eq:sdt}) to inspection decisions
in agent supervision. The usefulness of that application requires calibration
and evaluation.

\paragraph{Information foraging and the cost of reading.}
Read-poverty (Hypothesis~\ref{def:readpoverty}) is the agent-coordination instance of
\textbf{Pirolli \& Card}'s \emph{information foraging theory}~\pdcite{pirolli1999}
(information-seekers follow ``information scent'' and abandon a patch when its expected
yield falls below the cost of foraging) and of the long-context degradation
results that make reading expensive even for machines (\textbf{Liu et
al.}~\pdcite{liu2024}; \textbf{Anthropic}~\pdcite{anthropic2025}). The remedy is the
classical database remedy, an index, recast as a directory over agents.

\paragraph{Agent discovery, naming, and trust.}
The directory has deep prior art in \textbf{FIPA}'s Directory Facilitator
and Agent Management System~\pdcite{fipa00023}, re-invented for LLM agents as the Agent
Name Service (\textbf{Huang et al.}~\pdcite{huang2025ans}). For the trust layer that sits above
discovery, \textbf{EigenTrust}~\pdcite{kamvar2003} supplies a trust-propagation
model whose modified algorithm relies on pretrusted peers. \textbf{Douceur}'s
Sybil analysis~\pdcite{douceur2002} makes identity assumptions a separate issue;
it does not turn an aggregate trust score into a general Sybil defense.
\textbf{Ostrom}~\pdcite{ostrom1990} offers governance comparisons, not a theorem
selecting this architecture. The proposed trust layer still owes explicit
anchors, entry rules, and an attack model. Our specific result here is the \emph{split ranker}
(Thm.~\ref{thm:split-ranker}): collaborator fit and inspection priorities can
strictly conflict. The comonotonicity result states when a common ordering can
nevertheless serve both readers.

\paragraph{What the chapter proves, and how to read it.}
Each result above carries its own proof or proof sketch: the legibility lower
bound (Thm.~\ref{thm:lowerbound}), the comonotone characterization
(Thm.~\ref{thm:comonotone}) behind the rank-order conflict in
Thm.~\ref{thm:split-ranker}, the regret head (Thm.~\ref{thm:regret-head}), the
zoom bounds for plain halving and for Hwang's splitting
(Thm.~\ref{thm:zoom-advantage}) and the Erlang-C boundary
(Thm.~\ref{thm:specialization}, derived in Example~\ref{ex:queue-boundary}). The
sweeps reported beside them are finite checks, not further proofs. Two results
need careful reading. The extractive counterexample in
Thm.~\ref{thm:split-digest} states a feasible summary class and budget; it does
not forbid shared storage. The pooling gain (Thm.~\ref{thm:pooling-gain})
compares lower bounds at an equal opening budget; it says nothing about encoders.

\section{The time axis and the bridge to the economy}\label{sec:bridge}
% ═════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

\subsection{Legibility over time, not just the present}\label{sec:timeaxis}

It is tempting to treat legibility as a snapshot: what is the swarm doing
\emph{now}? But most operator decisions are forensic: \emph{why} did this land? The
time axis is a distinct legibility mode: a replay scrubber over history,
commit-anchored provenance on every annotation, and an append-only event log that
answers ``how did we get here?'' (Scott's cadastral map is static; a swarm needs a
legible \emph{history}.) Together with the projection mode
(\S\ref{sec:sa}), legibility spans all three tenses: the forensic past (replay), the
inspectable present (digest-with-zoom), and the projected future (freeze-probe SA).

\subsection{What this layer hands upward (the through-line)}\label{sec:handoff}

The single-operator product records continuity as well as current work. The
accompanying chapters use that evidence in the following proposed architecture:

\pdthesis{This design combines memory, checkpoints, and witnessed outcomes to
support continuity. Reputation uses attributable outcomes under stable identity.
A checkpoint that restores an exact running computation is needed only when the
workload requires that form of resumption; it is not a prerequisite for every
agent market. Today's notes and oracle-bound commitments provide narrower forms
of continuity than execution snapshots and neutral graded outcomes.}

Concretely, the legibility layer provides upward:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em]
\item \textbf{Legible continuity.} Resurrection-with-memory, episodic memory, and
the briefing together make recorded continuity inspectable. The intended route
is memory and task state supporting continued work, with attributable outcomes
supporting reputation. A score alone says nothing about whose work was evaluated
or whether the evidence is valid. The required checkpoint fidelity depends on
the task; preserving a reviewable outcome history need not restore a running
process. The proposed cross-operator attestation is an additional mechanism
whose trust assumptions and implementation \pdchapref{he}{The Harbor Economy}
must declare.
\pdstatus{\BuiltWeak}{non-forgeable identity: daemon-minted \texttt{actor-souls} and a bounded newcomer pool ship; universal write-boundary enforcement does not}
\item \textbf{The completion ledger.} In the target design, every verified
completion (and every loud-failed one) becomes an outcome event. This layer is the \emph{outcome witness}; the
reputation layer is the \emph{scorer}. The split ranker
(Thm.~\ref{thm:split-ranker}) and the costly-escalation signal (\S\ref{sec:gates})
supply the candidate generation and trust signals the reputation head consumes.
\pdstatus{\BuiltWeak}{durable commitments, oracle-bound closure and overdue detection ship; neutral grading and reputation updates do not}
\item \textbf{The consent grant as a priceable object.} A hosted-trust offering is
the transfer of a consent grant (Invariant~\ref{def:consent}) to a third party, which the
pricing model in \pdchapref{he}{The Harbor Economy} must specify.
\item \textbf{Operator-attention economics.} The loss in
\S\ref{sec:operator} offers a model for costing inspection and errors when
supervising a rented fleet. Estimating those costs and checking the inspection
assumptions are part of applying it.
\end{itemize}

The Leviathan that makes your swarm legible to you, without lying to you with a pretty
map, is the product. Everything above it (federation, the market) is a second
Leviathan made of the first.

\keyidea[Local oversight comes before markets]{A single-operator tool can help
Alice authorize work, inspect supporting evidence and record unfinished
obligations before any cross-operator market exists. Its contract is already
substantial: revocable authority, verifiable zoom targets, calibrated demands
on attention and honest completion records. The later chapters ask what
additional identity, evaluation and settlement rules are needed when another
operator must rely on those records.}

\section{Implementation and status}\label{app:honest}

The body of this chapter argues mechanisms on their merits and does not depend on any of
them being built. This appendix, and \emph{only} this appendix, records the
concrete engineering status of each mechanism in the reference implementation, named
\emph{Port Daddy}. We use four honest labels, with one rule for the whole table:
confusing what is \emph{implemented} with what is merely \emph{specified} is itself
the failure this chapter argues against, so each row carries its evidence.

\smallskip
\noindent\Built\ = code exists in the reference implementation today; \BuiltWeak\ =
code exists but is partial, unwired, or honest about a known gap; \Designed\ = a
concrete, accepted design with no merged implementation; \Vision\ = a mechanism argued
in this chapter with no design document yet.

\smallskip
\noindent A second key, in the same grammar, labels \emph{claims} rather than code.
Formal results checked outside this document carry the marker \Verified\ directly
under them; the others are argued in the chapter. The markers mean:
\Verified\ = independently re-derived and checked numerically, or subjected to a
pre-registered falsification sweep, outside this document; \Proved\ = argued here with
a proof or proof sketch and not independently checked; \Unproved\ = stated as a design
target whose proof is not carried in this chapter. The two keys are orthogonal on
purpose: a \Verified\ result about a \Vision\ mechanism is a perfectly ordinary
state of affairs, and conflating them is the failure this appendix exists to prevent.

Three points about the reference implementation are important enough to restate:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item \textbf{The anomaly monitor is detect-and-compensate, not prevention}
(Invariant~\ref{prop:regiment}). The monitor at \texttt{lib/arbiter.ts:328} is a
post-commit log subscriber; it cannot prevent a violation that has already
committed. A later safety gate would be a separate mechanism. ``Physically unreachable'' is reserved for constraint- or boot-gate-enforced
states only.
\item \textbf{The unified ranker is two rankers} (Thm.~\ref{thm:split-ranker}). The
discovery router maximizes \emph{fit}; the attention queue maximizes
\emph{regret-if-ignored}. They share candidate generation and decay, not an objective.
\item \textbf{Every read-surface decays} (Invariant~\ref{prop:decay}). Briefing,
resurrection, discovery, and attestation projections all carry recency-decay; the
dead-claim case (\S\ref{sec:staleness}) is the worked failure this closes.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Notation and the nomenclature key}\label{app:notation}

Two distinctions in this chapter are easy to collapse and essential to keep separate.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
\item \textbf{Capability vs.\ permission.} \emph{Capability} is \emph{ability}: what
the execution sandbox physically makes possible. \emph{Permission} is \emph{normative
status}: what the policy layer allows. They must never be collapsed: ``able but
forbidden'' (a dangerous escape-hatch flag that exists in the tooling but must not be
used) has to stay expressible, or the system cannot reason about a capability it
possesses but is not permitted to exercise.
\item \textbf{Two distinct delegation objects.} The \emph{authorization chain} is a
cryptographic, hop-bound, attenuation-monotonic delegation chain (a security object,
developed in \pdchapref{anchor}{The Anchor Protocol}). The \emph{coordination lineage} is the
task graph over which loop-detection runs (a liveness object, in this chapter's
protocol layer), and it is the same object \pdchapref{swk}{The Single-Writer Kernel}
names under that name: one object, one name, which is the point of this entry.
They are different artifacts with different invariants; ``delegation
chain'' unqualified conflates a security claim with a coordination claim and must be
avoided.
\item \textbf{Physically unreachable} is reserved for constraint-enforced or
boot-gate-enforced states only (Invariant~\ref{prop:regiment}); a detect-and-compensate
monitor is never given the phrase.
\end{itemize}
