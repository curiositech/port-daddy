\caption{\textbf{One quality vector supports two legitimate, opposite
decisions.} Outcome A is the chapter's own worked example (Heron's audited
job, S3): $\mathbf{q}=(1.0,0.7,0.9)$. A security-weighted buyer (Bob's actual
weights $0.2,0.6,0.2$) scores it $0.2{\cdot}1.0+0.6{\cdot}0.7+0.2{\cdot}0.9=0.78$,
exactly the chapter's number. Outcome B, $\mathbf{q}=(.85,.5,1.0)$, is a
constructed contrast strong only on efficiency; a cost-weighted buyer
($0.1,0.1,0.8$) scores it $0.94$ against A's $0.89$ under the same weights,
reversing the ranking. Averaging either vector into one scalar before
disclosure would have erased whichever decision the buyer did not make
[outcome A and the $0.78$ figure verified against \S\ref{sec:adr0049};
outcome B and the cost weights are a constructed illustration, not a
chapter-reported number].}
